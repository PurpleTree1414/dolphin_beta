In January 2024, Daniel Windred and colleagues at Monash University published a study in *Sleep* using accelerometer data from roughly sixty thousand participants in the UK Biobank cohort that has reset the empirical baseline for what good sleep means. The participants who slept on the most regular schedule — measured by an index called the Sleep Regularity Index, which captures the day-to-day consistency of an individual's sleep-wake pattern — had a thirty percent lower risk of all-cause mortality across the seven-year follow-up than those with the most irregular schedules, and a thirty-eight percent lower risk of cardiometabolic mortality. More striking still, when sleep regularity was directly compared against sleep duration as a mortality predictor, regularity was the stronger of the two. The implication, which has not yet fully diffused into popular sleep guidance, is that the conventional emphasis on hours per night may have been measuring the wrong thing, or at least not the most informative thing.

The Sleep Regularity Index was developed by Andrew Phillips and colleagues in a 2017 paper in *Scientific Reports*. It is technically a measure of the probability that an individual is in the same sleep-wake state at any two time points twenty-four hours apart, averaged over a week of recording. Practically, it captures whether someone tends to fall asleep and wake at consistent times across days, or whether their schedule shifts substantially. A perfectly regular sleeper has an SRI of 100; a perfectly irregular sleeper has an SRI of 0. The median SRI in the UK Biobank cohort was around 60, with the lowest fifth of the population scoring around 40 and the highest fifth around 75. The wide spread of the distribution itself tells us something useful: regularity, in the population at large, is far from a default state.

The Windred study did not stand alone. Lachlan Cribb and colleagues, also at Monash, published a separate analysis of the same UK Biobank data in *eLife* in late 2023, using a slightly different statistical approach with eighty-eight thousand participants. They found a similar pattern: a non-linear relationship between SRI and mortality, with most of the elevated risk concentrated in the lowest twenty percent of the regularity distribution. The convergence of two independent analyses on the same broad finding has made sleep regularity one of the more robust recent additions to the sleep epidemiology literature, although both studies share the limitations of the underlying cohort, which is older and less ethnically diverse than would be ideal for population-wide generalisation.

The previous popular framing of sleep optimisation was almost entirely about duration. Eight hours per night was treated as the canonical target, with deviations in either direction associated with worse outcomes. This framing was not wrong — sleep duration does predict mortality and morbidity — but it was incomplete in a way that turned out to matter. The person who sleeps eight hours every night but at unpredictable times is not getting the same biological experience as the person who sleeps eight hours every night at the same time. The duration measure could not see the difference; the regularity measure can.

The mechanism is straightforward in outline, though the underlying biology is intricate. The human circadian system, covered at depth in Body section 1.7, is a network of cellular clocks distributed throughout the body and synchronised by a master pacemaker in the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus. The system anticipates regularity and prepares the body's metabolism, hormone release, immune function, and cognitive readiness for predicted demand. When the schedule shifts unpredictably, the entire network is forced into continuous re-entrainment, with cellular clocks in different tissues becoming temporarily desynchronised from each other. The Mind pillar discusses this network in section 2.5 with respect to cognitive timing; the Lifestyle contribution is the recognition that the operational signature of circadian disruption is irregular sleep timing, and that this is a behavioral and scheduling phenomenon as much as a biological one.

The most useful term in this literature is social jet lag, introduced by Marc Wittmann, Jenny Dinich, Martha Merrow, and Till Roenneberg in a 2006 paper. Social jet lag refers to the discrepancy between an individual's biological sleep timing — what their body would do in the absence of social demands — and their actual sleep timing imposed by work, family, and social schedules. The most common manifestation is the difference between weekday and weekend sleep timing: someone who sleeps from midnight to seven on weekdays but from two to ten on weekends is experiencing the equivalent of a three-hour time-zone shift twice each week, with the corresponding circadian disruption. Roenneberg's research over the following two decades documented that social jet lag is associated with worse mood, higher BMI, increased smoking and alcohol use, and reduced cognitive performance, with effects detectable even in people who consider their sleep schedule unproblematic. The Windred and Cribb mortality findings are, in effect, the long-term consequences of the same underlying pattern that social jet lag captures at a behavioral level.

This is the empirical foundation underneath the Sleep Consistency metric in the Lifestyle pillar, which captures the user's interdaily sleep timing variability and contributes ten percent to the live Lifestyle score. The weight is deliberately modest. Sleep is the territory of the Body pillar, where the physiology is treated at depth — sleep architecture, the hormonal cascade of sleep onset, the cognitive and metabolic consequences of sleep loss, the relationship between REM and slow-wave sleep across the night. Lifestyle's contribution to sleep is narrower: the regularity layer, which is the behavioral signature most reliably tractable through scheduling and environmental design rather than through clinical intervention. The metric weight reflects this division of labour. Sleep matters enormously to wellbeing, but most of what determines its quality is physiological and is therefore measured and improved through the Body pillar; what Lifestyle adds is a measure of the consistency of the schedule, which is where the user's daily structure most directly shapes the system.

A practical implication of the regularity emphasis is that the most useful sleep intervention for a person already sleeping a reasonable number of hours is not usually to sleep more, but to sleep at more consistent times. The behavioral target is a stable wake time, including on weekends, with bedtime allowed more flexibility but anchored within a window of an hour or so on either side of a typical time. The wake time is the more important of the two anchors because it is the one most directly under voluntary control and the one most reliably entrained by light exposure, which is the subject of article 3.4.2. The bedtime tends to follow the wake time once the system has stabilised, particularly if light exposure during the day is adequate.

A note of qualification on the SRI literature, since it is recent and still actively developing. The methodology of the index itself remains under refinement, the generalisability of UK Biobank findings to younger and more diverse populations is one of the open questions in the field, and the longer-term causal pathway — whether irregular sleep is a primary driver of poor outcomes or a downstream marker of other disrupted patterns — has not been definitively established. What can be said with reasonable confidence is that consistency is now well-supported as a meaningful predictor of long-term health, that the size of the effect appears to be substantial relative to other modifiable behavioral factors, and that the operational implication is a practical intervention that the data clearly supports even as the underlying mechanisms continue to be elucidated.

What the recent SRI research has done, in effect, is to make visible something that was hiding inside the older sleep duration literature: the people who slept the right number of hours but at unpredictable times were carrying a kind of biological cost that the duration measure could not see, and that the consistency measure now does.
