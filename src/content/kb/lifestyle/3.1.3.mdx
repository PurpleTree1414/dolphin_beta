The seven-day week is one of the few major units of human time that has no direct correlate in nature. The day tracks Earth's rotation. The month roughly tracks the lunar cycle. The year tracks the orbit. The week tracks nothing astronomical — it is, as the sociologist Eviatar Zerubavel argued in his 1985 book *The Seven Day Circle*, a cultural invention with religious roots that has propagated to nearly every modern society. This makes the week unusual: a unit of time that is universally recognised but biologically arbitrary. And yet it functions as the dominant unit of life planning, the period over which most professional and social rhythms organise themselves, and the window over which Dolphin's Habit Streak metric is calculated.

The week's structural utility comes from its size relative to certain rhythms that do not fit cleanly into days. Some forms of recovery — physical training cycles, deep cognitive work, social cycles — operate on scales too long for a single day to capture and too short for the month. A meaningful exercise programme typically requires two or three sessions per week with rest days between, which can only be arranged at the weekly level. Significant social connection, particularly outside immediate household relationships, is rarely a daily phenomenon and is poorly captured by daily measurement. Project-level work in most knowledge professions has natural arcs that span multiple days. The week is the smallest unit of time over which most adult lives can be meaningfully said to have a shape.

This is why Dolphin tracks Habit Streak weekly rather than daily. A daily measure of habit adherence rewards perfection and penalises ordinary disruption — sickness, travel, unusual demand — in ways that produce a distorted picture of behavioral consistency. A weekly measure asks the more honest question: across the past seven days, what proportion of the user's committed habits were maintained? This permits an off day or two without resetting the structure, while still imposing the consistency requirement that makes a habit a habit rather than an intermittent activity. The metric design embeds an empirical position: that consistency is properly measured at the week, not the day.

Planning is the deliberate organisation of the week, and the literature on planning interventions is more mixed than the popular productivity discourse suggests. Claessens and colleagues' 2007 review of the time management literature, drawing on several decades of empirical work, found that time-management interventions reliably improve subjective satisfaction and reduce perceived stress, but the effect on objective performance is modest and inconsistent. People who plan their weeks feel better about how they spend them; whether they actually accomplish more is less clear. This is worth saying explicitly because the planning industry — books, apps, courses — markets itself on objective performance gains that the underlying research does not robustly demonstrate.

What planning does demonstrably improve is the alignment between intention and action. Gollwitzer's implementation intentions research, discussed in article 3.1.2, shows that linking a specific behavior to a specific time and context substantially improves follow-through. A weekly plan that specifies when a behavior will occur is materially different from a list of intentions that does not. The honest version of the planning argument is therefore not that planning makes people more productive in some absolute sense, but that planning is the mechanism by which intentions become more likely to become behaviors.

The classic finding that all planning systems must contend with is the planning fallacy. Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin, and Michael Ross documented in a 1994 paper that has been replicated extensively since that people systematically underestimate the time required to complete tasks, and that this underestimate persists even when individuals have repeatedly experienced tasks taking longer than predicted. The fallacy is robust across domains — academic, professional, personal — and resistant to debiasing. The implication for weekly planning is that the realistic plan tolerates the fallacy by leaving meaningful slack rather than packing the week to its theoretical capacity. A plan that can survive ordinary slippage is more likely to be executed than one that requires perfect time estimation.

The weekly review is the meta-habit of the planning system. Carver and Scheier's work on self-regulation, particularly their 2001 synthesis, identified periodic comparison of current state against intended state as the central mechanism of behavior change in any domain. The review is the practical instantiation of this comparison. Without it, the gap between intention and execution can grow silently for weeks before it becomes visible.

The structure of an effective weekly review is more modest than the popular literature suggests. The empirical case for elaborate review systems — multi-page templates, scoring rubrics, exhaustive life-area inventories — is essentially absent. What the self-regulation literature supports is something simpler: a recurring time at which the previous week is examined with reasonable honesty, and the upcoming week is shaped in light of what was observed. Carver and Scheier's research suggests this comparison need not be lengthy to be effective; what matters is that it occurs reliably and that the recalibration it produces is acted on. Locke and Latham, whose goal-setting research culminated in a widely-cited 2002 paper, made a similar point at the goal level: the mechanism by which goals improve performance depends on the presence of feedback, and the weekly review is the most natural feedback interval for goals operating at the scale of an ordinary life.

The weekly review also performs a function that no daily practice can: it permits the user to notice patterns that operate at the weekly scale. A pattern of low energy on Mondays, social isolation across consecutive weeks, or a recurring slip in habit adherence tied to particular conditions — these are visible at the week level and invisible at the day level. The review is, among other things, the only time most people meaningfully observe their own life at this resolution.

The week endures as a unit of life not because it tracks any natural cycle but because it is the smallest interval over which a human life acquires shape, and the discipline of regularly looking at that shape is the precondition for any meaningful adjustment of it.
