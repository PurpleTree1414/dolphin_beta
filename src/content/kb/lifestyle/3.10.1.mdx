Until 2015, the question of whether human physiology has meaningful seasonal variation had been generally treated as more or less settled, with the common view being that humans, as a tropical species turned global, had largely escaped the strong seasonal patterns observed in other mammals. A study published that year in *Nature Communications* by Xaquin Castro Dopico, John Todd, and an international research team substantially complicated the picture. Drawing on whole-blood gene expression and adipose tissue data from sixteen thousand participants across multiple cohorts in the United Kingdom, Iceland, the United States, Australia, and The Gambia, the researchers documented that more than four thousand protein-coding genes — roughly one quarter of those expressed in the tissues studied — showed statistically significant seasonal variation. The pattern was particularly pronounced in immune-related genes, with a markedly pro-inflammatory transcriptomic profile during European winter, including elevated levels of soluble IL-6 receptor and C-reactive protein. The seasonal patterns were inverted between the northern and southern hemispheres, as one would expect if the variation tracked daylight rather than calendar, and were attenuated near the equator, where seasonal daylight variation is small. The finding established, with empirical force the older literature had not provided, that the human body has a substantially seasonal physiology that the modern indoor environment masks but does not abolish.

The biological mechanism underlying this seasonal physiology is the photoperiodic response, the system by which animals adjust their internal state to match the length of daylight. Thomas Wehr's research at the National Institute of Mental Health, particularly his 1991 paper in the *Journal of Clinical Endocrinology and Metabolism*, demonstrated that humans kept under controlled photoperiods showed melatonin secretion durations and sleep architectures that varied substantially with daylength, in patterns broadly consistent with seasonally adapted mammals. The result challenged the prevailing assumption that humans had escaped photoperiodic regulation; modern indoor lighting and indoor heating, Wehr's work suggested, do not eliminate the photoperiodic response so much as override it with continuous summer-like signal. The Body pillar's section 1.7 covers the underlying chronobiology of the system, including the role of melatonin and the suprachiasmatic nucleus; the present article concerns the practical and behavioural consequences of seasonality, which is Lifestyle's distinctive contribution.

The most clinically visible expression of seasonality in humans is seasonal affective disorder, characterised in a 1984 paper by Norman Rosenthal and colleagues at NIMH that established the syndrome as a distinct clinical category. Their work, drawing on a small initial case series, identified a pattern of recurrent winter depressive episodes responsive to bright light therapy, with the underlying mechanism implicating the photoperiodic system rather than any specifically seasonal life event. SAD has since been substantially refined as a clinical category, and the Mind pillar's section 2.7 on depression covers it in its appropriate context. The relevant point for the present article is that SAD represents the severe end of a continuous distribution. Anna Wirz-Justice, in a 2018 review in *General and Comparative Endocrinology* synthesising several decades of research on seasonality in affective disorders, argued that subclinical seasonal variation in mood, energy, and cognition affects a much larger fraction of the population than meet diagnostic criteria for SAD, and that the popular conception of seasonality as either present (SAD) or absent (everyone else) misrepresents what is actually a graded variation across a substantial proportion of the population.

The practical implication, which the lifestyle architecture of most modern users does not adequately accommodate, is that the daily and weekly structure that works in one season may work poorly in another. Sleep timing tends to drift later in summer and earlier in winter, in patterns consistent with photoperiodic regulation; users on rigid year-round schedules are working against their biology in opposite directions in different seasons. Energy and mood show seasonal variation that, even subclinically, affects what kinds of work and social engagement the user can sustain. Exercise patterns shift with weather and daylight in ways that the user often experiences as inconvenient but which are partly an appropriate biological adaptation. Social patterns, in most cultures, have historically been more concentrated in particular seasons, and the modern levelling of social schedules across the year may be poorly matched to the underlying biological rhythms.

The seasonal recalibration that the literature supports is not radical. It does not require the user to live differently in winter than in summer, but it does suggest that some adjustment to daily and weekly architecture, made deliberately rather than as a passive drift, produces better outcomes than the rigid year-round schedule. Practical adjustments that the empirical record supports include shifting wake and sleep times somewhat with seasonal daylight where occupational schedules allow; increasing morning light exposure in winter, which the Brown consensus discussed in article 3.4.2 supports for its broader circadian-anchoring effects; modifying exercise patterns to accommodate weather and daylight without abandoning regularity entirely; and accepting that energy and mood may run somewhat lower in late autumn and winter and not interpreting this as a failure of motivation. The Lifestyle pillar's general principle that consistency belongs to the architecture and flexibility belongs to the contents, articulated in article 3.1.1, applies with particular force to seasonal recalibration: the underlying structure of routines should remain stable while the specific content within them adapts to the conditions the season provides.

A note of honest qualification on the seasonality literature, since the popular treatment has sometimes overrun the evidence. The Dopico finding on seasonal gene expression is robust and has been built on by subsequent work, but the precise translation from gene expression patterns to specific behavioural recommendations remains underdeveloped. The SAD literature is well-established for its narrow clinical territory, but the broader claims about subclinical seasonality affecting most of the population are based on more variable evidence, with some studies showing strong patterns and others finding weaker effects. The general principle that human physiology has meaningful seasonal variation is well-supported; the specific operational implications for any individual user are less precisely specifiable than the popular literature on this topic sometimes implies.

The seasonal recalibration that emerges from the literature is therefore less a programme than a principle: that the architecture of a life should be allowed to breathe with the year rather than being held rigid against it, and that the small adjustments which honour the biology of the season tend to produce a better-fitting structure than the more disciplined refusal to adjust at all.
