Occupational health researchers who study how workers fare across years and decades have a working observation that has shaped the field's empirical priorities for some time. The workers who maintain their health and performance over long careers are not always the ones who work the fewest hours; a substantial body of evidence shows that the relationship between working hours and outcomes is weaker and more conditional than the popular assumption suggests. The workers who fare best are those who, during the time they are not working, are genuinely not working — meaning that their cognitive engagement with work has actually paused, not merely that they have left the building. The variable doing the operational work in this distinction is what the field calls psychological detachment, and the gap between physical absence from work and psychological absence from work is one of the more consequential structural features of contemporary working life.

The conceptual articulation of detachment as a distinct construct is largely traceable to Sabine Sonnentag's research programme over the past two decades. Sonnentag and Charlotte Fritz, in a 2007 paper in the *Journal of Occupational Health Psychology* that has anchored much of the subsequent recovery literature, defined psychological detachment as "an individual's sense of being away from the work situation," operationalised through self-report items such as "I distance myself from work" and "I forget about work." Their empirical work, replicated extensively since, established that detachment during off-work hours predicts a range of subsequent outcomes — fatigue, sleep quality, mood, cognitive performance, and longer-term health markers — independent of total work time. The worker who logs off at six but spends the evening mentally rehearsing tomorrow's meeting has not, in any operationally meaningful sense, recovered; the worker who works late but achieves clean detachment afterwards may have. This is not the popular story about work-life balance, but it is the more empirically defensible one.

The structural problem is that knowledge work, the dominant form of work in advanced economies, has dissolved most of the natural cues that historically supported detachment. Industrial work was tied to a physical location and to physical tools; the act of leaving the factory floor was a structural disengagement from work that did not require psychological effort to accomplish. Knowledge work has neither of these properties. The work itself happens substantially in cognition, can be carried in the head from any location, and is now mediated by digital tools that follow the worker home. The boundary, in short, must be deliberately constructed because it is no longer structurally provided. Blake Ashforth, Glen Kreiner, and Mel Fugate's 2000 paper in the *Academy of Management Review* on what they termed micro role transitions established the conceptual framework for understanding how workers actually move between the work role and the home role across a typical day; the framework has since been substantially developed, but the central insight — that the transitions are not automatic and require deliberate structural support — remains. Most workers in the contemporary economy must manufacture, on a daily basis, a separation between work and not-work that earlier generations encountered as a built feature of their physical and temporal environment.

The empirical evidence on what supports and what undermines detachment is reasonably consistent. Communication technology use during off-work hours is one of the most reliably documented predictors of impaired detachment. Younghee Park, Charlotte Fritz, and Steve Jex, in a 2011 paper in the *Journal of Occupational Health Psychology*, demonstrated that workers who maintained higher levels of work-related communication technology use at home reported substantially lower psychological detachment and higher emotional exhaustion, with the relationship robust across different occupational categories. The mere availability of work email on a personal device, even when not actively checked, has been shown to impair detachment in subsequent research, because the cognitive system maintains a low-level monitoring state that competes with full disengagement. The implication is not that communication technology is intrinsically harmful, but that its use during off-work hours has structural consequences that the worker rarely registers in the moment and reliably underestimates.

The policy response to this problem has been instructive. France's right to disconnect legislation, which entered into force on January 1, 2017, requires firms with more than fifty employees to negotiate annually with employee representatives over the conditions under which workers can be expected to engage with work communication outside working hours. The legislation does not prescribe specific arrangements; it requires that the question be addressed deliberately rather than left to default. Several other jurisdictions have followed: Italy in 2017, Spain, Belgium, Portugal, and Ireland with varying approaches, and Australia under the Fair Work Act in 2024. The Australian provision, which came into force in August 2024, gives workers a statutory right to refuse to monitor or respond to work communications outside working hours unless the refusal is unreasonable. The empirical evidence on the actual effectiveness of these laws is mixed, partly because enforcement has often been weak and partly because cultural expectations about availability outpace formal legal frameworks. What the legal developments do indicate is that the underlying problem — the erosion of detachment under always-on communication norms — has been recognised at the policy level as severe enough to warrant statutory intervention, which is a significant institutional acknowledgement of what the occupational health literature has been documenting for two decades.

The practical implications for users of the Lifestyle pillar are reasonably specific, even where the broader policy environment provides little support. The most reliably effective intervention for impaired detachment is structural rather than psychological — removing the cues for work from the off-work environment, where possible, rather than attempting to suppress engagement through effort. Work email and messaging applications removed from personal devices outside working hours produce more reliable detachment than the same applications kept on the device with intentions to ignore them. A defined working location, even within a home, that is associated only with work produces more reliable detachment than work performed across multiple rooms. A defined finishing routine — closing the laptop at a specific time, performing a brief transition activity, communicating end-of-day completion — produces more reliable detachment than an indefinite tapering off. These interventions follow directly from the cue-binding mechanisms discussed in article 3.2 and from the environment design principles discussed in section 3.3; the present article applies the same principles specifically to the work-non-work boundary.

A note of honest qualification on the literature, which the popular framing has often oversimplified. Detachment is not always achievable, and in some occupations — emergency medicine, on-call roles, leadership positions during crisis — it cannot be expected on a continuous basis. The empirical evidence on detachment has been derived predominantly from studies of office and professional workers, and the recommendations may apply less directly to occupations with structurally different demands. The principle that detachment matters is robust; the practical means of achieving it must be calibrated to the actual demands of the role.

The deeper structural point, which connects this article to the broader Lifestyle architecture, is that the worker who has not constructed the boundary between work and not-work has not really finished work in the operational sense, regardless of what their calendar says — and the cumulative cost of incomplete detachment, integrated over the years that a working life lasts, is substantial enough to warrant treating boundary construction as a primary practice rather than as a residual one.
