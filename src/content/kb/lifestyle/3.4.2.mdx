In March 2022, an international panel of eighteen circadian researchers — including senior figures from Manchester, Harvard, Basel, Oxford, Northwestern, and Colorado, drawn from the labs that have produced most of the contemporary work on non-visual photoreception — published a consensus document in *PLOS Biology* on indoor light exposure. Their recommendations, expressed in a unit called melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance, were unusually directive: at least 250 lux during waking daytime hours, no more than 10 lux in the three hours before bed, and no more than 1 lux during sleep. The unusual feature of the document was not the mechanisms it described — those had been known for two decades — but the willingness of a large international consensus to issue prescriptive numerical targets on a topic that the field had previously been more cautious about. The Brown consensus, as it has come to be called after its lead author Timothy Brown, is the closest the light-and-health literature has yet come to a public health guideline, and it grounds most of what can usefully be said about the practical management of light exposure in daily life.

The recommendations map onto a biological asymmetry that has been understood since the early 2000s but has been slow to translate into practical guidance. The eye contains, in addition to the rod and cone photoreceptors that handle vision, a smaller population of intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells — ipRGCs — that respond to light independently of the visual system and project to the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the master circadian pacemaker. David Berson, Felice Dunn, and Motoharu Takao characterised these cells in a 2002 *Science* paper that identified them as the primary input to the human circadian clock, with peak sensitivity to short-wavelength blue light around 480 nanometres. The non-visual photoreception system is the mechanism by which environmental light tells the body what time it is, and the system responds to light very differently depending on when in the circadian cycle the light arrives. Daytime light strengthens and stabilises the rhythm; evening and night light disrupts it. The Body pillar treats the underlying neuroendocrine cascade in section 1.7; the Lifestyle contribution here is the practical management of the input that drives the system.

Daytime light exposure has three distinguishable effects: it increases the amplitude of the circadian rhythm, advances and stabilises the timing of the rhythm against environmental cues, and produces direct alerting and mood effects through hypothalamic and limbic projections. The Brown consensus places the daytime threshold at 250 melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance, a unit weighted to the response curve of melanopsin. Practically, this corresponds to roughly the level of light near a window on an overcast day, or a few minutes of direct outdoor exposure even in winter conditions. Most modern indoor environments — particularly during winter and in offices without windows — fall well below this threshold, sometimes by an order of magnitude. The implication is that for most users, the practical question is not whether to reduce daytime light exposure but how to substantially increase it.

Evening and nighttime light exposure operates in the opposite direction. Even modest light levels — below what most people would describe as bright — suppress the evening rise in melatonin, delay sleep onset, and shift the circadian rhythm later. Sean Cain and colleagues at Monash, in a 2020 study published in *Scientific Reports*, evaluated the impact of typical home lighting on the circadian system in a sample of nearly sixty households using a wearable spectrophotometer. The finding was striking: nearly half of the homes studied had evening light levels bright enough to suppress melatonin by fifty percent, with individual responses ranging from negligible suppression to almost ninety percent across the same exposure conditions. Homes with energy-efficient LED lighting had nearly double the melanopic illuminance of homes with older incandescent lighting. The Brown consensus recommendation of below 10 lux melanopic EDI in the three hours before bed is therefore quite restrictive — most rooms in most homes substantially exceed it during the evening — and the recommendation of below 1 lux during sleep is met by very few real bedrooms without specific intervention.

The mental health implications of these exposure patterns were quantified in a 2023 *Nature Mental Health* paper by Angus Burns, Daniel Windred, Sean Cain, and colleagues, who used the same UK Biobank actigraphy data underlying the sleep regularity findings to examine light exposure across nearly ninety thousand adults. The pattern was clean. Greater nighttime light exposure was independently associated with increased odds of major depressive disorder, generalised anxiety disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, psychosis, and self-harm behavior, with the highest exposure quartile showing roughly twenty to thirty percent increased odds across most outcomes. Greater daytime light exposure was independently associated with reduced odds of the same disorders. Both directions of effect held after adjusting for age, sex, ethnicity, photoperiod, employment, and physical activity. The Mind pillar discusses depression and anxiety at length in section 2.7; the Burns finding adds an environmental dimension that is specifically Lifestyle's territory: the light environment is one of the larger modifiable contributors to circadian-mediated psychiatric risk, and the intervention is structural rather than clinical.

What does this translate to in practice? The Brown consensus, when read against the broader empirical literature, supports a small set of recommendations that the user can act on without specialised equipment. The first is to maximise daytime light exposure, particularly in the morning, ideally outdoors but failing that near windows. Twenty minutes of outdoor light in the morning is a reasonable target for most users; longer is generally better, though the effect on circadian entrainment saturates at relatively modest exposure durations. The second is to reduce evening light exposure substantially, particularly in the three hours before bed. This means lower-intensity room lighting, less screen time at bright settings, and where possible warmer colour temperatures, which carry less of the short-wavelength light to which ipRGCs are most sensitive. The third is darkness during sleep — the Brown consensus recommendation of below 1 lux during sleep is met through some combination of blackout curtains, eye masks, and the elimination of LED indicator lights from the bedroom. None of these interventions is expensive; what they require is consistency.

The cross-pillar coordination on this topic is direct. Body covers the physiology of melatonin secretion, sleep architecture, and the consequences of circadian misalignment. Mind covers the cognitive and affective consequences of disrupted circadian function. The Lifestyle contribution is the environmental cue layer — the practical arrangement of light exposure across the day that sets the system's input. Article 3.4.1 in this section covers the consistency layer, which is the behavioral output. The two are operationally linked: a stable light exposure pattern supports a stable sleep schedule, and an irregular sleep schedule produces irregular light exposure, with each reinforcing the other. Most users who improve one will improve the other partly by default, and most who attempt to improve one in isolation will find the gains incomplete.

A final qualification, which the Brown consensus document itself emphasises, concerns individual variation. Recent laboratory work — including Phillips, Vidafar, Burns and colleagues' 2019 paper in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* — has shown more than tenfold variation between individuals in melatonin suppression in response to identical light exposures, and the underlying physiology of this variation is not yet understood. The recommendations are population-level guidance, designed to produce near-maximal beneficial responses across most healthy adults. Individuals at the more sensitive end of the distribution may benefit from more conservative evening light reduction; individuals at the less sensitive end may find the recommendations more restrictive than they need. The principle that morning light helps and evening light harms is robust across this individual variation; the specific thresholds at which each effect saturates are individual.

The light environment is among the cleanest examples of an environmental factor that quietly shapes a major dimension of health, and one of the few in which the empirical record now supports specific numerical guidance — which is itself a notable development in a field that has historically been more cautious about prescriptive claims.
