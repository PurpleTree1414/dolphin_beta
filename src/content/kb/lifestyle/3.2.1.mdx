In a 1982 experiment that has shaped subsequent thinking about habit at a mechanistic level, the experimental psychologist Christopher Adams trained two groups of rats to press a lever for a sucrose reward. One group received only modest training; the other received extensive overtraining. Adams then made the sucrose aversive — paired with a mild illness via lithium chloride — and observed what happened when the rats next encountered the lever. The modestly trained rats, having learned that the sucrose was now noxious, refused to press. The overtrained rats kept pressing. The behavior had detached from its outcome. This was, in mechanistic terms, the empirical signature of a habit: a behavior performed without sensitivity to whether the original reward still mattered.

This finding was extended into human subjects by Elizabeth Tricomi, Bernard Balleine, and John O'Doherty in a 2009 fMRI study, which showed that overtraining produced both behavioral insensitivity to outcome devaluation and a measurable shift of neural activity into the posterior dorsolateral striatum — the brain region most associated with habitual control. The picture emerging from this and adjacent research is that habits are not simply behaviors performed regularly. They are behaviors that have transitioned from one neural system, broadly described as goal-directed, to another, broadly described as habitual, with the transition characterised by a loss of responsiveness to the original goal.

Bernard Balleine and Anthony Dickinson, in a series of papers beginning in the late 1980s and consolidated in their 1998 review, formalised this distinction as the dual-process model of instrumental behavior. The goal-directed system is model-based: it represents the relationship between actions and outcomes and adjusts behavior when outcome value changes. The habitual system is model-free: it reinforces stimulus-response associations through reward feedback, without representing the outcome explicitly. Both systems are active in everyday behavior, and the balance between them shifts with experience. Early in learning, behavior is goal-directed; with sufficient repetition in a stable context, it becomes habitual. The transition is not all-or-nothing — research by Henry Yin and colleagues has shown that the two systems compete for behavioral control depending on context, recent reward history, and stress.

The neuroscience of this transition is most thoroughly characterised in the work of Ann Graybiel and her colleagues at MIT. Graybiel's 2008 review in *Annual Review of Neuroscience* synthesised two decades of research on the basal ganglia, identifying the dorsolateral striatum as central to habit learning and the dorsomedial striatum as central to goal-directed action. Her work also introduced the concept of action chunking — the observation that overtrained sequences become bracketed in striatal activity, with neural signatures marking the start and end of the sequence rather than each component action. A morning routine, on this view, is literally encoded as a single chunk in the brain, initiated by a cue and unfolding without separate decisions for each step. This is what creates the cognitive offloading benefit discussed in article 3.1.1. A habitual sequence does not require fresh deliberation for each component; the full chunk runs to completion once the initial cue is encountered. Body 1.12 covers the basal ganglia neuroscience at greater depth; the present article concerns the structural implications for how habits work as units of daily life.

One implication of the two-system structure has direct relevance to the cross-pillar architecture. Lars Schwabe and Oliver Wolf, in a 2009 study in the *Journal of Neuroscience*, demonstrated that acute stress shifts the balance of behavioral control toward the habitual system in human subjects. Stressed participants performed previously learned behaviors with reduced sensitivity to outcome devaluation; in everyday terms, stress promotes habit, including the habits a person has been working to break. This finding is the mechanistic basis for an observation made repeatedly in clinical practice: stress is when old habits return. The Mind pillar covers stress physiology in detail in section 2.7. What matters here is that habit formation and habit disruption interact with stress in ways that make the two pillars genuinely interdependent rather than parallel.

The popular framing of habits — the cue-routine-reward loop most associated with Charles Duhigg's 2012 book *The Power of Habit* — captures the essential structure but flattens the underlying research. The academic version, drawing on the Adams-Balleine-Graybiel lineage and synthesised in Wendy Wood and Dennis Rünger's 2016 review, is more precise. A habit, properly defined, is a learned association between a context cue and a response, where the response has become automatic through repetition. The reward is critical to the formation of the association — Wolfram Schultz's foundational work on dopaminergic prediction error, particularly his 1998 paper, established that the dopamine signal is what binds cue to response — but once formed, the habit can run without continued reinforcement. This is what the Adams devaluation experiment demonstrated: the rats kept pressing even when the reward had become aversive. The implication for any framework that aims to change habits is significant. Removing the reward, as the popular framing might suggest, is not necessarily sufficient to break a habit. The cue-response association persists. What can disrupt the habit reliably is removal or alteration of the cue, which is the topic of article 3.2.4.

Several practical implications follow. The first is that a habit is not a behavior performed willingly; it is a behavior triggered by context. This is why people who have decided to stop a habit nonetheless find themselves performing it when the cue arrives — checking the phone on hearing a notification, eating a snack on entering a particular room, reaching for a drink at a particular hour. The behavior is not a failure of will; it is the proper functioning of a learned association, performing exactly as designed. The second is that habit-tracking, as Dolphin operationalises it through the Habit Streak metric, is most useful when it tracks the cue-bound behaviors that are stable enough to count as habits in the technical sense, rather than treating any repeated behavior as habitual. A behavior performed deliberately every day for three weeks is not yet a habit by the criteria that matter mechanistically; Lally and colleagues' 2010 finding placed the median transition at sixty-six days, with substantial individual variation. The metric, calculated over a rolling weekly window, gives the user a continuous signal about whether the structure is holding while the underlying association consolidates.

A habit, in the strict sense the research supports, is not what someone does often but what their context produces in them, and the question of how a life is lived is in significant part the question of which contexts have been allowed to do that work.
