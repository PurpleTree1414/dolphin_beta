Across the comparative diet trials of the past two decades, an unusual pattern has emerged that has reshaped how serious nutrition researchers think about the structure of dietary advice. The pattern is the absence of meaningful between-diet differences. Bradley Johnston and colleagues, in a 2014 *JAMA* meta-analysis comparing weight loss outcomes across the major named diets — Atkins, Zone, Ornish, Weight Watchers, and several others — found that the differences in weight loss between diets at twelve months were small and largely indistinguishable from chance. Christopher Gardner's Stanford DIETFITS trial, published in *JAMA* in 2018, reached the same conclusion through a single large head-to-head study: assigning six hundred adults to either a healthy low-carbohydrate or a healthy low-fat diet for twelve months produced essentially equivalent weight loss in the two groups, with mean differences of less than two pounds. Liwei Ge and colleagues' 2020 *BMJ* network meta-analysis of fourteen named dietary programmes confirmed the broader pattern at a higher level of synthesis. The variable that did predict outcomes, in all of these analyses, was adherence — whether the person actually followed the assigned diet over time. The diet, in other words, mattered less than the doing of the diet.

This finding has been quietly revolutionary for the field, although the popular discourse on nutrition has been slow to absorb it. The implication is not that diet composition is irrelevant — extreme compositional choices clearly do matter, and the diets compared in these trials were all reasonable starting points — but that within the range of diets that competent nutrition science would endorse, the choice between them is dominated by sustainability rather than by any nutritional optimum. The user who follows a moderately good diet for ten years will be substantially healthier than the user who follows an optimally designed diet for three months and then abandons it. This is the empirical foundation for the Lifestyle pillar's framing of nutrition as a consistency problem rather than an optimisation problem.

The Body pillar covers the underlying nutrition science at depth in section 1.2, where the questions of what to eat — protein adequacy, fat composition, micronutrient density, the role of ultra-processed foods — are addressed in their own right. The contribution of the present article is the behavioral layer: the recognition that the question of what to eat is, for most users with already-reasonable diets, less consequential than the question of how consistently the chosen pattern is actually maintained. The two pillars handle different problems, and the cross-pillar architecture is necessary because the answer to either question alone is incomplete.

This is the empirical foundation underneath the Nutrition Consistency metric in the Lifestyle pillar, which contributes twenty-five percent to the live Lifestyle score — tied with Habit Streak for the highest weight in the pillar. The weight is not arbitrary, and the structural argument for it is worth making explicit. Three propositions, each well supported in the literature, justify the size. The first is that diet composition explains less of the variance in nutritional outcomes than diet adherence does, which means that tracking adherence is closer to tracking the outcome of interest than tracking specific nutritional choices would be. The second is that nutrition is one of the largest single modifiable contributors to long-term health, with effects on cardiovascular disease, metabolic disease, cancer risk, and cognitive function that are larger in magnitude than most non-pharmacological interventions. The third is that the variability of food intake across days is itself a measurable, behaviorally tractable quantity that the user can directly influence through structural choices about meal timing, planning, and environment. The metric is therefore measuring something behaviorally meaningful, with substantial health consequences, and which is genuinely under user control.

What does Nutrition Consistency actually capture? In the Dolphin implementation, it is calculated from food intake data — either logged directly through Dolphin's own interface or imported from established tools such as MyFitnessPal or Cronometer, which are addressed at greater length in article 3.5.2. The metric tracks the day-to-day variability of caloric intake, macronutrient distribution, and the regularity of eating windows across the recent measurement period. A user whose intake patterns are reasonably stable across days — neither extreme caloric restriction followed by compensatory overconsumption, nor wide swings in macronutrient composition, nor erratic eating windows — scores well. A user whose patterns are highly variable, regardless of the average composition, scores less well. The metric is agnostic about which specific diet the user follows; it cares about the consistency with which the chosen pattern is maintained.

A note of honest qualification on the consistency framing. The empirical case for nutrition consistency as a meaningful target draws on a more diffuse evidence base than, for example, the sleep regularity literature discussed in article 3.4.1, which has the advantage of a single well-validated index. The intake variability literature is less consolidated. There is reasonable evidence from the eating frequency and meal timing literatures — including work on time-restricted eating that has matured considerably since 2018 — that erratic eating patterns are associated with worse metabolic outcomes than regular ones. There is robust evidence from the dietary adherence literature that consistency over time is the operational variable separating successful from unsuccessful dietary change. The synthesis of these into a single consistency metric is a reasonable extrapolation, but it is an extrapolation rather than a direct replication of, say, the Windred et al. sleep regularity findings. The metric is supported by the field's general direction; the specific operationalisation will continue to be refined as the evidence base develops.

The practical implication of consistency-as-target is that the user's relationship to nutrition becomes a question of structure rather than of perfection. The Dolphin philosophy on this matter, articulated in the principle of progress over perfection, follows directly from the empirical record: the user who eats moderately well most days will, over time, achieve outcomes that the user who alternates between idealised eating and frank disregulation will not. The consistency target is forgiving of imperfect days while demanding stability across the time horizon at which nutrition actually shapes physiology, which is months and years rather than meals.

What the recent meta-analytic record on diet comparison has done, in effect, is to dissolve the older question of which diet is best and replace it with a different question entirely — which is whether the user can sustain whatever diet they have chosen — and to make clear that the second question, properly answered, contains most of what matters in the first.
