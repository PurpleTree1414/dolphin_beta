Environmental psychologists who work with architects, hospital designers, school administrators, and workplace planners report a working observation that has been remarkably consistent across these otherwise quite different domains. The same physical space can either support or undermine the behaviors that people are attempting to perform within it, and the difference between these two outcomes is rarely a matter of aesthetics or square footage. It comes down to the alignment between what the space affords — what it makes easy, what it makes visible, what it cues — and what its inhabitants are actually trying to do. Practitioners' working knowledge has converged on this point ahead of the academic literature catching up to formalise it. The term most commonly used to capture the principle is affordance.

The concept originates in the ecological psychology of James J. Gibson, whose 1979 book *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception* introduced the term to describe the action possibilities that an environment offers to a particular organism. A chair affords sitting; a flat horizontal surface at appropriate height affords being written on; a doorway affords passage. The affordance is not a property of the object alone, nor of the perceiver alone, but of the relationship between them. Gibson's argument was philosophical, but Donald Norman's 1988 book *The Design of Everyday Things* translated the principle into a practical doctrine for designers: a well-designed object is one whose affordances are visible and unambiguous, communicating its intended use without instruction. Norman's work became foundational to interaction design, and the affordance concept has since spread across architecture, urban planning, and digital interface design as the basic vocabulary for what makes a space or a tool support its intended use.

The practical application to habit and lifestyle design is straightforward in principle, though it requires a particular kind of attention. The relevant question for any space the user inhabits regularly is what behavior the space affords most easily, and whether that behavior is the one the user actually wants to be performing there. The bedroom is the cleanest example. A bedroom that contains a television, a desk, and a phone affords entertainment, work, and digital engagement in addition to sleep, and the cue structure of the room is therefore conflicted. The Mind pillar covers the cognitive arousal mechanisms that disrupt sleep onset in section 2.6, and the Body pillar covers sleep physiology in section 1.7; the Lifestyle contribution is the spatial arrangement that determines which cues the bedroom is presenting at the relevant times. A bedroom with a single dominant affordance — sleep — is a bedroom in which sleep occurs more reliably, not because the occupant is more disciplined but because the cue structure is no longer competing with itself.

A second example, drawn from one of the better-replicated findings in environmental psychology, concerns the role of natural exposure. Roger Ulrich's 1984 study published in *Science* examined surgical recovery rates among patients in a Pennsylvania hospital, comparing those whose rooms had a window view of trees against those whose rooms had a view of a brick wall. The patients with the natural view had shorter hospital stays, required less pain medication, and had fewer minor postsurgical complications, with all other factors controlled. The original study was small — forty-six patients across the two conditions — but the finding has been built on extensively in the decades since, and the broader literature on nature exposure has held up well in replication. Stephen Kaplan's Attention Restoration Theory, developed in a 1995 paper, provided one of the more useful theoretical accounts of why this should be the case: directed attention is a limited resource that becomes depleted under sustained cognitive demand, and natural environments allow attentional recovery in a way that built environments do not. Terry Hartig and colleagues' 2014 review in the *Annual Review of Public Health* synthesised the considerable subsequent literature and found consistent if modest effects of nature exposure on stress, attention, and mood across diverse research designs.

This is not a recommendation that a person needs a forest to be well, which would be impractical advice and not what the literature supports. The relevant principle is that some level of natural exposure, at some regular interval, appears to produce measurable benefits, and that the design of supportive spaces should ideally include some access to it where possible. A workspace with a window beats a workspace without one. A daily walk in a park beats no walk. A houseplant or two beats a sealed environment. The effect size of any single such intervention is small; the cumulative effect of consistently incorporating natural exposure into the spaces of daily life is real, in the way that the cumulative effect of small environmental adjustments tends to be.

The digital environment is, for many users, now a larger contributor to daily attentional state than the physical environment, and the same affordance principles apply with appropriate translation. A phone home screen organised around messaging and social applications affords interruption; a phone home screen organised around tools and reference materials affords more deliberate use. The notification environment — what alerts arrive, when, and how prominently — operates as an attentional cue structure with the same mechanics as physical cues. The Mind pillar discusses attentional fragmentation at depth in section 2.4; the Lifestyle contribution is the structural arrangement of devices and notifications that determines what the attentional environment actually is. Sophie Leroy's 2009 research on attention residue, examining the cognitive cost of switching between tasks, established that incomplete task transitions leave a residual cognitive load that degrades performance on the subsequent task, and the digital environment is the most prolific source of such forced transitions in most contemporary working lives. The design of digital affordances is therefore consequential for cognitive function in a quite literal way.

The broader principle that emerges across these examples is that supportive spaces are not necessarily beautiful, expensive, or elaborately designed. They are spaces in which the affordances align with the behaviors the inhabitant is trying to perform, and in which the affordances of competing behaviors have been deliberately reduced. This is rarely accomplished all at once. It is more commonly the result of accumulated small adjustments, each of which addresses a single mismatch between space and behavior, and the cumulative effect of which is a set of spaces that quietly support what the user is trying to do rather than quietly undermining it.

A final principle, often missed in the popular literature on space design, concerns differentiation. The Kaplans' work on restorative environments suggests that spaces designed for focus and spaces designed for restoration have different structural properties, and that an environment optimised for both is generally optimised for neither. The home office that doubles as the dining table, the bedroom that doubles as the workspace, the living room that hosts both quiet reading and high-stimulation media — each represents an undifferentiated space in which the cues for different activities compete with each other. Most homes do not have the luxury of dedicated rooms for every function, and the empirical literature does not require this. What it does suggest is that within whatever space is available, some functional differentiation is materially better than none, and that the differentiation can often be achieved through arrangement and visual separation rather than through additional square footage.

The room that supports the work, the bedroom that supports the sleep, the kitchen that supports the eating, the digital environment that supports the attention — these are not luxuries of design but the operational substrate on which the more visible parts of a life are performed.
