A counterintuitive finding from George Bonanno's research on responses to major life events — bereavement, serious illness, divorce, terrorism, exposure to disaster — has reshaped how the field of psychology thinks about human adaptation. Across multiple studies conducted over more than two decades, Bonanno and colleagues found that the most common response trajectory after even severe life events is not the prolonged distress that the popular framing assumes, but a relatively stable course of functioning that recovers within a few months to a year. The resilience trajectory, in Bonanno's terminology, accounts for somewhere between forty and sixty-five percent of cases in most studies, depending on the specific event and population. The minority pattern of prolonged dysfunction, while real and clinically significant, is not the modal response. The implication, articulated in Bonanno's 2004 paper in *American Psychologist* and developed extensively in his subsequent work, is that humans are substantially more capable of adapting to disrupted circumstances than the popular discourse on transitions has typically acknowledged.

The 2018 review by Isaac Galatzer-Levy, Sandy Huang, and Bonanno in *Clinical Psychology Review* synthesised the trajectory literature across more than seventy studies and confirmed the broad pattern. Across heterogeneous events and populations, four trajectories appeared with reasonable consistency: resilience (the modal pattern, characterised by minimal disruption to functioning); recovery (initial impairment followed by gradual return to baseline over twelve to twenty-four months); chronic dysfunction (sustained impairment); and delayed onset (initially intact functioning followed by later deterioration). The relative proportions of the trajectories varied with the type of event and the support context, but the broad pattern that resilience is more common than commonly assumed has held up across diverse research designs.

This does not mean that transitions are easy, or that the structural disruption they cause is minor. The empirical case for transitions as significant disruptions to lifestyle architecture is well-established, going back to Thomas Holmes and Richard Rahe's 1967 paper in the *Journal of Psychosomatic Research*, which introduced the Social Readjustment Rating Scale — the first systematic attempt to rank life events by their stress impact. Their scale, drawing on retrospective reports from a clinical sample, assigned weighted values to forty-three life events, ranging from the death of a spouse at 100 to minor violations of law at 11. Subsequent research has refined the specific weights and questioned some of the methodology, but the broader insight — that life events impose differential stress loads that can be quantified — has been robust across the subsequent literature. The weighted total of life events in a recent period predicts, with moderate reliability, subsequent health outcomes including illness, accident, and psychological distress, with the predictive validity holding across populations and decades.

What the resilience research adds to the Holmes-Rahe framework is the recognition that the same nominal event produces substantially different outcomes across individuals, and that the relevant variables governing the response are partly identifiable. Nancy Schlossberg's 1981 transition theory, articulated in *The Counseling Psychologist*, proposed a four-factor framework for understanding adaptation to transitions: the situation (timing, source, control), the self (resources, demographics, psychological characteristics), the support (interpersonal, community, institutional), and the strategies (coping repertoire). The framework has been substantially developed since but has held up well as a practical organising structure for understanding why two people facing nominally similar transitions produce different adaptive trajectories. The variables Schlossberg identified are largely the variables Bonanno's subsequent work confirmed as important, and the convergence of the clinical-counseling and the empirical-resilience literatures on similar conclusions is one of the more useful integrative observations in this area.

The structural implication for lifestyle architecture is direct, and it is the operational point this article concerns itself with. Routines, habits, and lifestyle systems built around one set of life conditions do not automatically transfer to a new set of conditions. The user who marries, becomes a parent, changes careers, relocates, or loses a primary relationship is, in lifestyle-architecture terms, operating in a partially new context for which the old architecture is partially obsolete. The expectation that the previous routines will simply continue under the new conditions is one of the more common reasons that life transitions produce extended disruption to wellbeing measurements: the routines are failing not because the user is failing, but because the conditions in which the routines made sense have changed. Article 3.1.1 made the related point that flexible systems hold their architecture even when contents shift; the transition case is the test of that flexibility.

The practical heuristic that emerges from the research is that life transitions warrant deliberate architectural revision rather than passive adjustment. The user who anticipates a major transition is generally better served by deliberately reconstructing the lifestyle architecture for the new conditions than by allowing the old architecture to slowly fail and then reactively patching it. The reconstruction can occur in advance of the transition where the timing is known (planned career change, anticipated parenthood, planned retirement) or shortly after the transition where it is not (sudden job loss, unexpected loss of a relationship, unanticipated health change). In either case, the practice that the resilience literature consistently identifies as supporting good outcomes is the early establishment of new daily and weekly structures that fit the new conditions rather than the persistent attempt to maintain the old ones.

A few specific dimensions of lifestyle architecture warrant particular attention during transitions. Routines, discussed in section 3.1, often need substantial revision because the cues and timing windows of the previous life have changed. Habits, discussed in section 3.2, are particularly vulnerable to transitions because the contextual cues that supported them may no longer be present, and existing habits may need to be re-anchored to new cues or replaced. Social connection, discussed in section 3.6, is often unintentionally disrupted by transitions because the recurring contexts that produced the connection have changed; deliberate maintenance of existing close ties and deliberate development of new contexts for connection are typically required rather than optional. Recovery practices, discussed in section 3.7.3, are often the first thing to go during transition periods, precisely when they would most matter; protecting them is often the highest-leverage intervention available during the disrupted period.

A note of honest qualification on the resilience research is warranted because the popular interpretation has sometimes overrun the empirical claims. The finding that resilience is the modal response does not mean that transitions are easy, that everyone recovers without intervention, or that the prolonged distress trajectory is somehow a failure of disposition. Some transitions overwhelm even strong adaptive systems, and the chronic dysfunction trajectory remains real and clinically significant. The empirical contribution of Bonanno's work is the correction of an earlier overestimate of universal trauma response, not a claim that transitions are unproblematic. The user navigating a major transition who finds themselves in extended distress is not necessarily failing at resilience; they may be in the minority pattern that the literature has consistently identified as warranting professional support.

What emerges across the transition literature is the recognition that the architecture of a life is partly a function of the conditions it was constructed in, and that the change of conditions imposes the work of partial reconstruction whether the user undertakes it deliberately or has it imposed on them by the accumulated failures of an unrevised structure — and the user who takes the deliberate path is generally the one who arrives at the other side of the transition with their wellbeing largely intact.
