Clinical dieticians who work with patients on long-term dietary change report a pattern that the academic literature has only gradually formalised. The diets that succeed in producing sustained behavior change at one year, three years, five years are rarely the most ambitious or the most rigorously designed. They are the ones the patient could imagine continuing indefinitely. Patients who lose substantial weight on highly structured short-term programmes — replacement shakes, very low-calorie diets, prescribed meal plans — almost universally regain it once the programme ends. Patients who make smaller, less dramatic changes that they integrate into their existing way of eating maintain more of their gains over time. The pattern is so consistent across decades of clinical experience that the working principle in long-term weight management has effectively become that a diet a patient cannot follow for ten years will not work.

The academic literature on dietary maintenance has converged on the same finding from a different direction. The National Weight Control Registry, established by Rena Wing and colleagues in 1994, has tracked individuals who have lost at least thirty pounds and maintained that loss for at least one year, and now contains data on more than ten thousand such individuals followed for periods extending past a decade. Wing and Suzanne Phelan's 2005 synthesis of the registry's findings, published in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*, identified the behavioral characteristics that distinguished successful maintainers. The characteristics were largely about consistency rather than about specific dietary content: maintaining a relatively stable eating pattern across weekdays and weekends, eating breakfast regularly, consuming a relatively low-fat diet (though not extremely so), and weighing themselves regularly. The maintainers did not, by and large, follow any particular named diet. They followed the diet they had developed in the course of their weight loss, with a high degree of consistency, for years.

This finding raises a structural question about how dietary change should be approached. The popular framing — adopt a programme, follow it intensively, achieve a target — is at odds with the maintenance evidence, which suggests that the intensity of the initial change is a poor predictor of its eventual sustainability and may even be inversely correlated. Alexander Rothman, Paschal Sheeran, and Wendy Wood's 2009 paper on the initiation-versus-maintenance distinction, discussed at length in article 3.2.4, applies directly here. The change that begins as an extension of one's existing pattern is more likely to integrate into long-term behavior than the change that requires the temporary suspension of one's existing life. The ten-year diet, to use the clinical adage, is more useful as a design constraint than as a literal claim.

The Mediterranean dietary pattern provides one of the cleaner empirical examples of this principle. The PREDIMED trial, originally published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 2013 and republished in corrected form in 2018 after a randomisation issue was identified in part of the original cohort, randomly assigned over seven thousand adults at high cardiovascular risk to one of three diets: a Mediterranean diet supplemented with extra-virgin olive oil, a Mediterranean diet supplemented with mixed nuts, or a low-fat control diet. The trial was stopped early after a median follow-up of just under five years because the Mediterranean diet groups showed a thirty percent relative risk reduction in major cardiovascular events compared with the low-fat control. The honest qualification on PREDIMED is that the original publication was retracted and republished due to randomisation problems in part of the cohort; the corrected analysis maintained the central finding, but the cleaner version of the trial is the republished one, and the specific magnitudes should be taken with appropriate caution. What is genuinely informative about PREDIMED, beyond the cardiovascular outcomes, is that adherence to the Mediterranean dietary pattern — a way of eating that resembles how millions of people across southern Europe have eaten for generations — was sustainable across years in a way that more restrictive interventions typically have not been. The diet's structural feature is not its specific composition but its compatibility with continuous human life.

The ten-year-test framing, applied seriously, generates a small set of practical questions for any user considering a dietary change. Could this be sustained on a normal working day? Could it be sustained on holidays, at family gatherings, when traveling, when ill, when unusually busy? Could it be sustained without specialised foods that require ongoing access to particular supply chains? Could it be sustained without continuous willpower? A change that fails any of these tests is at risk in a way the maintenance literature suggests is decisive, and the user is generally better served by selecting a less ambitious version that survives them than by selecting a more ambitious version that does not.

The cross-pillar coordination on sustainable nutrition is direct. Body's section 1.2 covers the nutrition science questions of what dietary patterns are most physiologically supportive — protein adequacy, fat composition, fibre intake, the role of ultra-processed foods, the evidence on specific dietary patterns including Mediterranean, DASH, and broadly plant-based approaches. The Lifestyle pillar's contribution is the adherence and sustainability layer: the recognition that whatever pattern Body's evidence supports must also be one the user can actually maintain across the time horizon at which nutrition shapes long-term health. The two pillars are operationally complementary. A nutritionally ideal diet that the user cannot sustain is, in the long run, worse than a nutritionally good diet that the user can sustain, because the integration over time strongly favours sustained moderate behaviour over intermittent ideal behaviour.

A final observation that the maintenance literature consistently surfaces concerns the role of the surrounding life in determining whether a dietary pattern is sustainable. The same diet that one user can maintain effortlessly is unsustainable for another user, not because of differences in willpower or dietary preferences, but because of differences in the surrounding architecture of the day — what time they eat, who they eat with, what is available, how much time they have to prepare food, whether their work schedule permits regular meals. Article 3.3 covered the broader role of environment in supporting behavior; the same principle applies with particular force to nutrition, because eating is a behavior almost wholly mediated by what food is in the immediate environment at the moment of hunger. Sustainable nutrition is, in operational terms, less a matter of choosing the right diet than of arranging the surrounding life so that the chosen diet is the path of least resistance most of the time.

The dietary choices that survive the years are the ones that have stopped being choices, in any moment-to-moment sense, and have instead become the texture of how the user lives.
