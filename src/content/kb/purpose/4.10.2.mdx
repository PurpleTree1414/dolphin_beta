The mistake people most often make when they try to re-align their lives is treating the problem as something a single decision could resolve. The mistake is intuitive. If the gap between stated values and revealed allocations has grown to the point where the person notices it, the gap feels like something that should be closeable in roughly the same way it became wide: through a definitive change, recognisable in retrospect as the moment when things turned. Some misalignments do resolve this way. Most do not. The empirical literature on sustained behaviour change supports a different pattern, in which re-alignment is more often produced by accumulated small choices than by single dramatic ones, and in which the architecture of those small choices matters more than the initial commitment that began them.

This is the recovery counterpart to article 4.9.2's misalignment diagnostic. Where the diagnostic treats how to recognise sustained drift between stated values and actual behaviour, this article treats the operational question of how to close the gap once it has been recognised. The two articles together describe a coupled process: the diagnostic surfaces the gap; the realignment work narrows it. Both processes operate slowly, and both involve substantially more architectural patience than the popular literature on values-driven change typically acknowledges.

Crystal Park's meaning-making model, which article 4.9.1 introduced, also provides the most useful frame for the recovery question. The model describes meaning as operating at two levels — global meaning, which encompasses foundational values and goals, and situational meaning, which is the appraisal of specific events as they happen. Distress arises when sustained accumulation of situational appraisals diverges from global meaning. Re-alignment work addresses the same gap from the other side. The person can do one of two things, and most actual recoveries involve some combination of both. They can adjust the situational stream — change what they spend time on, what relationships they invest in, what commitments they keep and which they release — so the data of weeks begins to fit the global framework again. Or they can adjust the global framework — recognise that some of the stated values had become aspirational rather than working, and that the actual values revealed by behaviour are not necessarily wrong; they may be more accurate than the verbal self-report acknowledged. The second move is the harder one and the one the popular literature avoids most strenuously. People rarely want to discover that their actual values have shifted in directions they had not chosen, and the work of acknowledging the shift before deciding whether to honour or reverse it is uncomfortable enough that it gets compressed into the more flattering narrative of merely needing to *get back* to the original values.

What does re-alignment actually look like operationally? The Sheldon self-concordance work, treated in article 4.6.1, gives part of the answer. Goals and commitments that pass the self-concordance test — that the person would defend the same way under different framings, that they can articulate the underlying values for in their own terms, that they would still endorse a year later — are the ones around which re-alignment is most reliably sustainable. The first move in re-alignment is therefore not changing behaviour but testing the values the changed behaviour would be supposed to express. Many failed re-alignment attempts fail because the person is trying to behave in service of values they would not actually endorse on careful examination, and the behaviour change fails to sustain because the underlying values do not support it. Sheldon's research consistently found that self-concordant goal pursuit produces durable behaviour change in ways that self-discordant goal pursuit does not. The implication is that the values-clarification work belongs before, not after, the behaviour change.

The acceptance and commitment therapy literature, treated in article 4.2.2, provides one operational framework for this kind of values-clarification work that has empirical support behind it. ACT's values-based interventions involve identifying the broader life directions the person wants to move in — what Steven Hayes and colleagues have called *valued directions* — and then committing to specific behavioural moves consistent with those directions, regardless of internal experience. The framework's strength for re-alignment work is that it treats values as orientations rather than as fixed entities, and that it makes the disconnection between values and behaviour the focal target of intervention. The framework's limitations include the empirical caveats article 4.2.2 already treated. ACT works for the populations and conditions it has been tested on; its application to the broader life-direction reorientation the popular literature has adapted it for is less well-supported.

The architecture of small accumulated choices, which article 4.4.3 treated under consistency, also applies to re-alignment work. A person who has drifted into sustained misalignment did so through accumulation; the re-alignment, in most cases, requires the same accumulation in the other direction. The mechanism is partly behavioural — small consistent choices in the value-aligned direction produce the patterns over which the alignment is rebuilt — and partly identity-related, treated in article 4.1.2. As the small choices accumulate, the person begins to recognise themselves as someone whose behaviour matches their stated values, and the recognition produces further alignment without requiring continuous willpower. The recursive loop is the same one article 4.8.3 described for meaningful work. The aligned identity sustains the aligned behaviour, which sustains the aligned identity.

What does this leave us with as honest practical guidance? The first move is the diagnostic from article 4.9.2: look honestly at what the data of recent months reveal about actual allocations, before deciding what changes to make. The second is the values examination: ask whether the stated values the misaligned behaviour appears to be misaligned *with* are actually working values or aspirational performances. The third is the architectural decision: identify two or three small behavioural changes that would produce visible movement in the value-aligned direction, and commit to them as recurring patterns rather than as one-time changes. The fourth, and the one most consistently underweighted in the popular treatments, is patience. Re-alignment produced through architectural accumulation is sustainable; re-alignment produced through dramatic resolution is unstable. The Reflection Frequency metric in Dolphin captures the diagnostic side of this process; the Goal Completion Rate metric captures the architectural side; the Weekly Alignment Score captures the experiential side. The three metrics together describe the re-alignment work the article treats. No single metric is sufficient. The work happens in the relationship among them, sustained across enough time for the small accumulated changes to register as larger pattern shifts.

Two qualifications belong with this material. The first concerns the cases the architectural-accumulation framing does not fit. Some misalignments are produced by structural conditions that no amount of small behavioural change can address — a person in an exploitative work situation, in a coercive relationship, in conditions of severe material constraint. The popular self-development literature routinely advises architectural accumulation for problems that require structural change, and the inverse error — advising structural change for problems that would respond to architectural accumulation — is equally common. The honest position is that the appropriate intervention depends on which kind of misalignment is operative, and that the diagnostic work article 4.9.2 treats is supposed to surface that distinction before any re-alignment work begins. The second concerns the time horizon. Re-alignment that has taken years to develop typically requires months to years to resolve, and the popular framings that promise quick recovery from sustained misalignment underrepresent the timeframes the empirical literature actually documents.

What was misaligned is rarely re-aligned through the same scale of change that produced the misalignment. The drift was slow. The work is slow. The slow work, sustained, is what produces the recoveries that hold.
