The popular literature on finding your values is enormous, and most of it has weak empirical support. This is the awkward truth that any honest treatment of the topic has to begin with. Values-clarification exercises have been a staple of self-help books, leadership workshops, and personal-development apps for at least four decades. The interventions vary in detail but tend to share a common structure: a long list of value-words is presented, the participant is asked to narrow it to ten, then five, then three, sometimes ranking the survivors and sometimes writing a paragraph about why each matters. The exercise feels productive. Whether it changes anything afterwards is a different question, and the answer is mostly no.

The original values-clarification movement, developed in the 1960s by Louis Raths, Merrill Harmin, and Sidney Simon for educational settings, was subjected to substantial empirical scrutiny in the 1970s and largely found wanting. The interventions did not reliably change behaviour in the directions predicted, and the theoretical framework — that simply identifying one's values would translate naturally into living by them — turned out to assume more than it could deliver. The contemporary echoes of that movement, repackaged for adult audiences, inherit most of the same limitations. A person can complete a values-clarification worksheet on Sunday and behave on Tuesday in ways that bear no relationship whatever to what they wrote down.

What does work, on the available evidence, is a different family of approaches grounded in what is now called the third-wave behavioural therapies. Steven Hayes, Kelly Wilson, and Kirk Strosahl's Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, formalised in the 1999 first edition and substantially expanded in the 2012 second edition, treats values not as items to be identified from a list but as chosen life directions to be discovered through a different procedure: examining what gives various life domains their personal weight, what one would want said at one's funeral, what one feels regret about not pursuing, what activities produce the experience of *vitality* even when they are difficult. The technique is procedural rather than declarative — it tries to get at values through the side door, by examining their footprints in actual experience, rather than asking the person to name them directly. The empirical record is reasonably strong. A 2015 meta-analysis by Joep A-Tjak and colleagues, covering thirty-nine randomised controlled trials and 1,821 patients, found ACT outperformed control conditions across mental and somatic health outcomes with moderate effect sizes (Hedges' g = 0.57), and a 2020 review by Andrew Gloster, Maria Walder, Marcel Levin, Michael Twohig, and Maria Karekla examined twenty meta-analyses of ACT outcomes across diverse populations and found broadly positive effects.

The interpretive caveat here matters. ACT is a therapy with multiple components — acceptance, defusion, present-moment awareness, self-as-context, values clarification, and committed action — and the meta-analytic evidence cannot cleanly attribute outcome gains to the values component alone. Process research suggests psychological flexibility (the capacity to act in line with chosen values in the presence of uncomfortable internal experiences) is the active mechanism, of which values clarification is one ingredient. So the strongest claim the evidence supports is not that values clarification *by itself* changes lives. It is that values clarification, embedded in a broader practice of psychological flexibility, contributes to outcomes that simple values-naming exercises do not produce.

What does this mean practically, for someone trying to identify what they actually value rather than what they think they ought to value? The procedural approach has three characteristic moves. The first is examining behaviour rather than self-report. The Schwartz Portrait Values Questionnaire, validated across forty-nine cultural groups in Sagiv and Schwartz's 2022 measurement work, asks people to indicate similarity to short descriptions of fictional characters whose values are inferred from their priorities. This is more useful than direct self-report because people answer indirectly, through judgment of someone else's choices, and the indirect route is harder to game with idealised self-presentation. A homemade version of the same logic — looking at one's calendar, one's bank statement, one's last six weekends — produces similar information. Where time and resources actually go is what one's behaviour reveals one to value, regardless of what one would say if asked.

The second move is examining absence rather than presence. The questions *what activities have I declined recently? what would I regret not having tried by the end of my life? what am I avoiding examining?* tend to reveal value commitments more reliably than questions about what one wants. This is partly because absence is harder to confabulate. The third move is testing values against trade-offs. A stated value that survives no actual cost is rarely the value it claims to be. When a person says they value family but routinely chooses work in the moments where the two genuinely compete, the operative value is the one their behaviour selects, not the one their self-description names. This is uncomfortable to notice. It is also the only route to information.

The Reflection Frequency metric in Dolphin operationalises this third route in a low-cost way. A person who reflects weekly on what they actually did, what they declined, and what felt important versus what felt obligatory accumulates the kind of behavioural record that the introspection literature treated in article 4.1.1 suggests external observers ordinarily provide. Over months, the pattern that emerges is a more accurate map of values-as-enacted than any one-time clarification exercise could produce. Claudia Russo and colleagues' 2022 systematic review of value-manipulation experiments confirms the related point: values do shift through deliberate engagement, but slowly, and through repeated structured exposure rather than through a single reflective session.

Two qualifications belong here. First, the popular framing in which a person discovers their *true* values through sufficient introspection is not supported by the research. Values, as treated by the empirical literature, are constructed and maintained through ongoing engagement with one's environment; they are not buried artifacts waiting for excavation. The procedural approach works because it leans on this — it produces values clarification through behaviour and reflection over time, not through a single moment of inward looking. Second, ACT's evidence base is strong but not unqualified. Critics including James Coyne have raised methodological concerns about specific trials, and the field has acknowledged that distinguishing ACT's specific contributions from those of cognitive-behavioural therapy more broadly remains empirically open. The procedural approach to values clarification has the strongest evidence base available in this territory; it is not the only approach, and the field's certainty has been overstated in popular treatments.

The values one would name in a worksheet are an unreliable signal. The values one's calendar reveals, examined honestly across enough weeks to surface the pattern, are the working ones. That gap is not a personal failing. It is the gap most people have, and closing it is what values clarification is actually for.
