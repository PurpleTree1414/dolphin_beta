The simplest diagnostic for sustained value misalignment is also the most uncomfortable. Examine where the time, money, and attention actually went over the past two months. Compare what they reveal against what the person would have named, asked, as the things that matter most to them. The gap between the two is the working definition of misalignment as the empirical literature operationalises it. A small gap is ordinary — values are aspirational, situations are constrained, and no one's actual allocation perfectly tracks their stated priorities. A sustained, growing gap that the person either does not notice or notices and cannot close is something different. It is the condition this article treats, and it is more common than the popular discourse on values acknowledges.

Article 4.1.3 treated alignment as the foundational construct — the operational understanding of what it means for stated values and behaviour to correspond. The present article treats its diagnostic counterpart. Where 4.1.3 was constructive and definitional, focused on what alignment is and how it is built, this article is acute and recognitional. The question here is what sustained misalignment looks like, what it tends to cost, and how it appears in the slow data of a life rather than in any single decision.

The behavioural pattern most consistently documented in the literature is incremental. A single small misaligned choice is easy to absorb. A pattern of small misaligned choices, sustained across enough weeks for the pattern to become structural, is harder to absorb and harder to notice from within. The mechanism is the one article 4.6.2 treated under the visible-yes-and-invisible-no framing: each accepted commitment is a yes whose displacement effects are diffuse, and the cumulative displacement is rarely noticed at the moment of any individual yes. People drift into misalignment one small commitment at a time, almost always without intent, and they typically register the drift only when its consequences have accumulated to a threshold the system can no longer mask. The threshold tends to involve some combination of energy depletion, relational friction, and the specific phenomenological experience of feeling that one's days no longer belong to oneself.

The burnout literature has documented one specific version of this with considerable rigour. Christina Maslach and Michael Leiter's Areas of Worklife model identifies values as one of six dimensions on which person-job mismatch produces burnout, alongside workload, control, reward, community, and fairness. The values dimension operates on its own mechanism. Workers whose jobs systematically require them to act against what they take to be their values — through deceptive practices they consider their work to be opposed to, through participation in institutional behaviours they would not defend, through pressure to compress or distort what they consider the work to be for — develop a specific kind of work-related distress that the depletion framework does not capture. The standard interventions for burnout — workload reduction, recovery time, better resources — do not resolve values-driven burnout, because the problem is not depletion. The problem is contradiction. The work is producing the experience of acting against the self, and no rest restores what continuous self-contradiction is wearing down. Lifestyle 3.9 carries the broader burnout treatment; the values-incongruence finding from Maslach and Leiter's work is the structural anchor for understanding why this particular kind of misalignment is so resistant to the usual recovery strategies.

What does sustained misalignment look like outside the work context specifically? The patterns generalise. A person whose stated relationships do not match the relationships their time and emotional energy actually go into; a person whose stated commitments to creative or developmental projects sit perpetually in the abstract while concrete time accumulates in domains they would not name as priorities; a person whose stated values around presence, generosity, or contribution have a steadily growing distance from the documented evidence of how their weeks were spent. In each case the same architecture is at work. The stated values function as a self-description the person genuinely endorses. The behaviour functions as a structural fact the person has lost the capacity to attend to honestly. The Weekly Alignment Score in Dolphin captures the experience of this gap when it is sustained: users in chronic misalignment report systematically lower alignment scores even when no single week's events would account for the pattern. The metric is sensitive to the slow signal precisely because it operates on the lived experience of the gap rather than on the architectural conditions that produced it.

Recovery from sustained misalignment is the explicit subject of articles 4.10.1 and 4.10.2, and a full treatment belongs there. What is worth saying briefly here is that the empirical literature does not support the popular framing in which a single decisive change — quitting the job, ending the relationship, abandoning the project — reliably resolves the underlying misalignment. Sometimes the structural change is necessary. More often the underlying misalignment was produced by gradual accumulation, and what resolves it is gradual reconstruction: a sequence of smaller choices that begin to close the gap incrementally, with the architecture rebuilt rather than torn down and replaced. The popular self-development literature emphasises the dramatic version because the dramatic version is narratively satisfying. The empirical record on sustained behaviour change emphasises the gradual version because the gradual version is what most actual recoveries look like.

Two qualifications belong with this material. The first is that the diagnostic itself depends on the reliability of the stated values, which articles 4.2.1 and 4.2.2 have already complicated. People's verbal self-reports of what they value are partially constructed performances, partly accurate descriptions, and partly aspirational projections of who they would like to be. A gap between stated values and behaviour can mean misalignment in the operational sense; it can also mean that the stated values were never quite the person's working values to begin with. The diagnostic question — examine what your behaviour reveals — has to be paired with a parallel diagnostic that asks whether the values being compared against the behaviour are themselves accurate. The Reflection Frequency metric in Dolphin supports both diagnostics simultaneously, which is part of why the metric is weighted as it is.

The second is that some apparent misalignment is structural rather than personal, and the popular framing routinely conflates the two. People living in high-constraint environments — sustained caregiving demands, severe financial pressure, work conditions that allow no meaningful autonomy over time allocation — sometimes have very little practical capacity to align their behaviour with their values. Telling such a person that their misalignment reflects a personal architectural failure is both inaccurate and unhelpful; the misalignment in such cases reflects the conditions, not the alignment work. The Purpose pillar treats this distinction because the inverse error is common in the popular literature, where structural constraint is regularly reframed as personal failing. The honest version of the misalignment diagnostic asks two questions in sequence. First: is there meaningful capacity to change the allocation given the actual constraints? Second: if there is, where is the gap and what would close it incrementally? Skipping the first question and applying the second universally produces predictable harm.

Misalignment is rarely the result of a wrong decision. It is more often the result of many decisions that were locally reasonable, made in sequence, in conditions that did not require any one of them to be examined against what the cumulative effect would be.
