In 2019, Lauren Eskreis-Winkler and Ayelet Fishbach published a paper in *Psychological Science* whose title was already a provocation: *Not Learning from Failure — the Greatest Failure of All*. Across five studies with a total of 1,674 participants, they tested what the popular self-development literature treats as obvious — that failure is an effective teacher. The methodology was elementary. Participants answered binary-choice questions on tasks ranging from foreign-language vocabulary to professional knowledge to social judgments. After each answer, they were told either that they had answered correctly (success feedback) or that they had answered incorrectly (failure feedback). Both types of feedback conveyed the same information, because in a binary-choice task knowing the wrong answer logically tells you the right answer. The participants were then tested on the same material a second time. The participants who had received success feedback consistently outperformed those who had received failure feedback, even though both groups had been given the same information in different framings. The provocation in the title was empirical. People do not learn from failure as well as they learn from success, even when the failure contains the same teachable information.

The mechanism Eskreis-Winkler and Fishbach proposed and tested across the five studies is straightforward: failure feedback is ego-threatening, and people tune out from ego-threatening information rather than processing it. When the researchers removed the ego component — having participants learn from observing others' failures rather than from their own — the asymmetry disappeared. People learned just as much from witnessing others fail as from watching others succeed. The problem is not failure as a category of information; the problem is failure as it is experienced by the failing self. The cognitive cost of attending to one's own failure is, for most people in most contexts, higher than the cost of attending to one's own success, even when the informational content is identical.

This finding has not gone unchallenged in the subsequent literature. The Eskreis-Winkler and Fishbach 2022 review in *Perspectives on Psychological Science* synthesises the broader picture, including the multiple replications and the boundary conditions identified in later work. A 2022 *Psychological Science* paper by Nina Keith and colleagues found that loss-framing of feedback and corrective explanation substantially mitigates the tune-out effect. A 2025 commentary by Stephen Spiller raised methodological concerns about the original Script Task paradigm, arguing that some of the apparent failure-to-learn-from-failure effect could be attributed to participants' tendency to answer consistently rather than to a true learning differential. The replies have refined the original claim without overturning it. The honest reading of the current state of the literature is that people learn less from failure than from success in many contexts; the gap can be reduced through specific framing interventions; and the popular *failure is the best teacher* framing is, as a general claim, not supported by the research.

This leaves the question of what failure does productively when it does. Amy Edmondson's 2023 book *Right Kind of Wrong*, drawing on three decades of her research on psychological safety in organisations, offers a typology that the popular *fail fast, fail often* framing misses. Edmondson distinguishes three kinds of failure. Basic failures are deviations from known correct procedures — forgetting to attach a critical document to an email, missing a step in a known protocol. Basic failures are not informative in interesting ways; they are usually traceable to inattention or process gaps and are best avoided rather than embraced. Complex failures emerge from interactions of multiple causes in familiar systems, like the well-documented industrial-accident pattern in which several individually-minor problems combine to produce a disaster. Complex failures are informative for system design but not generally productive as personal-development data. Intelligent failures, the only category Edmondson treats as genuinely growth-producing, are the undesired results of careful experiments in genuinely new territory — situations where the person could not have known the answer in advance, where the attempt was thoughtful, and where the failure produces information not otherwise available. The Silicon Valley *fail fast* framing tends to conflate all three categories under a single banner that valorises failure regardless of type. Edmondson's research suggests this conflation produces worse outcomes than the more discriminating approach. Most failures are not intelligent failures, and treating them as if they were degrades both the learning and the practice.

What this leaves us with is a more procedural account of failure-learning. The first move is distinguishing the type of failure encountered. The second is recognising the ego-threat structure that makes self-failures harder to learn from than other-failures or success information. The third is the deliberate construction of conditions that support learning from failure when it does occur: loss-framing rather than identity-attribution, corrective feedback that provides the structural alternative, and what Edmondson's broader research has called psychological safety — the social condition in which acknowledging failure does not produce status loss or punishment. None of these moves is unintuitive once stated; all of them are systematically violated in the popular *embrace failure* framing.

The Reflection Frequency metric in Dolphin treats one piece of this architecture. Regular structured reflection on failed goals, in a context where the reflection does not produce status consequences, is closer to the conditions the failure-learning literature suggests are necessary than the typical real-time self-criticism that follows failure in unsupported environments. Users who reflect on missed goals briefly and structurally, identifying the specific failure category and the specific informational content, accumulate the conditions under which failure can become learning. Users who fail goals and then either ignore the failure or absorb it into self-criticism without structural review do not.

Three honest qualifications. First, the failure-undermines-learning finding is real but moderate, and the popular treatments that draw the opposite conclusion are not entirely wrong — they are correctly identifying that failure can produce learning under specific conditions, while overestimating how naturally those conditions occur. The empirical reality is between the two popular positions. Second, the Edmondson typology is useful but not always operationalisable in real time. The category of *intelligent failure* sometimes only becomes clear in retrospect, and the popular adaptations of her framework often blur the distinctions in ways that lose the productive specificity. Third, the literature on failure-learning is largely conducted on individual decisions in laboratory or short-run organisational contexts. The application to the slower cumulative failures of personal life — the failure of a relationship over years, the failure of a career direction, the failure of an identity-defining project — is less directly supported by the research and warrants more caution than the popular adaptations suggest.

Failure is not the great teacher the popular framing makes it. It is a teacher whose lessons are hard to receive, whose value depends on conditions most people do not naturally create, and whose information is often easier to extract from watching others encounter it than from encountering it oneself.
