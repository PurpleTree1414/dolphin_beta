In 2008, Elizabeth Dunn, Lara Aknin, and Michael Norton published a paper in *Science* that has become one of the more cited findings in the prosocial behaviour literature. The methodology was elementary. The researchers gave participants either $5 or $20 and randomly assigned them to spend it on themselves or on someone else by the end of the day. That evening they were assessed for happiness. Participants who had been assigned to spend the money on others reported greater happiness than participants who had spent it on themselves, and the difference held regardless of the amount. The effect was small in the original sample but suggested a counterintuitive structural claim: the relationship between money and happiness depends substantially on what the money is spent on, with spending directed toward others producing more happiness than spending directed toward the self.

The subsequent research record is more nuanced than the popularisation of this finding sometimes implies. Lara Aknin and colleagues' 2013 *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* paper, drawing on survey data from 136 countries, found that the prosocial-spending-and-happiness association replicated cross-culturally as what they characterised as a psychological universal, with effect sizes that varied by national context but rarely reversed direction. The fMRI evidence — including studies showing that prosocial decisions activate brain regions associated with reward processing — has been consistent with the behavioural findings. But the picture has not been uniformly clean. A 2022 *PLOS One* replication of the original 2008 experiment by Garam Kim and colleagues, using a substantially larger sample, did not reproduce the main effect at conventional significance levels, though a supplementary analysis using a more direct happiness measure did show an effect in the predicted direction. The Dunn-Aknin-Norton review in *Current Directions in Psychological Science* (2014) treats the field's broader evidence base; the effect is real, smaller than initial reports suggested, and conditional on factors including whether the prosocial behaviour is autonomously chosen, whether it produces a sense of social connection, and whether the impact is visible to the giver.

What distinguishes the prosocial-wellbeing relationship empirically is that it does not appear to operate through generic altruism. The 2013 Aknin paper specifically tested the conditions under which prosocial spending produces happiness gains and found three factors mattered consistently: prosocial spending produces happiness most reliably when the giving satisfies the giver's psychological needs for relatedness (connection to the recipient), competence (a sense of having made a real difference), and autonomy (the act being genuinely chosen rather than externally pressured). The mechanism is consistent with the broader self-determination theory framework treated in article 4.3.1. Giving that fulfils need-thwarting conditions — required donations made under social pressure, charitable contributions that feel coerced, prosocial acts that produce no sense of impact — does not reliably produce the happiness gains the popular literature has come to expect from generosity.

The deeper pattern emerges from a different research tradition. Robert Waldinger and Marc Schulz's 2023 book *The Good Life*, synthesising eighty-five years of data from the Harvard Study of Adult Development, is the most recent consolidation of one of the longer-running longitudinal studies on adult psychological development. The study began in 1938 with 268 Harvard sophomores, expanded to include a parallel sample of inner-city Boston youth, and has followed the original participants and their descendants across the full arc of adult life. The headline finding, repeated across multiple synthesising treatments by successive study directors over the decades, is that the strength and quality of close relationships is the most consistent predictor of both physical health and subjective wellbeing across the lifespan, outperforming income, professional achievement, IQ, and most personality variables. The replication of this finding across multiple methodological treatments and subsequent confirmatory studies has been substantial, and it is not particularly contested in the underlying research literature, even where popular treatments of the Waldinger work have run ahead of the more measured claims the original studies support.

The convergence between the prosocial-spending research and the long-term relationships research points toward a more specific empirical claim than the popular *giving makes you happy* framing supports. The dimension of life that the data most consistently identifies as protective and wellbeing-promoting is not service in the abstract but sustained relational engagement — the actual relationships one is embedded in, the connections one tends, the people one is available to. Service that produces relational connection appears to be the active ingredient; service that does not produce relational connection has weaker effects. This explains why prosocial spending in the Aknin experiments worked best when the spending produced a sense of connection to the recipient, why anonymous large donations produce smaller happiness gains than smaller donations within networks of relationship, and why the volunteering research has consistently found that high-contact volunteering produces wellbeing gains that low-contact volunteering does not.

The implication for the Purpose pillar is that *service and impact* is not best operationalised as a generic prosocial orientation but as a more specific question about how the user is embedded in relationships of mutual support and contribution. The Lifestyle pillar's work on social connection (Lifestyle 3.6) treats the broader epidemiology of relationship and health. The Purpose contribution is the meaning-architecture layer: the question of whether one's relationships are producing the experience of mattering to others and having others matter to oneself, in ways that the person can defend as expressive of their values rather than as obligations they would not endorse on reflection.

Three honest qualifications. First, the prosocial-spending literature is genuinely mixed on replication. The original 2008 finding has been replicated in some studies and not in others, and the 2022 Kim et al. failure-to-replicate suggests the effect is more conditional than the initial framing implied. The honest reading is that prosocial spending under conditions of relatedness, competence, and autonomy produces happiness gains; prosocial spending without those conditions produces smaller effects or no effects. The popular framing of *spend money on others to be happier* drops the conditions that make the prescription work. Second, the Harvard Study of Adult Development has limitations that are widely acknowledged in the underlying literature and frequently elided in popular treatments. The original sample was extraordinarily unrepresentative — initially 268 men selected from the Harvard sophomore class, drawn from a narrow demographic and socioeconomic slice that did not include women at all. The findings about relationships have been replicated across more representative samples, but the specific claims drawn from the original Harvard cohort should not be treated as if they generalise without qualification. Third, the broader literature on service-and-wellbeing has documented failure modes the popular treatments rarely acknowledge. Volunteering and caregiving under coercion or chronic strain — eldercare for declining parents, sustained work in helping professions without adequate support, prosocial behaviour that depletes the giver — produces measurable wellbeing harms rather than gains. The framework of service-as-meaning works under specific conditions; it is not a general prescription that survives application to all forms of giving in all circumstances.

Service that produces meaning is service that produces connection. Service that produces connection is service the person could not give as easily without the person they are giving it to.
