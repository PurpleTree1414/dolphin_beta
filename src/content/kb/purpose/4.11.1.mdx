A twenty-year-old, a forty-year-old, and a seventy-year-old asked what gives their lives meaning will, on average, give substantially different answers. The pattern across the answers is not random. The twenty-year-old is more likely to name relationships still being built, possibilities still open, identities still being tried on. The forty-year-old is more likely to name commitments already made — to people, to work, to projects — and the question of whether the commitments have been worth what they cost. The seventy-year-old is more likely to name what has been lived through, the relationships that endured, the things that turned out to matter when much else did not. This is one of the more replicated patterns in life-span developmental psychology, and it is the empirical foundation on which any honest treatment of meaning across the lifespan rests.

The most useful contemporary theoretical framework for understanding the pattern is Laura Carstensen's socioemotional selectivity theory, developed across four decades of work and consolidated in her 2021 *Annual Review of Psychology* paper *Socioemotional Selectivity Theory: The Role of Perceived Endings in Human Motivation*. The theory's central postulate is that perceived time horizons — not chronological age — drive the shift in what gives life meaning. When future time is perceived as expansive, as it typically is in earlier adulthood, motivation favours knowledge acquisition, social network expansion, and the kinds of experience-seeking that build toward an open-ended future. When future time is perceived as limited, as it typically becomes with age but also under conditions of mortality salience that can occur at any age, motivation shifts toward emotionally meaningful goals, deepening existing relationships, and savouring rather than accumulating. The empirical evidence for the theory is substantial. Carstensen and colleagues' experimental work has shown the shift can be induced in younger adults by priming time-limitation, and eliminated in older adults by priming time-expansion. The pattern, the research consistently supports, is about the perception of remaining time, not about the chronological age that usually correlates with it.

Erik Erikson's earlier stage theory of psychosocial development, treated already in articles 4.1.2 and 4.8.3, remains influential and deserves more careful treatment than it usually receives. The stage framework — eight psychosocial tasks across the lifespan, each centred on a specific developmental challenge — has had real empirical support for some of its proposed stages, particularly identity development in adolescence and generativity in middle adulthood. It has had less support for the strict sequential structure Erikson proposed, the universalism of the stages across cultures, and the specific resolution criteria attached to each stage. The honest position on Erikson is that he identified developmental challenges that subsequent research has confirmed as real and operationalised more rigorously, while the specific stage architecture has been treated more as a heuristic than as a strict empirical model in the current literature. Dan McAdams's narrative identity research, also treated earlier in the pillar, extends this in a useful direction: rather than fixed stages, the McAdams framework describes recurring narrative tasks that operate across the adult lifespan with different emphases at different times.

The popular literature has flattened this developmental complexity into the *midlife crisis* concept, which has had a more turbulent empirical history than its cultural prominence suggests. David Blanchflower and Andrew Oswald's 2008 work identified what they called the U-shape of happiness across the lifespan — a cross-sectional pattern in which life satisfaction declines from young adulthood, reaches a nadir somewhere in the late forties, and rises again into later life. The framework was popularised through *The Economist*, Jonathan Rauch's 2018 book *The Happiness Curve*, and substantial media coverage. The subsequent academic literature has been more sceptical. Nancy Galambos and colleagues' 2020 *Perspectives on Psychological Science* paper argued the U-shape is far less robust than its popular treatments suggest — present in some cross-sectional data and absent in others, varying by country and cohort, and obscured by considerable individual variability. Felix Bittmann's 2021 cluster analysis across 81 countries identified three distinct age-related happiness patterns rather than a single U-shape. By 2025, Blanchflower himself, working with Jean Twenge, reported that the U-shape appears to have *vanished* in recent data from English-speaking countries, with young adults now reporting lower happiness than older adults in patterns that no longer fit the original framework. The careful position is that life satisfaction varies across the lifespan in patterns that are real but more individual-specific and cohort-dependent than the popular framing implies, and that *midlife crisis* as a universal phenomenon is not supported by the strongest evidence.

What the developmental research does consistently support is that the content of meaningful activity shifts across life stages in ways that the Carstensen framework captures well. Earlier adulthood is, on average, more concerned with identity formation, mate selection, career establishment, and the building of social networks. Middle adulthood is, on average, more concerned with generativity — the production of contributions, the mentoring of younger people, the building of institutions and creative work that will outlast the contributor. Later adulthood is, on average, more concerned with integration — the work of making sense of the life that has been lived, deepening the relationships that remain, and approaching the closure of the personal narrative. These shifts are statistical patterns rather than individual fates. Many individuals do not follow them; the variation around the mean is substantial. The patterns describe what populations look like in aggregate. They do not prescribe what any particular life should contain.

The implication for the Purpose pillar's architecture is that the constructs the pillar has treated — values, identity, motivation, goals, meaning, contribution — do not operate identically across the lifespan. The same value commitments may be expressed differently in different stages. The same identity material may be foregrounded or backgrounded depending on the developmental moment. The same purpose architecture may be built, sustained, lost, and rebuilt multiple times across a long life. The empirical record does not support the popular framing in which purpose is something one discovers once and maintains thereafter. It supports a more dynamic framing in which purpose is something one constructs at every stage, with the construction taking different forms as the time horizon and the developmental moment shift.

Two qualifications belong with this material. The first is that the stage frameworks are at best heuristics, not laws. They describe statistical patterns across populations, not invariant sequences in individual lives. A user whose actual developmental experience does not match the stage framework's predictions is not deficient; they are reflecting the substantial individual variation the population-level patterns mask. The second is that the popular literature on life stages has often co-opted the academic frameworks for purposes the research does not support — the rigid prescriptions about what one should be doing at twenty, forty, or sixty, the marketing of midlife reinventions as universal projects, the elision of socioeconomic and structural conditions that profoundly shape what any particular stage actually involves. The Carstensen framework is honest about this. The popularised versions usually are not.

The question that organises a life at twenty is not the question that organises the same life at sixty. What does not change is the question itself: what would I want this stretch of time, however long it turns out to be, to have contained.
