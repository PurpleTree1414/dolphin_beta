What does it mean for a person to live in alignment with their values? The question sounds clear until one tries to operationalise it for empirical research. By 2024, when Constantine Sedikides and Rebecca Schlegel published their integrative review of authenticity in *Nature Reviews Psychology*, the construct had splintered into four distinct theoretical positions, each generating its own measures and its own findings. Sedikides and Schlegel identify these as authenticity-as-self-accuracy (the candid processing of identity-relevant information), authenticity-as-self-consistency (the alignment of behaviour with internal standards, goals, and values), authenticity-as-self-ownership (the experience of acting from one's own agency rather than external pressure), and authenticity-as-self-enhancement (the experience of expressing what one regards as one's positive core). The four positions overlap but do not collapse into one another, and the empirical literature on each is shaped by which definition it began from.

For the Purpose pillar, the relevant operationalisation is the second: alignment as the relation between what a person says matters to them and what their behaviour reveals to actually matter. This is the framing Kennon Sheldon and Andrew Elliot established in their 1999 self-concordance model. A goal is self-concordant when it represents a person's enduring interests and self-defining values rather than external pressures or borrowed expectations. Self-concordant goals, in their longitudinal data, were both more likely to be sustained through difficulty and more likely to produce wellbeing gains when achieved. Subsequent cross-cultural replications, including Sheldon and colleagues' 2004 study across United States, Chinese, South Korean, and Taiwanese samples, found that the self-concordance effect held across cultural contexts that differ markedly in their emphasis on individual versus collective goal selection. The construct travels.

The mechanism Sheldon and Elliot proposed is direct and, on the available evidence, robust. When a goal expresses what a person genuinely values, sustained effort is more available because the goal does not need to be defended against the rest of the self. The cognitive and emotional resources that would otherwise be spent on internal negotiation — *do I really care about this; is this what I should be doing; whose goal is this anyway* — can be deployed on the work of the goal itself. The opposite is true for goals adopted to satisfy external expectation, social pressure, or contingent self-worth. Such goals are not weaker as goals; they are more expensive to maintain. Each act of pursuit costs against an internal contradiction that the more aligned goal does not generate. The wellbeing gap between concordant and non-concordant goal pursuit, replicated across multiple decades and methodologies, is one of the more reliable findings in the goal-striving literature. Recent work by Ehrlich, Bipp, and colleagues, published in 2024, has extended self-concordance theory by examining how it relates to the more recently developed goal-striving-reasons framework, finding that both predict eudaimonic wellbeing through somewhat different pathways.

The diagnostic question this opens, for someone trying to assess their own life rather than a research participant's, is uncomfortable: what would the data show? If a sympathetic but uninformed observer reviewed how someone actually spent the past month — what they did, what they declined, what they postponed, what they rushed — would the values they would infer match the values the person would describe if asked directly? The two converging often is what alignment is. The two diverging often is what misalignment is. The honest answer requires looking at behaviour rather than at intention, and most people are not in the habit of examining themselves this way.

The values-behaviour gap is itself well-established as an empirical phenomenon, descended from the broader intention-behaviour gap that Peter Gollwitzer's work on implementation intentions made central to behavioural science (and which article 4.5.1 treats at length). People reliably overestimate the consistency between their stated values and their actual time and resource allocations. Some of this is plain motivated reasoning. Some of it is the introspection limit treated in article 4.1.1 — people simply do not have the kind of behavioural record on themselves that would let them notice the gap unaided. The gap is not, in most cases, evidence of moral failure. It is evidence that values are more legible in retrospect than in moment-to-moment choice. Alex Wood, Stephen Joseph, and colleagues' authenticity research, the foundational work for the Authenticity Scale published in 2008, operationalised this directly: their three-factor model treats self-alienation, accepting external influence, and authentic living as distinct components, each independently associated with wellbeing outcomes. The first two are the failure modes; the third is the sustained accomplishment.

The Dolphin Weekly Alignment Score, the simplest of Purpose's four metrics, is an honest acknowledgement of what the literature on subjective wellbeing measurement has established: a single, well-framed question about lived experience captures alignment more reliably than longer instruments aggregating fragmented self-reports. The question — *how aligned did your week feel with what matters most to you* — does not ask the user to perform self-knowledge. It asks them to register a state. The signal is rough by intent. What it tracks across weeks, across months, across the slower drift that characterises most actual life, is whether a person's relationship to their own days is moving toward integration or toward fragmentation. This is the diagnostic counterpart to article 4.9.2 Value Misalignment, which addresses what to do when the score, sustained over weeks, points clearly toward the latter.

Two qualifications. First, the construct of authenticity is contested in psychology in ways the popular discourse rarely acknowledges. Sedikides and Schlegel are explicit in their 2024 review that the field has not converged on a single definition, and they argue the empirical evidence is in fact stronger for the self-enhancement view than for the self-consistency framing used here. The Purpose pillar takes a position rather than pretending there is consensus: alignment is treated behaviourally because the self-consistency operationalisation has the strongest evidence base for predicting sustained goal pursuit, and because behaviour is the only dimension Dolphin can actually track. Other operationalisations are defensible; this one is the operationalisation that matches the metric. Second, there is no reading of the empirical literature that supports the popular framing of a hidden, essential, perfectly-knowable "true self" against which one's life can be measured for fidelity. Strohminger, Knobe, and Newman's 2017 work suggests that this folk-essentialist intuition is widespread and cross-culturally stable, but its widespread occurrence is not evidence of its accuracy. Alignment, in any responsible reading of the research, is not the relation between one's life and one's hidden essence. It is the relation between one's stated values and one's actual choices, observed over enough time for a pattern to emerge.

The Lifestyle pillar's work on identity-based habit formation (Lifestyle 3.2) carries the downstream implication: when alignment is sustained, the daily habits that emerge from a particular identity become available without internal negotiation. Misaligned habits cost in friction; aligned habits cost in less. The Purpose contribution sits one level upstream — at the question of what values the habit architecture is meant to express in the first place.

Alignment is not a feeling about oneself. It is the slow disclosure of what one's actual life is for.
