A practice that began as a clinical observation about elderly patients has accumulated, across six decades, one of the more impressive bodies of empirical support available in the psychotherapeutic literature. Robert Butler, a geriatric psychiatrist working with older adults in the early 1960s, observed that his patients spent substantial portions of their time reviewing their lives — recalling significant events, evaluating their choices, working through unresolved conflicts, integrating positive and negative memories into coherent narrative. The clinical literature at the time treated this process as evidence of cognitive decline or pathological withdrawal. Butler argued the opposite. In his 1963 paper in *Psychiatry*, he proposed that life review was a normative developmental process that emerged naturally in later life as people approached the closure of their personal narratives, and that the process could be facilitated therapeutically to support what Erik Erikson had called ego integrity — the eighth and final stage of psychosocial development, in which a person comes to terms with the life they have lived. Butler's reframing changed the field. Subsequent research, developed across multiple decades by Gerben Westerhof, Ernst Bohlmeijer, Martin Pinquart, and others, has produced an empirical base for life review interventions that is among the most replicated in geriatric psychotherapy.

The most recent meta-analytic synthesis is Lin and colleagues' 2024 *Journal of Affective Disorders* paper, which examined 42 randomised studies covering 3,361 older adults with depression and reported a large effect of life review therapy and reminiscence on late-life depression — a Hedges' g of 1.41, corresponding to a number-needed-to-treat of approximately two. Pinquart's 2024 update extended the picture across depression, anxiety, and quality-of-life outcomes, finding consistent though smaller effects than the late-life-depression specific finding. The earlier Pinquart and Forstmeier 2012 meta-analysis covered 128 studies and established the effect across a broader range of outcomes including ego integrity, well-being, and meaning in life. Westerhof's 2019 work in *Clinical Psychology: Science and Practice* tightened the methodological criteria and confirmed moderate effects for depression and ego integrity. The literature is unusually consistent for a psychotherapeutic intervention; the effects replicate across cultures, settings, and operationalisations of the protocol.

What is life review, in the empirical operationalisation the research has refined? The practice is structured rather than free-form. Participants work through their lives chronologically, in sessions guided by a therapist or facilitator, focusing on different life stages — childhood, adolescence, young adulthood, middle adulthood, later life — and on specific themes within each stage. Both positive and negative memories are addressed. The work involves not merely recalling but evaluating: what the events meant at the time, what they came to mean later, how they fit into the larger pattern of the life, what they would mean if framed differently. The goal is not pleasant nostalgia or score-settling but integration — the production of a coherent narrative that includes the difficult material rather than excluding it, and that allows the person to recognise the life as theirs in a way the unintegrated narrative did not. The McAdams narrative-identity research treated in article 4.1.2 provides the broader theoretical framework for understanding why this work matters. Life review is the natural setting for the narrative reconstruction the McAdams programme identifies as central to identity coherence across the adult lifespan.

The temporal scale here is different from the weekly reflection treated in article 4.10.1, and the difference matters operationally. Weekly reflection examines the data of a recent period — a week, a month — and surfaces patterns that the user can act on within similar timeframes. Periodic life review examines the data of years or decades, and the patterns it surfaces operate on a different cognitive register. The weekly reflection answers questions like *what did the past week actually contain, and how does it compare with what I had intended it to contain?* The life review answers questions like *what has the past decade come to mean, and how does the meaning fit with the meaning I had originally assigned it?* The two practices are complementary. The weekly reflection captures the small data that the life review will eventually integrate. The life review captures the larger arc that the weekly reflection cannot see from inside its own timeframe.

The application beyond geriatric populations is less empirically settled, and any honest treatment has to acknowledge this. Most of the rigorous life review research has been conducted on older adults, typically over sixty, often with depression or substantial life events that motivated the intervention. The generalisation to midlife adults, to younger adults at major life transitions, or to anyone simply interested in the practice as a regular tool of meaning-making, is plausible but not as well-established. The construct of ego integrity that grounds Butler's original framework is, in Erikson's theory, the developmental task of later life specifically. Whether the practice produces similar benefits when applied at different life stages is partly an open empirical question. The popular adaptations of life review into midlife memoir-writing workshops, *life mapping* exercises, and structured autobiographical journals draw on the methodology without the same empirical anchoring. The honest position is that the practice appears beneficial in older adults with substantial evidence and may be beneficial earlier in life with much thinner evidence.

What kind of cadence does the empirical research support for periodic life review outside the formal clinical protocol? The structured therapeutic versions typically involve weekly sessions across eight to twelve weeks. The non-clinical adaptations vary widely; the popular framings sometimes suggest annual or seasonal life reviews, sometimes longer intervals. The empirical record does not support a single optimal cadence. What it does support is that the practice should occur at intervals long enough to allow new material to accumulate — a life review conducted too frequently risks becoming repetitive recall of the same accumulated narrative rather than integration of new material into the larger pattern. The natural intervals appear to be years rather than weeks or months. A user reviewing their life every quarter is probably reviewing the same material with diminishing returns; a user reviewing every five or ten years, or at major life transitions, has accumulated enough new material for the practice to do its integrative work.

Two distinct failure modes belong with this material. The first is the drift from integration into nostalgia. Periodic life review, performed without the evaluative dimension Butler emphasised, can become extended pleasant recall of positive memories while excluding the difficult material the integration work requires. The popular adaptations are particularly vulnerable to this drift, and the empirical record on life review specifically depends on the evaluative dimension; pleasant reminiscence without integration does not produce the same effects. The second failure mode is the inverse: life review that drifts into score-settling, where the practice becomes extended replay of grievances, missed opportunities, or perceived failures. This is the rumination pattern article 4.10.1 treated, applied to the longer temporal scale. Life review done as sustained rumination produces the same kind of harm as weekly reflection done as rumination, and the empirical distinction between the two forms holds across the longer timeframe as well.

The implication for the Purpose pillar's structural treatment of reflection is that the practice operates at multiple temporal scales, each with its own purpose and its own failure modes. Weekly reflection captures the immediate data. Periodic life review captures the longer arc. The two together describe what reflective practice across a life actually involves. The next article, the final article of the entire knowledge base, treats how the architecture across all four pillars holds together across the longer arc still — the lifelong arc within which weekly reflection and periodic life review are themselves nested practices.

A life examined once is a life partly seen. A life examined at the natural intervals — when there is enough new material for the examination to integrate — is a life that, over years, becomes its own slow accumulation of recognition.
