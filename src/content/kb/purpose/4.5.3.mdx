In 2009, four organisational psychologists — Lisa Ordóñez, Maurice Schweitzer, Adam Galinsky, and Max Bazerman — published a paper in *Academy of Management Perspectives* whose title alone signalled the seriousness of their argument: *Goals Gone Wild: The Systematic Side Effects of Overprescribing Goal Setting*. The paper provoked a sharp rejoinder from Edwin Locke and Gary Latham, the founders of the modern goal-setting research tradition, and a counter-rejoinder from Ordóñez and colleagues. The exchange remains one of the more honest scholarly debates about the limits of an established intervention. Goal-setting, the Ordóñez paper argued, is one of the most replicated paradigms in the management literature, and its beneficial effects are real and well-documented. They are also accompanied by systematic harms that the field has been slow to acknowledge. The authors identified specific side effects: a narrow focus that neglects non-goal areas, distorted risk preferences, a measurable rise in unethical behaviour, inhibited learning, corrosion of organisational culture, and reduced intrinsic motivation. The metaphor they used was medical: goal-setting is a potent medication, capable of producing both beneficial effects and serious adverse events, and it should be carefully prescribed rather than dispensed as a benign cure-all.

The Locke and Latham response argued that the side effects Ordóñez and colleagues identified had largely been documented within the goal-setting research programme itself, and that critiques drawing on anecdotal evidence and rhetorical metaphor breached the principles of good scholarship. The substantive disagreement, however, concerned the prescriptive implications. Both sides agreed the side effects exist. They disagreed about whether goal-setting should be applied more cautiously and selectively or whether the existing prescriptions adequately accounted for the failure modes. For our purposes here, the substance matters more than the dispute. The literature documents specific, replicable failure modes of goal-setting. Any honest treatment of healthy goal pursuit has to identify them.

Six failure modes recur across the empirical literature. The first is goal substitution, sometimes called Goodhart's Law in the economics tradition or Campbell's Law in the social-policy tradition: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The mechanism is that people reliably optimise for the measurable proxy of a goal rather than for the underlying construct the proxy was meant to track. Schoolteachers whose performance is measured by student test scores teach to the test rather than to the underlying education. Sales teams whose performance is measured by revenue book sales they expect to be cancelled rather than building durable customer relationships. Personal goal versions of this are equally common: a person who tracks daily steps starts taking fragmented walks they would not otherwise take, optimising for the metric rather than for the underlying fitness the metric was meant to track. The trap is structural; it is not a failure of the user but a predictable consequence of measurement.

The second is goal proliferation. Carol Dweck and her collaborators, alongside the broader self-regulation literature, have repeatedly found that holding many active goals simultaneously degrades pursuit of each. The cognitive cost of switching, the distributed attention, and the diluted commitment all impair the focal goal-shielding effect treated in article 4.5.1. People typically can sustain robust pursuit of two to four genuine goals at once. Beyond that, the additional goals tend to function as displacement rather than as expansion — they consume the cognitive resources the focal goals require while producing little real progress on themselves. The popular practice of setting many goals at the start of a year and then attempting to make progress on all of them is a particularly clean example of the failure mode.

The third is goal substitution in the within-person sense — what the goal-fatigue literature calls *the substitution of easy goals for hard ones over time*. As the original difficult goal encounters friction, the person revises it downward, often without acknowledging the revision. The revised goal feels achievable; achievement of the revised goal feels like progress; the underlying ambition has decayed without the person noticing. This is not a failure of motivation but a failure of meta-cognition — the inability to track one's own goal-revision honestly enough to distinguish meaningful adjustment from defensive shrinkage.

The fourth is hedonic adaptation post-achievement, which is the structural reason outcome-focused goal-setting often produces less satisfaction than its proponents predict. Sonja Lyubomirsky and Kennon Sheldon's 2019 *Journal of Positive Psychology* paper, revisiting their sustainable happiness model, treats the empirical record on this directly: gains in subjective wellbeing from goal achievement decay rapidly toward baseline, particularly for outcomes the person can become accustomed to (income, status, possessions). The phenomenon does not refute goal pursuit; it refutes the implicit theory that achieving the goal will produce the durable wellbeing the person anticipated. Goals organised around the hedonic payoff at the end consistently disappoint relative to the prediction; goals organised around sustained engagement with the process, treated in article 4.5.2, produce smaller per-moment gains but more durable accumulated satisfaction.

The fifth is escalation of commitment, treated already in article 4.4.1 through Barry Staw's foundational research. People consistently over-invest in failing courses of action when the alternative requires acknowledging that the original commitment was a mistake. The mechanism is well-documented: the original investment becomes a sunk cost the person treats as warranting protection, and additional resources are committed to defend the prior commitment rather than to maximise expected value going forward. Goal-pursuit is particularly susceptible to this failure mode because the goal itself becomes part of the person's identity, and abandoning the goal feels like abandoning a piece of the self. Article 4.6.1 will treat the commitment-revision question directly.

The sixth is the displacement of meaningful but unmeasurable life from the goal-tracked life. A person who only counts what is measured tends, over time, to live what is counted. Relationships, contemplative time, undirected creative wandering, the slow background processes by which insight accumulates — none of these fit neatly into goal frameworks, and a life heavily organised around tracked goals can systematically squeeze them out. The person achieves the goals at the cost of the life the goals were originally meant to serve. The Ordóñez critique addresses this directly under the heading of *narrow focus that neglects non-goal areas*.

What follows from these failure modes is not that goals should be abandoned. The empirical case for goal-setting under appropriate conditions remains robust, and the alternative — drift, default, the absence of intentional structure — has its own substantial costs. What follows is that goal-setting should be deployed with the same caution one would apply to any potent intervention. Goals should be selected sparingly, reviewed honestly, and embedded in a broader life that contains substantial ungoaled time and unmeasured commitments. The Reflection Frequency metric in Dolphin operationalises the review function. The Weekly Alignment Score operationalises the broader-life check — whether the goals are pointed at something the user actually values, or whether they have decoupled from the underlying values they were meant to serve.

Three honest qualifications. First, the dark-side literature on goal-setting has its own conceptual difficulties. Some of the side effects the Ordóñez paper identified — distorted risk preferences, unethical behaviour — emerged in laboratory settings that may not generalise cleanly to personal goal-pursuit, and the Locke-Latham response is correct that the field had documented many of these failure modes within its own research before the 2009 critique consolidated them. The dispute is genuinely live in the academic literature; the popular self-improvement literature has been slower to incorporate either side. Second, the framing of goals as *traps* implies the person is being harmed against their will, which sometimes describes the situation and sometimes does not. Many of the failure modes treated here require the person's active cooperation, and the cooperation is itself driven by deeper architecture — values, identity, the upstream layers articles 4.1 and 4.2 treat. The traps are most dangerous when the upstream architecture is weak and the goals are doing more identity work than they should. Third, the metric weight assigned to Goal Completion Rate is not an endorsement of more goals as universally better. The metric measures the rate at which set goals become completed actions; it is silent on whether the user is setting too many goals, too ambitious goals, or goals pointed at the wrong things. Those upstream questions belong to the values and direction articles, and a user with a high Goal Completion Rate on poorly-chosen goals is not in the architectural position the metric weight would suggest. The metric is a useful signal, not a sufficient one.

The goal that gets completed is not the same as the goal worth setting. Knowing the difference is the work the goal itself cannot do.
