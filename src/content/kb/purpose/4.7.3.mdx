In 2018, Victoria Sisk, Alexander Burgoyne, Jingze Sun, Jennifer Butler, and Brooke Macnamara published two meta-analyses in *Psychological Science* that complicated one of the more influential popular psychology stories of the previous twenty years. Carol Dweck's growth-mindset framework, developed over four decades of research and widely adopted in education, business, and parenting literatures, had argued that students who believe their abilities can be developed through effort — growth-mindset students — outperform students who treat their abilities as fixed traits. The framework had become enormously influential, generating school interventions across many countries, corporate training programmes, parenting bestsellers, and an entire popular vocabulary around *fixed* and *growth* mindsets. The Sisk meta-analyses examined both the strength of the relationship between mindset and academic achievement (across 273 studies, 365,915 participants) and the effectiveness of mindset interventions on academic achievement (across 43 studies, 57,155 participants). The findings, in the authors' own summary, were that *overall effects were weak*. Mindset accounted for approximately 1% of the variance in academic achievement. Mindset interventions, on average, produced small effects that did not consistently translate into educational outcomes.

The picture became more complicated rather than less in the subsequent years. In the same period, David Yeager and a large consortium of researchers ran the National Study of Learning Mindsets, the largest experimental evaluation of a mindset intervention ever conducted: a brief online intervention delivered to a nationally representative sample of 12,490 ninth-graders in the United States, with academic outcomes followed up across the high school transition. The results, published in *Nature* in 2019 with Carol Dweck as a co-author, found genuine but modest effects. The intervention improved grades among lower-achieving students and increased enrolment in advanced mathematics courses, but only in specific school contexts — particularly schools where peer norms supported challenge-seeking. The effect size for the targeted population was small but non-trivial, roughly 0.1 standard deviations. The intervention did not improve outcomes for higher-achieving students, did not work in all school contexts, and did not produce the dramatic gains the popular adaptations of growth-mindset theory had claimed.

The most recent and most rigorous meta-analysis, conducted by Macnamara and Burgoyne and published in *Psychological Bulletin* in 2023, examined 63 distinct growth-mindset intervention samples with attention to methodological quality. After controlling for study quality, the meta-analytic effect was statistically non-significant. A parallel meta-analysis by Jeni Burnette and colleagues in the same year, also in *Psychological Bulletin*, examining 53 distinct samples, found a small significant positive effect (d = 0.14) with substantial heterogeneity across studies. The two meta-analyses, examining largely overlapping literature with different methodological choices, reached different conclusions. The field is genuinely divided. The honest summary, treated by Carol Dweck and David Yeager themselves in their 2019 *Perspectives on Psychological Science* paper *Mindsets: A View from Two Eras*, is that the original framework was correct in identifying implicit theories of ability as a measurable construct that varies across individuals and is malleable through specific interventions. The framework was overstated in its popular adaptations, where growth-mindset interventions were treated as broadly transformative when the empirical evidence supports more modest, context-dependent effects.

What survives the methodological scrutiny is a more modest version of the original claim. People do hold implicit theories about the nature of ability — whether intelligence, athletic capacity, or social skill is fundamentally fixed or fundamentally developable through effort and strategy. These theories shape how people respond to setback. Fixed-mindset beliefs are associated, in observational research, with more avoidance of difficulty, more disengagement after failure, and lower persistence in challenging domains. Growth-mindset beliefs are associated with greater willingness to take on difficult tasks, more constructive responses to failure, and higher persistence. The correlations are real but moderate. The causal pathway from changing the beliefs to changing the outcomes is real but smaller than the popular framings imply, and works most reliably for specific subpopulations under specific conditions.

The implication for Purpose is that the *adaptive mindset* construct, in the version the empirical literature actually supports, is one piece of the broader architecture rather than the dominant explanatory variable popular adaptations treated it as. Mindset belief alone does not produce the outcomes; mindset beliefs interact with environmental conditions, peer norms, structural opportunities, and the specific architecture of how challenges are framed and feedback delivered. A user who adopts a growth-mindset orientation but operates in an environment that punishes visible struggle, or that lacks the structural feedback necessary for skill development, will not see the outcomes the popular adaptations promised. A user whose environment supports challenge-seeking, who is in a learning phase where development is possible, and who treats setbacks as informative rather than diagnostic, will see more of the predicted benefits — but the gains will be smaller and slower than the popular framings imply.

This connects to articles 4.7.1 and 4.7.2 in a specific way. The growth-from-discomfort literature treated in 4.7.1 establishes that development happens at the upper edge of capacity, under conditions that support sustained engagement. The failure-learning literature treated in 4.7.2 establishes that failure is informative under specific conditions and ego-threatening under most. The adaptive-mindset construct sits at the cognitive layer of this architecture: it is the belief structure that allows discomfort to be interpreted as growth-opportunity rather than as identity-threat, and failure to be processed as information rather than as evidence of fixed incapacity. The belief structure is real and consequential. It is also one input among several rather than the central determinant the popular adaptations made it.

Three honest qualifications. First, the field is currently in the middle of an active methodological dispute about the magnitude of growth-mindset effects, and any synthesis available now will be revised by subsequent research. The 2023 Macnamara-Burgoyne and Burnette meta-analyses reached different conclusions from the same broad literature. The careful position is that the construct is real and the interventions produce small effects in some populations under some conditions; stronger claims in either direction are not currently supported. Second, the popular adaptation of growth-mindset theory in corporate and educational contexts has frequently involved interventions that bear only loose resemblance to the research-validated protocols. *Growth mindset* as corporate jargon, used to dismiss employees' concerns about structural constraints with appeals to attitude, is not what the research supports and has produced its own backlash. Third, the construct interacts with structural conditions in ways the popular framings rarely acknowledge. Telling students from under-resourced schools that their academic difficulties reflect a fixed mindset, when the difficulties actually reflect under-resourced schools, conflates a real construct with a structural problem and produces predictable harm. The research itself, particularly the Yeager 2019 finding that effects depend on contextual support, has been clearer about this than the popular adaptations.

An adaptive mindset is not the belief that effort guarantees success. It is the belief, calibrated against actual conditions, that current capacity is not the same as future capacity.
