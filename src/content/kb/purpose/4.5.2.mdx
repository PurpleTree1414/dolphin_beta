In 2022, a systematic review and meta-analysis by Christopher Williamson, Dustin Lochbaum, Marc Hoover, and Marcia Lochbaum examined the performance and psychological effects of different types of goals across the sport-and-exercise literature. They synthesised twenty-seven studies meeting strict inclusion criteria and reported effect sizes that ought to unsettle anyone who still treats goal-setting as a uniform intervention. Process goals — goals focused on how one performs during the activity — produced an effect on performance of d = 1.36, very large by any standard. Performance goals — goals focused on a specific level of achievement — produced d = 0.44, medium. Outcome goals — goals focused on the result of competition or comparison with others — produced d = 0.09, statistically indistinguishable from setting no goal at all. The gap between process goals and outcome goals on the same dependent variable was an order of magnitude. The kind of goal one sets, in this evidence, mattered more than the fact of setting one.

This finding is consistent with a longer tradition in achievement-motivation research. Andrew Elliot's work on achievement goal orientations, refined across three decades and most recently consolidated in the 2 × 2 framework that distinguishes mastery-approach, mastery-avoidance, performance-approach, and performance-avoidance goals, has consistently found that mastery-approach orientations — goals organised around personal improvement and task mastery rather than around outperforming others — produce more durable engagement, more effective learning strategies, and more sustainable performance over time. The body of literature is large enough that it has its own meta-analyses and its own subfield disputes, but the central pattern has held: orientation toward the process of getting better outperforms orientation toward beating a benchmark or outperforming others, particularly across longer time horizons and more complex tasks.

Why does the difference matter so much? Three mechanisms emerge from the literature. The first is locus of control. Process goals are about what the person does; outcome goals are about what eventually happens. The former is reliably under the person's control; the latter is partly contingent on factors the person does not influence — competitors' performance, situational conditions, luck. Goals organised around what one cannot fully control produce more anxiety, more disengagement when feedback is poor, and weaker sustained motivation over time. The second mechanism is the feedback structure. A process goal generates ongoing feedback as the activity unfolds — *am I executing the technique I committed to?* — while an outcome goal generates feedback only at the resolution. This makes process goals more diagnostically useful for adjustment and more psychologically containing through the long stretches of effort that outcomes require. The third mechanism is hedonic structure. Outcome achievement, in the well-replicated hedonic adaptation literature, produces a brief satisfaction that fades within weeks or months; the satisfaction of process engagement, when the process is genuinely engaging, persists across the duration of the activity. People who organise their lives around outcome attainment chase a satisfaction that recedes; people who organise their lives around process engagement participate in a satisfaction that is continuously available.

Atul Gawande's research on surgical checklists, summarised most accessibly in his 2009 book *The Checklist Manifesto* and grounded in a series of rigorous trials including the World Health Organization's 2008 Safe Surgery Saves Lives Study published in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, illustrates the point at organisational scale. The intervention was elementary: a brief pre-surgical checklist of process steps, completed verbally by the surgical team before each operation. Across eight hospitals on four continents, mortality rates fell from 1.5% to 0.8% and complication rates fell from 11% to 7%. The checklist did not change the difficulty of the surgeries, the quality of the surgeons, or the complexity of the cases. It reorganised the team's attention from the outcome (successful surgery) to the process (each step completed in sequence). The process-oriented intervention produced outcome improvements that direct outcome focus had not been able to achieve. The pattern recurs across high-stakes performance domains.

The implication for personal goal-setting follows. The goal *I will write a book this year* is structurally an outcome goal; it produces feedback only at the resolution and is partly contingent on factors the writer does not control. The goal *I will write five hundred words a day, six days a week* is structurally a process goal; it produces daily feedback, is fully under the writer's control, and accumulates the book as a side effect of the practice. The two goals, applied to the same eventual outcome, predict very different sustained behaviour. The outcome-goal version produces higher anxiety, more all-or-nothing thinking about whether the project is on track, and more frequent abandonment when feedback is unclear. The process-goal version produces lower anxiety, clearer daily decisions, and more reliable accumulation. The book at the end is, in Gawande's logic, a side effect of the process executed.

This is also the architecture James Clear popularised in *Atomic Habits* through the framing *you do not rise to the level of your goals; you fall to the level of your systems*, a popularisation of older ideas that captures the practical implication well even when its empirical anchoring is less explicit than the underlying research deserves. The framing is correct in its central claim: outcomes are downstream of processes, and process design is therefore where the leverage lives. The framing is incomplete in that it occasionally treats outcome goals as unnecessary, when in practice they serve a useful function — they tell the person whether the process is producing the results the process was meant to produce, and they warrant adjustment of the process when results diverge from intent over long enough horizons.

The deeper conceptual link runs through the deliberate practice literature, originating in Anders Ericsson's research on expert performance and refined across multiple subsequent decades. Deliberate practice, in Ericsson's foundational framing, is not the accumulation of hours but the structured engagement with specific aspects of performance that lie just beyond current ability, with immediate feedback and conscious refinement of technique. The construct is process by design. A musician practising deliberate-practice scales is engaging with a process specification — *play this passage at this tempo with this attention to articulation* — rather than with an outcome specification. The 10,000-hour rule popularised by Malcolm Gladwell substantially mischaracterised Ericsson's claim, as Ericsson himself documented in his 2016 book *Peak* and in subsequent interviews. The original research did not claim that 10,000 hours of any kind of practice produces expertise; it claimed that approximately 10,000 hours of *deliberate* practice — the structured, feedback-rich, process-specified kind — characterises elite performers in skill-based domains. The popular framing dropped the qualifier, and the resulting expectation that mere accumulation of time would produce mastery has been subsequently weakened by Brooke Macnamara's 2014 meta-analysis and subsequent work, which found deliberate practice explained substantially less of the variance in elite performance than Ericsson's framework predicted. The qualifier matters; the structured-process specification of the practice is what produces the gains, and the popular treatments that drop it produce the disappointment one would predict.

The Goal Completion Rate metric in Dolphin treats this asymmetry honestly. The metric measures completion of the goals the user has set, regardless of whether those goals are framed in process or outcome terms. The interpretive layer the Purpose articles supply is that users completing process goals at high rates are usually building durable architecture; users completing outcome goals at high rates may be doing the same or may be cherry-picking achievable outcomes; and users completing very few goals of either type are usually facing a setting problem rather than a doing problem. The pattern is more diagnostic than the raw number, which is why the Reflection Frequency metric weights the qualitative review of completion patterns alongside the quantitative track. The Lifestyle pillar's treatment of habit architecture (Lifestyle 3.2) carries the related downstream point: process goals translate into habits more naturally than outcome goals do, because the process specification is already in the form a habit requires.

Three honest qualifications belong here. First, the process-versus-outcome dichotomy is conceptually messier than the popular treatments suggest. Many goals are hybrid — they specify both a process and a desired outcome, and the question is which dimension the person attends to in moments of difficulty. The clean dichotomy of the meta-analyses reflects studies designed to compare extremes; in practice, most personal goals sit somewhere in the middle of the spectrum and the optimisation question is more nuanced than *use process goals*. Second, pure process focus has its own failure modes. A person who attends solely to process and never reviews whether the process is producing meaningful results can sustain disciplined practice for years toward outcomes that no longer matter. The process discipline is not a substitute for the periodic outcome review that articles 4.10 and 4.11 treat directly. Third, the achievement goal orientation literature has its own replication concerns. Some specific findings within the 2 × 2 framework, particularly around mastery-avoidance goals, have shown inconsistent effects across replications, and the field has not entirely converged on which orientations produce which outcomes under which conditions. The broader pattern — process and mastery orientations outperforming outcome and performance orientations on most outcomes that matter — is robust. Specific predictions within the framework are less so.

The goal you reach is the side effect. The process you maintained is what you actually did.
