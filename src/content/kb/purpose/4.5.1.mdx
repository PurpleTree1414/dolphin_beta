The most useful empirical finding in the entire goal-pursuit literature is also the most uncomfortable: people generally do not do what they intend to do. Paschal Sheeran and Thomas Webb's 2016 paper in *Social and Personality Psychology Compass*, synthesising decades of research on what they called the intention-behaviour gap, opens with the line *bitter personal experience and meta-analysis converge on the conclusion that people do not always do the things that they intend to do*. The size of the gap, in their synthesis, is consistent across populations and domains. A medium-to-large change in intention typically translates into a small-to-medium change in actual behaviour. The slippage is not a personal failing of unmotivated individuals. It is a structural feature of the relationship between intending and acting, and any honest treatment of goal-setting has to begin from this finding rather than from the popular assumption that strong intentions reliably produce action.

The research on what closes this gap converges on a small number of high-leverage interventions, of which one stands out for the consistency and magnitude of its effects. Peter Gollwitzer and Sheeran's 2006 meta-analysis, covering 94 independent studies and over 8,000 participants, found that forming an implementation intention — a specific *if-then* plan linking a situational cue to a particular action — produced medium-to-large effects on goal attainment, with a pooled effect size of d = 0.65. The technique is elementary in form. Instead of intending to exercise more, the person forms the implementation intention *if it is Tuesday at six p.m., then I will leave my apartment and walk to the gym*. The cue is concrete. The action is specific. The decision has been made before the situation arrives. The mechanism Gollwitzer and his collaborators have identified across decades of subsequent research, including Frank Wieber, J. Lukas Thürmer, and Gollwitzer's 2015 review of physiological correlates, is that implementation intentions effectively delegate goal pursuit from conscious self-regulation to automatic situational triggering. The cue arrives; the action initiates. The motivational negotiation that would otherwise be required at the moment of choice has been pre-empted.

This is the structural foundation on which the rest of healthy goal-setting rests. The classical goal-setting framework, developed by Edwin Locke and Gary Latham across more than five decades and consolidated in their 2019 retrospective in *Motivation Science*, identifies three core mechanisms that make goals effective: specificity (concrete goals outperform vague aspirations), difficulty (challenging goals outperform easy ones), and commitment (goals genuinely owned by the person outperform externally imposed ones). The Locke-Latham framework remains the most empirically supported account of goal-setting available in psychology, with hundreds of replications across organisational, educational, and personal contexts. Its limitations, treated in article 4.5.3, are equally well-documented. The framework predicts goal-pursuit effects reliably; it has less to say about which goals are worth pursuing in the first place, a question that belongs to the upstream territory of articles 4.2 and 4.6.

The popular SMART goals framework — specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound — is sometimes presented as the practical translation of Locke-Latham theory, but the relationship is looser than the popular framing implies. SMART originated as a managerial heuristic in a 1981 *Management Review* article by George Doran, not as a research-derived recommendation. Its empirical validation has been thin. The framework's requirement that goals be achievable runs directly against Locke-Latham's finding that more difficult goals outperform easier ones. Its emphasis on measurability, while useful for some goals, has been shown in multiple studies to drift toward the easily-measurable rather than the genuinely meaningful. SMART is not bad as a starting checklist. It is not a replacement for the actual research, and the research has moved well past it.

Beyond implementation intentions, three additional findings from the recent literature shape what healthy goal-setting actually looks like. Benjamin Harkin and colleagues' 2016 meta-analysis in *Psychological Bulletin*, covering 138 studies on goal-progress monitoring, found that prompting people to monitor their goal progress reliably increases goal attainment, with effects roughly comparable to implementation intentions. The mechanism is simple: monitoring keeps the goal cognitively active in contexts where it would otherwise be displaced by competing demands. James Shah, Ronald Friedman, and Arie Kruglanski's 2002 *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* paper formalised what they called *goal shielding* — the cognitive process by which an active goal automatically inhibits attention to competing goals — and demonstrated that strongly committed goals shield more reliably than weakly committed ones, which is part of why commitment matters operationally rather than just affectively. And the broader self-determination theory literature treated in article 4.3.1 establishes that goals expressing identified or integrated regulation are sustained more reliably than goals expressing introjected or external pressure, with implications for how goals should be selected before they are operationalised through implementation intentions.

This is the body of evidence that makes Goal Completion Rate the highest-weighted metric in the entire Flow Score system at 0.35. The structural argument, in the three-proposition form the brief specifies for high-weighted metrics, runs as follows. First, goal completion tracks a substantial modifiable predictor of meaningful outcomes: the empirical record connecting goal pursuit to academic achievement, career trajectory, health behaviour, financial wellbeing, and subjective wellbeing is one of the most replicated bodies of work in psychology, and the rate at which people complete what they have committed to is a better predictor across these outcomes than most personality variables, including most measures of motivation as a state. Second, goal completion is genuinely tractable through deliberate user choices, in a way that other components of meaningful living are not: implementation intentions, monitoring, commitment, and environmental design all have robust evidence bases for raising completion rates, and the techniques can be applied immediately rather than requiring years of identity-level change. Third, goal completion propagates across the other measured dimensions of the Flow Score: completion produces identity feedback that supports the internalisation processes treated in article 4.3.1, generates the momentum effect that sustains subsequent goal pursuit, and contributes directly to the Weekly Alignment Score because completed aligned goals produce the lived experience of integration that the alignment metric tracks. The 0.35 weight reflects all three propositions converging on a single metric that is simultaneously well-supported, intervention-responsive, and integrative across the rest of the Purpose architecture.

The product implication is direct. Dolphin's Goal Completion Rate is not measuring whether the user is achieving impressive things. It is measuring whether the goals the user has set for themselves are reliably becoming the actions the user takes. The metric is sensitive to the gap rather than to the absolute level of ambition. A user completing 80% of modest goals is registering a healthier relationship to their own intentions than a user completing 30% of ambitious goals, and the metric weights accordingly. This is what the empirical literature on the intention-behaviour gap supports: the gap matters more than the level. A small consistent gap across years compounds into a life that follows the person's actual choices; a large persistent gap produces the experience of being a stranger in one's own life, watching intentions accumulate while the actions diverge.

Three honest qualifications. First, the implementation intentions effect, while one of the more replicated in social psychology, has documented boundary conditions. The technique works most reliably for goals where the situational cue is genuinely available and recognisable; for goals embedded in highly variable contexts or requiring complex multi-step planning, the effect attenuates. The 2014 review by Wieber and colleagues addresses this directly. Second, goal-setting research is not without serious empirical critique, treated in detail in article 4.5.3. The claim that healthy goal-setting reliably improves wellbeing assumes that the goals being set are themselves well-chosen — a question the goal-setting literature largely brackets and that the values literature in articles 4.2 and 4.6 takes up directly. Third, the metric weight reflects empirical and structural arguments rather than a claim that goal completion is the most important thing in human life. Reflection, alignment, and the slower work of internalisation matter, and the other three Purpose metrics weight them appropriately. Goal completion is weighted highest because it is the most tractable, the most measurable, and the most propagating of the four — not because it is the deepest. The deepest layer is what the goals are pointed at, and that layer belongs to the upstream pillars of the Purpose architecture.

Goals are not the architecture of a good life. They are the architecture of a life in which the things one has chosen actually get chosen, again, when the moment to choose them arrives.
