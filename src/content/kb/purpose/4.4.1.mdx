The behavioural economics literature has produced a finding that, on its face, seems to contradict everything the popular self-help tradition assumes about willpower. When people are offered the chance to lock themselves out of certain choices in advance — to put money into accounts they cannot withdraw from, to enrol in smoking cessation programmes that fine them if they relapse, to deposit funds that will be donated to causes they oppose if they fail to meet their goals — many of them voluntarily take the deal. Even more strikingly, when they take the deal, they substantially outperform the people who simply intended to do the same things without the commitment device. Xavier Giné, Dean Karlan, and Jonathan Zinman's 2010 study in the *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics* gave smokers in the Philippines the option to deposit savings that would be forfeited if they tested positive for nicotine six months later. Smokers who took the contract were more than thirty percent more likely to have quit at the six-month follow-up than smokers in the control group, and the effect persisted at twelve months. The participants knew, when they signed, that they were giving up the option to change their minds. That was the point.

The framework Gharad Bryan, Karlan, and Scott Nelson formalised in their 2010 *Annual Review of Economics* review distinguishes hard commitments, which involve actual costs for non-compliance, from soft commitments, which involve only psychological costs. Both work, in the relevant studies, though hard commitments are generally more powerful and soft commitments more frequently used. The mechanism is the same in both cases: the commitment closes off paths the future self would otherwise take. By the time the temptation arrives, the choice has already been made. Motivation in the moment of resistance is no longer required because the structure has been arranged in advance to make resistance unnecessary.

This is the architecture of commitment, and it stands behind the broader empirical case for commitment over motivation as the appropriate organising principle for sustained behaviour. The classical assumption — that commitment is a feeling one summons in moments of difficulty — has not held up well to scrutiny. Edwin Locke and Gary Latham's foundational goal-setting research, refined across more than fifty years and most recently treated in their continued work into the 2010s and 2020s, distinguishes goal commitment as a behavioural variable rather than an emotional one. People who are committed to a goal, in the Locke-Latham operationalisation, are not the people who feel strongly about it. They are the people whose behaviour reliably follows from it: they show up, they decline competing options, they restart after disruption, they treat the goal as a settled question rather than as a recurring decision. The commitment is what they do, not what they feel.

Peter Gollwitzer's implementation intentions research, which article 4.5.1 will treat in depth, can be read as the low-cost form of this same architecture. An implementation intention — a specific *if-then* plan linking a situational cue to a particular response — operates by closing the decision in advance. Instead of negotiating, every Tuesday at six p.m., whether to go to the gym, the person who has formed the implementation intention "if it is Tuesday at six p.m., then I will leave my apartment" has converted a recurring decision into an automatic response. Gollwitzer and Sheeran's 2006 meta-analysis covered ninety-four independent studies and found medium-to-large effect sizes for implementation intentions across goal pursuit domains. The mechanism is exactly what it appears to be. The person has committed in advance, in concrete operational terms, and the moment-to-moment motivation that would otherwise be required is no longer relevant.

Identity-based commitment, treated in articles 4.1.2 and 4.3.1, runs the same architecture through a different mechanism. When an activity has been integrated into one's sense of self — when *I am someone who does this* — the moment-to-moment commitment to the activity does not need to be summoned because it is already part of who the person takes themselves to be. The person who identifies as a runner does not negotiate every morning about whether to run; the negotiation has been settled at the identity level. The person who identifies as a parent who reads to their children at night does not have to renew commitment to the practice each evening; the practice expresses an identity that does not require nightly endorsement.

The implication is that the question *am I committed enough?* belongs to the same family as the question *am I motivated enough?* — both are framed at a level the brain handles poorly and both invite delay. The relevant questions are operational: what have I arranged so that this happens regardless of how I feel about it on any given day, and what would I need to arrange differently for the system to hold up under predictable disruption? The Goal Completion Rate metric in Dolphin operationalises this through outcome-tracking: it measures whether goals were completed, not whether the user felt committed to them. The metric is honest about what matters. Commitment, as a felt state, is unstable across days and weeks. Commitment, as the structure of the user's actual choices, is what produces results.

This relates directly to article 4.3.3's argument that motivation follows action rather than precedes it. The same logic applies to commitment. The commitment that endures is rarely the commitment that begins as a strong feeling and is then translated into action. It is more often the commitment that begins as a structural choice — a contract, a calendar entry, an environmental design, an identity claim — and produces the felt commitment as the activity proceeds. Commitment, in the architecture the empirical literature supports, is not the upstream cause that produces sustained behaviour. It is the structural arrangement that makes sustained behaviour possible while the felt commitment is being produced by the doing.

Three honest qualifications belong here. First, the commitment-over-motivation framing can drift into rigidity. Sustained commitment to projects whose underlying value has eroded is not a virtue; it is the sunk-cost fallacy in slow motion. Barry Staw's 1981 research on the escalation of commitment, refined across decades, showed that people consistently over-commit to failing courses of action when they cannot acknowledge that the original commitment was a mistake. The commitment architecture this article describes is appropriate for goals that remain aligned with values; for goals that have stopped serving the person, what is needed is not more commitment but the harder work of commitment revision, which articles 4.6.1 and 4.7.2 treat directly.

Second, commitment devices have boundary conditions the popular literature tends to elide. They work best for goals that are concrete and externally verifiable; they work less reliably for goals that are diffuse, qualitative, or whose outcomes are hard to measure. They also require some baseline of commitment to use in the first place — a person whose primary issue is ambivalence about whether to pursue the goal at all will not be helped by a commitment device until the upstream question is resolved. The literature is honest about this. The popular treatments often are not.

Third, the commitment framing can overshoot into a kind of stoic dismissal of inner experience that does not serve the person. Felt commitment is not nothing. When sustained activity continues to produce the experience of working against the self rather than from it, that is real signal — that the activity has not been internalised, that the values are misaligned, that the architecture is wrong somewhere upstream. Commitment that has to be continually defended against inner resistance is usually evidence that something is not yet integrated. The architecture works when the upstream layers are in place. When they are not, more commitment is the wrong intervention; what is needed is the slower work of articles 4.1, 4.2, and 4.3.

Commitment is not the strength of the feeling. It is the structure of what gets done when the feeling is gone.
