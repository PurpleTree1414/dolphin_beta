What does it mean to make a value-based decision? The phrase is one of the more abused in popular self-development literature, where it tends to be deployed as if its meaning were transparent. The empirical record is less generous. Values, as treated in article 4.2.1, are plural and structurally arranged in tension with each other; any non-trivial decision in adult life involves trade-offs between values rather than the expression of a single one. The decision to leave a stable career to pursue a more meaningful but less remunerative one is not a value-based decision in the sense of expressing a value; it is a value-based decision in the sense of resolving a conflict between values — security versus self-direction, achievement versus self-transcendence — that the situation has forced into the open. The popular framing of value-based decision-making as *consult your values, then choose* assumes a unified value-self that does not exist in the empirical literature on values.

What the research does support is a more procedural account. A value-based decision, in the operational sense Kennon Sheldon's self-concordance work has refined across decades and most recently treated in his 2014 *Personality and Social Psychology Review* paper *Becoming Oneself*, is a decision the person can defend on grounds they themselves recognise as authentically theirs, and that they would defend the same way under different framings. The diagnostic is not whether the person feels confident in the moment of choice — feelings of confidence track poorly to decision quality, as the maximisation literature treated below demonstrates — but whether the choice survives reasonable temporal and counterfactual perturbation. The person who can describe the same choice in roughly the same terms a year later, and who would have made the same choice if the option had been offered with different surface features, has made what the literature would recognise as a value-based decision. The person whose justifications shift, whose framing depends on how the question is asked, or who needs continuous reaffirmation of the choice has not.

This is harder than the popular framing implies, and it is harder for a specific reason that Barry Schwartz, Andrew Ward, and colleagues identified in their 2002 *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* paper introducing the Maximization Scale. People differ systematically in how they approach decisions. Maximisers seek the best possible option across all available alternatives; satisficers seek the first option that meets their standard. The distinction has been validated across many subsequent studies, including a 2009 *Personality and Individual Differences* paper by Ilan Dar-Nimrod, Catherine Rawn, Darrin Lehman, and Schwartz extending the framework. Maximisers, in this body of research, often achieve objectively better outcomes — they negotiate higher salaries, choose more carefully-vetted partners, accumulate more information before committing. They also report systematically lower satisfaction with the outcomes they achieve, more regret, more depressive symptoms, and more rumination on missed alternatives. The phenomenon Schwartz called the maximisation paradox is that the strategy of pursuing the best objectively delivers worse subjectively, because the cost of the search and the haunting of unchosen alternatives consume what the better outcome would otherwise have provided.

The implication for value-based decisions is direct. The maximiser approach assumes that the right decision is the one that optimises across all available criteria, and that more information always reduces the risk of error. The satisficer approach assumes that the right decision is the one that meets the criteria the person has already identified as mattering, and that further information past that threshold buys nothing the person can use. The empirical record favours the satisficer approach for personal decisions of moderate stakes, primarily because the psychological cost of the search and the post-decision rumination outweigh the small marginal gains the additional search produces. For high-stakes infrequent decisions — choice of a profession, a partner, a city — the calculus shifts; for the routine moderate-stakes decisions that constitute most of adult life, satisficing produces both better outcomes and higher satisfaction.

The role of intuition in value-based decisions is one of the more contested territories in the broader decision literature. Gary Klein's research on naturalistic decision-making, summarised in his 2008 *Human Factors* review, has shown that experts in domains they have substantial experience with — firefighters, military commanders, surgeons, chess players — make most of their decisions through rapid pattern-recognition rather than through deliberate analysis, and the decisions they make this way are reliably good. The Klein framework, called recognition-primed decision-making, treats expert intuition as the compressed product of thousands of structured observations rather than as a mystical access to truth. The intuition is reliable in the domain where the expertise was built; it is unreliable outside that domain, including for decisions that look superficially similar but involve different underlying structure. The popular self-development framing of *trust your gut* drops this qualifier and produces the unreliable case, where intuitive judgment is applied to novel decisions for which the person has no relevant pattern-library.

For value-based decisions specifically, this means intuition is reliable when the person has substantial experience with structurally-similar decisions and unreliable when they do not. A decision about how to handle a familiar conflict in a long-running relationship may be amenable to intuitive judgment; a decision about whether to accept an unprecedented job offer is not, because the person has no compressed pattern-library to draw on. The Sheldon self-concordance procedure, deliberation that examines what the person would defend the same way under different framings, becomes more important precisely as the decision becomes more novel.

The Goal Completion Rate metric in Dolphin treats the downstream side of this question. Goals that get completed reliably are usually goals that have survived the value-based-decision process at the setting stage; goals that systematically fail to be completed are often signalling that the decision to set them was not value-aligned in the first place. The Reflection Frequency metric treats the upstream review side: regular structured examination of recent decisions, asking whether the choices made would still be made the same way in retrospect, surfaces value-misalignment patterns that real-time decision-making cannot reach. The two metrics work as a coupled diagnostic for whether the user is making decisions that fit their values or accumulating a backlog of choices that do not.

Three honest qualifications. First, the maximiser-satisficer distinction has had its own measurement disputes. The original Maximization Scale has been factor-analysed into three subcomponents — high standards, alternative search, and decision difficulty — that may be empirically distinct, and not all of them correlate with the negative outcomes the original framework predicted. The picture is more nuanced than the popular treatments imply. Second, the value-based decision framing can drift into a kind of solipsistic decisional architecture in which only the person's own values matter and the impact of decisions on others is treated as ancillary. The empirical literature on relational decision-making has generally found that durable life satisfaction depends on accommodating the values of close others alongside one's own, and the *make decisions from your values* framing, taken to its individualistic extreme, produces relationships that do not survive the second decade. Third, the entire discussion assumes the person has reasonably accurate knowledge of their own values, which articles 4.1.1 through 4.2.2 have already complicated. The procedural commitment to value-based decision-making is necessary but not sufficient; the upstream values knowledge is what gives the procedure something to be based on.

A value-based decision is not the one that feels right in the moment. It is the one a person can still defend, in their own terms, when the moment is gone.
