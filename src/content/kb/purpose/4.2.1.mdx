When Shalom Schwartz set out, in the early 1990s, to test whether human values had a universal structure, he expected to find substantial cultural variation. What he found instead was one of the more surprising regularities in social psychology. Across more than eighty countries, with samples drawn from teachers, students, and members of the general public, the same set of basic values kept appearing in the same structural relationships to one another. The content varied — what people emphasised, what they ranked higher, what they were willing to sacrifice — but the architecture did not. Self-direction sat opposite conformity. Achievement sat opposite benevolence. Stimulation, hedonism, security, tradition, power, universalism — each took its place on a circular continuum in which adjacent values were compatible and opposing values pulled against each other. The structure held in cultures that had never met, in religious traditions that had never spoken, in economic systems built on contradictory assumptions about human nature. The architecture appeared to be human, not local.

The 2012 paper in which Schwartz and his collaborators refined this theory into nineteen narrowly-defined values, organised under four higher-order categories — openness to change, self-enhancement, conservation, and self-transcendence — has since been validated across forty-nine cultural groups in a 2022 study by Schwartz and Jan Cieciuch using the revised Portrait Values Questionnaire. The structure replicates. Lilach Sagiv and Schwartz's 2022 *Annual Review of Psychology* synthesis treats this as established: human values are not a culturally arbitrary list but a structured motivational space in which most of us locate ourselves somewhere, with predictable trade-offs between regions of the space.

This matters for Purpose because it lets us talk about values without falling into either of the two traps that dominate the popular literature. The first trap is treating values as a personal-discovery exercise — as if each person's values were idiosyncratic and unknowable until self-revealed. The second is treating values as universally prescribed — as if there were a correct ranking that all flourishing humans should approach. Neither matches the empirical record. Values are structurally universal but personally weighted; the architecture is shared, the emphasis is yours.

The four higher-order categories deserve brief unpacking, because the structural relationships between them are where most of the practical implications live. Openness to change values — self-direction, stimulation — sit opposite conservation values — security, conformity, tradition. A person whose weight is heavy in one region tends to feel internal friction with the values of the opposing region; the trade-off is real, not a failure of integration. Self-enhancement values — achievement, power — sit opposite self-transcendence values — universalism, benevolence. Again, the opposition is structural rather than moral; the architecture predicts that someone heavily weighted toward personal achievement will experience genuine cost when asked to subordinate it to others' wellbeing, and vice versa. Knowing where one sits in this structure does not solve the trade-offs but makes them legible. The friction stops feeling like a personal contradiction and starts feeling like the predictable cost of being weighted as one is.

What, then, is a value? In the Schwartz tradition, a value is a trans-situational guiding principle that orders preferences across decisions. This is the feature that distinguishes values from preferences, goals, and traits, three constructs the popular literature regularly conflates. A preference is what one wants in this instance. A goal is a specific outcome one is committed to pursuing. A trait is a stable disposition to behave in particular ways. A value runs across all three: it shapes which preferences feel acceptable, which goals feel worth setting, and which trait expressions feel like *me* and which like *not me*. Anat Bardi and Schwartz's earlier work, foundational for this distinction, found that the values-behaviour relationship is reliable but moderate — values predict consistent patterns of behaviour over time better than they predict any single decision in any single moment. This is what one would expect from a guiding principle rather than a behavioural rule.

Two larger conceptual frames sit alongside the Schwartz architecture, and both belong here. The first is the hedonic-eudaimonic distinction, made canonical in Richard Ryan and Edward Deci's 2017 synthesis of self-determination theory and treated extensively across the positive psychology literature. Hedonic wellbeing is the experience of pleasure and the absence of pain. Eudaimonic wellbeing is the experience of meaning, growth, autonomy, and connection. The two are positively correlated in most populations but not interchangeable. A life can be high in pleasure and low in meaning, or vice versa, and the values a person holds shape which of the two their daily choices privilege. Schwartz's self-transcendence values (universalism, benevolence) tend to load toward eudaimonic outcomes; his self-enhancement values (achievement, power) sometimes do, sometimes do not. The second frame is that values are not fully fixed. A 2022 systematic literature review by Claudia Russo and colleagues, examining the experimental literature on value change, found that targeted interventions reliably produce small-to-moderate shifts in stated value priorities — durable enough to be meaningful, modest enough to refute the claim that values are simply chosen.

For the Purpose pillar specifically, the implication of this body of work is that values are the upstream structure that gives the Goal Completion, Reflection Frequency, and Weekly Alignment metrics something to be measured against. A person whose stated value system is heavily weighted toward self-direction will register misalignment differently than someone weighted toward conformity, even if both are completing identical fractions of their goals. The Weekly Alignment Score does not assume any particular value architecture. It asks whether the week's lived experience felt aligned with what matters to the person — whatever they take that to be.

Two qualifications deserve naming. First, the Schwartz framework is the strongest empirical account of values *structure*, not values *content*. It can tell us reliably how values relate to each other, how they cluster, how they trade off. It cannot tell anyone what their values should be. The popular self-help literature regularly extracts a normative reading from a descriptive theory and treats certain values (typically self-transcendence and openness to change) as more *authentic* or more *mature* than others. The empirical literature does not support this. Different value profiles support different forms of flourishing in different contexts; cross-cultural psychology has been clear about this since Hofstede.

Second, values change more than people generally admit. The folk theory holds that values are settled in young adulthood and remain stable across the life course. The longitudinal evidence is more nuanced: values shift in measurable ways across major life transitions, in response to chronic exposure to new contexts, and following deliberate reflection. Russo and colleagues' 2022 review documents this in the experimental literature. Article 4.11.2 in the Lifelong Purpose section will return to this — purpose evolves because values evolve, and treating either as fixed produces precisely the kind of misalignment Purpose is designed to detect.

Values are not what a person believes about themselves. They are the structural map of what they will sustain effort toward when nothing in particular is forcing them to.
