Writing about a difficult experience for twenty minutes a day, for four days, produces measurable health benefits in some people and modest harm in others. The intervention is identical. The outcome diverges. This is the empirical situation any honest treatment of reflective practice has to begin from, and it is the situation the popular literature on journaling, meditation, and self-examination has been slowest to acknowledge.

The intervention in question is the expressive writing paradigm James Pennebaker introduced in his 1986 *Journal of Abnormal Psychology* study with Sandra Beall, and developed across the four decades of research that followed. The protocol is simple. Participants are asked to write about an emotionally significant experience — usually a difficult one — for fifteen to twenty minutes a day across three or four consecutive days, without concern for grammar or coherence, with the writing typically not shared with anyone. The accumulated evidence base, summarised in Joanne Frattaroli's 2006 *Psychological Bulletin* meta-analysis of 146 randomised studies, supports a positive average effect size for the intervention across physical health, psychological functioning, and self-reported wellbeing outcomes. The effect size is r = 0.075. The number is modest by any standard. Subsequent meta-analyses have arrived at varying conclusions — Smyth's 1998 synthesis found medium effects; Meads and Nouwen's 2005 analysis found no overall effects; Mogk's 2006 review reached similar null conclusions; Reinhold and colleagues' 2018 work identified specific subgroups for whom the intervention works better than others. The honest reading of the literature is that expressive writing produces real but small effects on average, that the effects vary substantially by population and context, and that the variation is not random — there are identifiable factors that distinguish the people who benefit from the people who do not.

The single most important of those factors is treated in a 1999 *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* paper by Paul Trapnell and Jennifer Campbell, whose methodological contribution to the field has been substantially larger than the citation count alone would suggest. Trapnell and Campbell argued that what had been treated in the self-consciousness literature as a single construct — *private self-attention*, the tendency to attend to one's own thoughts, feelings, and inner states — was in fact two motivationally distinct dispositions that had been incorrectly merged. The distinction they made remains the most useful one available. Reflection is curiosity-driven self-attention; the person attends to their own experience because they are genuinely interested in understanding it, and the attention is associated with openness, intellectual engagement, and a desire for self-knowledge. Rumination is threat-driven self-attention; the person attends to their own experience because they cannot stop attending to it, and the attention is associated with anxiety, perfectionism, and a tendency toward repetitive negative thought. The two dispositions look similar from outside the head — both involve a person spending substantial time thinking about themselves — but the cognitive process and the psychological effects are not the same. Trapnell and Campbell's data showed that reflection correlated with adaptive personality traits and self-knowledge gains, while rumination correlated with depression, anxiety, and impaired functioning.

The implication for reflective practice is consequential. Ryoko Takano and Yoshihiko Tanno's 2009 *Behaviour Research and Therapy* paper extended the original work and found that self-rumination *cancels* the adaptive effect of self-reflection in the same individual. People high in both traits do not get the benefits of reflection that people high in reflection alone receive; the ruminative dimension undoes what the reflective dimension would otherwise produce. This explains, in part, why expressive writing produces such variable results in the meta-analytic literature. The same writing protocol, applied to a population of reflectors, produces small positive effects. Applied to a population that includes substantial proportion of ruminators, the average masks two opposite outcomes — some people benefit, some people are mildly harmed, and the mean settles near zero. The intervention is not what produces the outcome. The intervention plus the disposition produces the outcome, and any honest treatment of reflective practice has to acknowledge that the disposition matters as much as the practice does.

This is the foundation on which the structural argument for Reflection Frequency at 0.25 metric weight rests. The argument runs as follows. First, reflective practice tracks a substantial modifiable predictor of meaningful outcomes — the empirical record across the Pennebaker tradition, the meaning-making research treated in article 4.9.1, the life review literature treated in article 4.10.3, and the broader self-distancing research established consistent though modest effects across psychological wellbeing, goal attainment, identity coherence, and recovery from disruption. Second, reflection is genuinely tractable through deliberate practice in ways the other Purpose constructs are not — a user can adopt a structured weekly reflection practice within hours of deciding to, and the practice produces effects within weeks rather than across years. Third, reflection propagates more thoroughly across the other measured Purpose dimensions than any other single behavioural variable available. Reflection surfaces the value misalignment that article 4.9.2 treats as the diagnostic for purpose drift; reflection enables the goal recalibration the Goal Completion Rate metric depends on for interpretive validity; reflection supports the identity coherence work articles 4.1.2 and 4.7.3 ground in implicit-theory and self-concept research; reflection is the natural mechanism through which the slower processes the entire Purpose architecture rests on become visible to the user. The 0.25 weight reflects this propagation pattern as much as it reflects the direct empirical effects. The reason the weight is lower than the 0.35 assigned to Goal Completion is that reflection's direct effect sizes are smaller and its failure modes are more substantial. The reason the weight is higher than Course Progress at 0.20 is that reflection is the foundation on which the other metrics' interpretive value depends — without periodic reflection, the data the other three metrics produce becomes uninterpretable to the user it is meant to serve.

The Mind pillar's article 2.8 treats the broader mindfulness and meta-cognition literature, including the substantial research on mindfulness-based stress reduction, the empirical case for mindfulness interventions in clinical populations, and the more cautious recent meta-analytic work that has refined the original popular claims. The Purpose contribution here is narrower. Reflection in the Purpose sense is not primarily a present-moment attentional practice in the way mindfulness is; it is a retrospective examination of how recent days and weeks have actually unfolded, against the values and goals the person endorses. The two practices are complementary rather than identical, and the literatures support roughly similar but partly independent constructs. A user practising mindfulness without retrospective reflection captures the present-moment benefits without surfacing the longer drift patterns the reflection literature addresses. A user practising retrospective reflection without present-moment attentional training captures the longer patterns without the moment-to-moment regulation mindfulness supports.

What this leaves us with operationally is more specific guidance than the popular reflection literature usually provides. The reflective practice that produces benefits is curiosity-driven, structured enough to surface patterns the unstructured mind would miss, brief enough to fit a sustainable weekly rhythm rather than producing the over-examination ruminators are vulnerable to, and oriented toward what the data of the week actually contained rather than toward repeated processing of fixed concerns. The reflective practice that produces harm is threat-driven, unstructured in ways that allow rumination to fill the time, sustained for hours rather than minutes, and oriented toward perceived failures and shortcomings that have already been processed many times before. Most popular treatments of journaling and reflection do not distinguish these clearly enough to help users tell which practice they are doing. The empirical literature distinguishes them sharply. Dolphin's reflection prompts are designed around the structural features the research supports, with explicit guidance away from the rumination patterns the literature has documented as actively harmful.

There is one further qualification that belongs here. The Frattaroli effect size is small, and small effects compound across long time horizons rather than producing dramatic short-term change. The popular framing of reflection as transformative practice, capable of producing major life shifts within weeks or months, runs ahead of what the evidence supports. What the evidence supports is more modest: reflective practice, sustained over years, accumulates the small data that allows the larger patterns to become visible. The transformation, when it happens, is usually the cumulative product of many small accumulated noticings rather than a single dramatic insight. This is the temporal pattern the Reflection Frequency metric is built to capture, and it is the pattern the user should expect rather than the dramatic version the popular literature has trained them to anticipate.

A week unexamined is not exactly a week unlived. It is a week whose data was not made available to the person whose life it was.
