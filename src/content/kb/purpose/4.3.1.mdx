In 2021, Joshua Howard, Julien Bureau, Frédéric Guay, Jane Chong, and Richard Ryan published a meta-analysis in *Perspectives on Psychological Science* covering 344 samples and over 223,000 participants. The question they asked was specific: across the different types of motivation that self-determination theory has spent fifty years differentiating, which ones actually predict the outcomes — academic achievement, persistence, wellbeing, goal pursuit — that motivation is supposed to predict? The answer was more interesting than the popular framing of motivation generally allows. Intrinsic motivation, the kind of pure interest in an activity for its own sake, was indeed associated with the best outcomes across most of the dependent variables. But the second-best motivation type was not, as one might assume, some form of extrinsic incentive that worked despite being external. It was identified regulation — a form of extrinsic motivation in which the person has come to genuinely value the activity, even though they did not start out finding it inherently interesting. Identified regulation tracks intrinsic motivation closely on most outcomes, and on some outcomes — including sustained academic performance — it actually outperforms it.

This finding matters because it dismantles one of the most common misreadings of self-determination theory in the popular literature: the framing in which intrinsic motivation is good, extrinsic motivation is bad, and the path to sustained behaviour is to find activities that are inherently interesting. SDT does not say that. What SDT says, in the version Edward Deci and Richard Ryan have refined across half a century and most recently consolidated in their 2017 book *Self-Determination Theory*, is that motivation lies on a continuum of internalisation. At one end is amotivation, the absence of any reason to act. Then external regulation, where the only reason to act is the threat of punishment or the promise of reward. Then introjected regulation, where the person has internalised an external pressure but not endorsed it — they act out of guilt, anxiety, or contingent self-worth. Then identified regulation, where the person has come to genuinely value the activity even if they do not particularly enjoy it. Then integrated regulation, where the activity has become part of who the person takes themselves to be. And finally intrinsic motivation, where the activity is its own reward.

The practical implication is that the road to sustained behaviour is rarely a search for activities one finds inherently interesting. It is more often a process of internalisation — of taking activities that started out external or obligatory and integrating them into one's sense of self until the motivation to do them no longer requires external scaffolding. A person who flosses every night because they would feel guilty otherwise (introjected) is not in the same place as a person who flosses because they have come to genuinely value oral health (identified) or a person for whom flossing is part of the kind of person they are (integrated). All three are doing the behaviour. The first one is doing it expensively, with a continual cost to internal resources. The second and third are doing it sustainably, and the third is doing it almost without negotiation.

Anja Van den Broeck, Howard, and colleagues' 2021 meta-analysis on workplace motivation, covering 124 samples, found a related pattern. Intrinsic motivation was the most important motivation type for employee wellbeing and engagement, but identified regulation was actually more powerful in predicting performance and citizenship behaviour. The takeaway is structurally important. Sustained, high-quality action does not depend on finding activities one happens to love. It depends on whether the activity has been internalised — on whether the person has come to value it as theirs, regardless of how they originally came to it.

The mechanism SDT proposes runs through three psychological needs that the theory treats as universal: autonomy (the experience of acting from one's own volition), competence (the experience of being effective at what one is doing), and relatedness (the experience of being connected to others). When activities support all three, internalisation tends to happen. When activities undermine any of them — most reliably autonomy — internalisation stalls and the person remains stuck at lower points on the continuum. This is why surveillance-heavy workplaces, controlling parental styles, and incentive structures that feel coercive tend to produce introjected or external motivation regardless of how attractive the underlying activity might otherwise be. The need-thwarting environment prevents the activity from being made one's own.

The needs framework also explains why some activities that should produce intrinsic motivation, by every theoretical prediction, in fact do not. A person can be doing meaningful work, with reasonable competence, in a job that supports their economic needs — and still find themselves stuck in introjected regulation, working from anxiety about not measuring up rather than from genuine endorsement. The diagnostic question SDT directs is which of the three needs is being thwarted. Autonomy thwarted: the work is being done for reasons that feel imposed. Competence thwarted: the work is producing the experience of repeated failure rather than progressive mastery. Relatedness thwarted: the work is being done in isolation or in conflict with people whose regard matters. Internalisation cannot proceed where the needs are not being met, and no amount of trying to *want* the activity more will compensate.

The Body pillar treats one specific instance of this in its work on intrinsic motivation for physical activity (Body 1.12.1). The argument there is that exercise sustained from intrinsic and identified motivations tends to be maintained across decades, while exercise sustained from external pressure or contingent self-worth tends to collapse during life transitions. The Purpose contribution is broader and upstream. Body covers the physical-activity domain specifically; Purpose treats the question of how internalisation works across the whole architecture of one's life. The same internalisation process that makes exercise sustainable makes work sustainable, makes relationships sustainable, makes any chosen direction sustainable. The pillar boundary is honest: Body owns the domain, Purpose owns the cross-domain logic.

Three honest qualifications belong here. First, while SDT is the most empirically validated motivation framework in psychology, it is not unchallenged. Goal-setting theory, achievement-goal theory, and expectancy-value theory all make complementary claims, and the relationship between SDT and these other traditions remains live in the research. The integrated picture probably draws on all of them. Second, the famous undermining effect — the finding that external rewards can reduce intrinsic motivation for already-interesting activities — has held up through multiple meta-analyses, but the boundary conditions are more nuanced than the popular treatments suggest. Rewards undermine intrinsic motivation reliably when the activity is already intrinsically interesting and the reward is presented as controlling; the effect is weaker or absent when the activity was not intrinsically motivating to begin with, or when the reward is informational rather than controlling. The lesson is more subtle than "rewards always backfire."

Third, the distinction between integrated regulation and intrinsic motivation is empirically harder to maintain than the theoretical model implies. A 2020 meta-analysis by Vasconcellos and colleagues found the two often correlate so highly in samples of children and adolescents that they cannot be cleanly distinguished, which is why most academic motivation scales now collapse them. The continuum is real and useful as a working model; the boundaries between adjacent points on it are softer than the textbook diagrams suggest.

Motivation is not what you find. It is what you build, by taking on activities seriously enough that they become yours.
