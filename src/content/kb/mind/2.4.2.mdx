You are not equally intelligent at every hour of the day. Most people would, if pressed, acknowledge this. Very few people structure their work around it. The cost of that gap, accumulated across years, is substantial: complex analytical tasks attempted at cognitive troughs, creative work scheduled into windows that suppress it, decisions made at the times of day at which decision quality is reliably lowest. The biological system that produces this variation has been one of the more intensively studied areas of physiology over the past four decades, and it shows up as a coordination point across three of Dolphin's four pillars.

The system is the *circadian rhythm* — a roughly twenty-four-hour cycle of physiological and behavioural variation, driven by an endogenous clock located primarily in the *suprachiasmatic nucleus* of the hypothalamus. The SCN was identified as the master pacemaker in the early 1970s and has since been characterised in considerable molecular detail. The cycle it produces is genuinely endogenous: even in the absence of all external time cues, in laboratory studies of participants kept in constant lighting, the cycle continues with a period close to twenty-four hours, although it tends to drift slightly longer without correction. The primary external cue that keeps the system synchronised to the actual day is light, particularly the morning light reaching specialised retinal cells (the intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells, identified in the early 2000s) that project directly to the SCN. Other inputs — meal timing, physical activity, social cues — contribute, but light is by some distance the strongest. This is the foundational physiology, and it is treated in greater detail in the Body pillar's article on sleep (Body 1.7.1) and in the Lifestyle articles on circadian alignment and light exposure (Lifestyle 3.4).

What concerns this article is the cognitive side. The circadian system regulates not only sleep and wake but also a wide range of other variables — body temperature, cortisol release, melatonin secretion, digestive function — and cognition is one of them. A widely cited 2007 review by Christina Schmidt and colleagues in *Cognitive Neuropsychology* synthesised several decades of research and reached a clear conclusion: cognitive performance varies systematically across the day, but different cognitive functions vary in different ways, and the variation is large enough to matter.

The classical pattern, particularly for tasks that depend on sustained attention and working memory, is a peak in the late morning, a noticeable trough in the early afternoon (the so-called *post-lunch dip*, which occurs even in fasted individuals and is therefore not primarily about food), a partial recovery in the late afternoon, and a decline through the evening. This pattern is robust across populations and has been demonstrated in tasks as varied as arithmetic, logical reasoning, and reaction time. Not all cognitive functions follow it, however. Research by Mareike Wieth and Rose Zacks, published in 2011, showed that *insight problems* — problems that require breaking out of a habitual frame to find a non-obvious solution — were solved more successfully at the time of day that did not match the participant's chronotype, that is, in the morning for self-described evening types and in the evening for morning types. The most analytically sharp time of day, in other words, is not the most creatively flexible time of day. The reduced top-down attentional control that comes with circadian off-peaks appears to allow more associative, lateral thinking.

Individual variation in the timing of these peaks and troughs is substantial. Chronobiologist Till Roenneberg's research over the past two decades has documented that the human population is distributed across a wide spectrum of chronotypes — from extreme morning types whose cognitive peaks fall in the early morning to extreme evening types whose peaks fall in the late evening, with most of the population somewhere in between. The distribution is partly genetic, partly developmental, and shifts predictably across the lifespan: adolescents reliably trend toward later chronotypes, older adults toward earlier. Two well-validated instruments have been developed to assess where an individual sits on this distribution: the *Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire* (MEQ) developed by Jim Horne and Olov Östberg in 1976, and Roenneberg's more recent *Munich Chronotype Questionnaire* (MCTQ), which infers chronotype from sleep timing on free days. Both produce reasonably stable measurements, and both have been used to demonstrate that the alignment of an individual's natural chronotype with the schedule imposed on them is itself a meaningful health variable. The mismatch — what Roenneberg termed *social jet lag* — has been associated in epidemiological studies with elevated rates of metabolic, cardiovascular, and mental health problems.

The applied implications are straightforward but rarely acted on. Demanding analytical work is best scheduled into one's cognitive peak. Routine, low-cognitive-load work tolerates the trough hours more gracefully and can usefully be parked there. Creative or exploratory work, where breaking habitual frames is part of the goal, may benefit from being placed at off-peak times rather than at the peak — a counterintuitive recommendation that is well-supported but goes against most popular productivity advice. Decisions that matter should not be made at the bottom of the trough if they can be deferred; the literature on time-of-day effects on decision-making, including studies of medical, legal, and financial decisions, has consistently found that decision quality declines through the working day in ways that practitioners are usually unaware of. Shai Danziger and colleagues' 2011 study of judicial parole decisions, although subsequently debated for confounders, illustrated the magnitude possible: favourable rulings declined sharply across each session before recovering after a break.

The cross-pillar coordination point is worth stating explicitly because it is one of the more concrete examples of how Dolphin's pillars are actually meant to interlock. The same circadian system shows up in three different articles — Body's treatment of sleep, Mind's treatment of cognitive timing, and Lifestyle's treatment of light exposure and daily rhythm — because the same biological mechanism is producing effects in three different domains. Improvements made in one pillar feed forward into the others. Better-timed light exposure in the morning (Lifestyle) sharpens the cognitive peak that afternoon (Mind) and improves sleep onset that evening (Body). Mistimed light at night (Lifestyle) blunts the next day's cognitive peak (Mind) and degrades the sleep that supports it (Body). Treating these three articles as separate topics misses the point. They are three views of one system, and the leverage in coordinating them is greater than the sum of the leverage in any one alone.

Daniel Pink's 2018 book *When* offers a useful applied synthesis for general readers, although the underlying research extends far deeper than any popular treatment. The point that Pink and the underlying literature converge on is the same: timing is not decoration on top of cognition. It is part of the structure of cognition. Working with the circadian system is one of the higher-leverage adjustments available, and it is a free one in the sense that it requires no additional resources — only attention to when work of different types is actually being done.

The system runs whether or not it is cooperated with. Cooperating with it is, in most knowledge-work contexts, one of the largest and most under-used moves available.
