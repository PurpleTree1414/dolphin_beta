The empirical literature on meditation is, by the standards of psychology research, both unusually extensive and unusually contested. The number of published studies has grown from a handful per year in the 1990s to thousands per year by the 2020s, and the range of claimed benefits has grown roughly in proportion. Some of those claims are well-supported by careful research. Some have failed to replicate when subjected to rigorous methodology. A 2017 paper by Nicholas Van Dam and a large interdisciplinary group of meditation researchers, published in *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, called explicitly for more methodological rigour in the field, noting that small sample sizes, inadequate control conditions, publication bias, and inconsistent terminology had inflated the apparent strength of evidence in some areas.

This article begins with that acknowledgment because the popular discussion of meditation tends not to. The honest reading of the literature is that meditation produces real, replicable, and sometimes substantial effects in some areas, while in others the evidence is weaker than the cultural enthusiasm would suggest. The product the user is engaging with — Dolphin's daily practice tracking, in which Meditation Minutes carries thirty per cent of the live Mind score — has to rest on what is actually supported, not on what is generally believed. This article therefore separates the two with some care.

A first definitional point is necessary. *Meditation* is not a single practice. It is a broad category covering a wide variety of techniques with different mechanisms and different outcomes. The taxonomy most widely used in the contemporary research literature divides meditation into three broad families. *Focused attention* practices train sustained attention on a chosen object — typically the breath, a sound, or a phrase — and the practice consists primarily of returning attention to the object whenever it has wandered. *Open monitoring* practices, sometimes called *mindfulness* in the technical sense, involve attending to whatever arises in awareness — thoughts, sensations, emotions — without selecting any particular object, and observing these contents non-judgementally as they pass. *Loving-kindness* and *compassion* practices, sometimes grouped as *constructive* meditation, involve the deliberate cultivation of specific mental states such as warmth, goodwill, or compassion toward self and others. These three families are not mutually exclusive in practice, and most actual practitioners cycle between them, but their cognitive demands and their measured effects differ enough that mixing them in research has been one of the contributors to inconsistent findings.

The most rigorously studied meditation programme, and the one with the strongest evidence base, is *Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction* (MBSR), developed by Jon Kabat-Zinn at the University of Massachusetts Medical School in 1979. MBSR is an eight-week structured programme combining elements of focused attention and open monitoring with body scanning and gentle movement. The programme was designed for patients with chronic pain and stress-related conditions, and it has been studied across a wide range of populations and outcomes since. A 2014 meta-analysis by Madhav Goyal and colleagues at Johns Hopkins, published in *JAMA Internal Medicine*, synthesised forty-seven trials covering more than three thousand participants and reached conclusions that are worth quoting closely: there was *moderate evidence* of improvement in anxiety, depression, and pain, with effect sizes broadly comparable to those produced by standard therapeutic interventions. There was *low evidence* of improvement in stress and quality of life. There was *insufficient evidence* of effects on attention, substance use, or weight. The conclusion was not that meditation is ineffective; it was that the effects are real but more specific than the popular discussion suggests, and concentrated in particular outcome domains.

The neuroscience picture is similarly more nuanced than the popular treatment. Studies of long-term meditators, including the substantial neuroimaging programme of Richard Davidson at the University of Wisconsin, have documented structural and functional differences in regions including the prefrontal cortex, the anterior cingulate cortex, the insula, and the amygdala. The original interpretation — that meditation produces these changes — has been moderated by subsequent research recognising that long-term meditators are a self-selected population, and that randomised trials produce smaller and more variable changes than cross-sectional comparisons. A 2022 meta-analysis by Kieran Fox and colleagues, drawing on neuroimaging studies of relative novices in randomised designs, found evidence of small structural changes in some regions after eight weeks of practice, with effect sizes substantially smaller than the cross-sectional studies had suggested. The honest summary is that meditation appears to produce measurable neural changes, that these changes are smaller than the popular discussion sometimes implies, and that the dose-response relationship — how much practice produces how much change — remains imperfectly characterised.

What, then, is reasonably well-supported? Three claims have stronger evidence than the others.

The first is that meditation, particularly focused attention practice, produces measurable improvements in *attentional control* — the same capacity treated in articles 2.1.1 and 2.3.1. The mechanism is straightforward: the practice consists of repeatedly noticing where attention is and bringing it back, which is also the operation that defines attentional control as a cognitive capacity. The work of Amishi Jha and colleagues, including her studies with military and high-load civilian populations, has shown that even relatively short periods of practice (twelve minutes a day for four weeks, in some studies) produce measurable improvements on standardised attention tasks. The effect is real, replicable, and one of the better-supported claims in the field.

The second is that meditation reduces *physiological reactivity to stress*. The mechanism connects to the autonomic regulation discussed in article 2.6.1: meditation practices that include slow breathing or body-based attention engage parasympathetic activity through the vagal pathways characterised in Thayer and Lane's neurovisceral integration model. The downstream effects — lower resting heart rate, higher heart rate variability, reduced cortisol response to acute stressors — have been documented across multiple studies and are reasonably consistent. The effect sizes are modest but reliable.

The third is that meditation improves *emotional regulation*, particularly the recovery side: the speed at which emotional reactivity subsides after a triggering event. The mechanism is partly the physiological one above, and partly the cognitive one introduced in article 2.2.2 — the act of labelling and observing emotional content rather than being absorbed in it. Affect labelling, treated in detail in 2.2.2, is structurally identical to one of the operations open-monitoring meditation practices train.

What is less well-supported, despite frequent claims, includes substantial cognitive enhancement (effects on memory, intelligence, or general cognition tend to be smaller and less reliable than effects on attention specifically), broad mood elevation in non-clinical populations (effects are stronger in clinical samples), and the various specific physical health claims that have proliferated in the popular literature. The evidence for meditation as a treatment for diagnosable anxiety and depression is reasonable, particularly within structured programmes; the evidence for meditation as a general well-being optimisation tool in healthy populations is more modest.

There are also documented adverse effects, which the popular treatment tends to under-discuss. A 2017 study by Jared Lindahl and colleagues, drawing on detailed interviews with experienced meditators, documented a range of difficult experiences including increased anxiety, dissociation, and re-emergence of past trauma, particularly during intensive retreat practice. The effects are not common, but they are not negligible, and people with substantial trauma histories or active mental health conditions are appropriately advised to begin meditation in clinical or at least guided contexts rather than independently.

What does basic practice actually look like? The structural elements that nearly all evidence-supported practices share are simple. A consistent time, ideally daily. A duration that is realistic enough to maintain — twelve to twenty minutes is well-supported by the research, and longer durations show diminishing returns relative to consistency. A posture that is alert but comfortable. An object of attention, most commonly the breath, that the practitioner returns to whenever the mind has wandered. The wandering itself is not the problem the practice is solving; it is the operation the practice is training. Each return is the rep.

A practical observation worth stating directly: the most common failure mode in meditation practice is not technical but motivational. People begin with high expectations, encounter the gap between the popular description and the actual experience (which is largely the experience of attention wandering and being returned, repeatedly, with no obvious dramatic change for substantial periods), and lose the practice within weeks. The realistic version is that meditation is closer to physical training than to a single intervention: small, consistent doses produce gradual change measurable across weeks and months, not days. This is why daily tracking matters. The cumulative effect of a sustained practice is what the literature supports; sporadic intensive practice followed by long gaps performs worse than less ambitious daily practice.

This is the structural reason Dolphin treats Meditation Minutes as a high-weighted metric. The metric is not primarily a measure of how much someone has meditated. It is a measure of consistency, and consistency is the variable on which the measurable benefits depend. A day of meditation is not transformative; a year of consistent practice produces effects that are well-documented. The metric tracks the underlying skill being built.

The most defensible thing to say about meditation, on the current evidence, is that it is a practice with well-established benefits in attentional control, stress reactivity, and emotional regulation; with smaller and more variable benefits in adjacent domains; with real but uncommon adverse effects in vulnerable populations; and with effect sizes that are modest but reliable when sustained over months. It is neither the panacea the popular literature sometimes presents nor the placebo the contrarian literature sometimes argues. It is one of the better-validated cognitive practices available, and the case for inclusion in a daily routine rests on what the evidence actually supports.
