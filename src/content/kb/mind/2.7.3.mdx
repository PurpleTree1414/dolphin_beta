The previous two articles in this section are written for someone who is monitoring their state and looking for signs. This one is written for someone who is past that point. The signs are not theoretical. The exhaustion is established. The cynicism is recognisable. The sense that nothing one does is making a real difference has settled in. The question is no longer whether burnout has happened but what to do given that it has.

The honest version of the literature on this question is more humbling than the popular discussion typically allows. Burnout that has consolidated through all three of Maslach's dimensions — emotional exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced efficacy — does not resolve on the timescale of a long weekend, a vacation, or a productivity-system redesign. The recovery timescale, in the studies that have measured it, is months. Severe cases run longer. This is not a function of personal weakness; it is a function of the regulatory systems that produced the syndrome having moved into a state from which the return to baseline is itself a slow biological and psychological process. Treating burnout with the kind of intervention one would apply to ordinary fatigue tends to fail, and the failure tends to be interpreted as evidence that one cannot be helped, when the actual signal is that the intervention was not calibrated to the condition.

This article therefore organises around what the literature has actually identified as effective, sequenced roughly in the order in which it tends to need to occur. The sequence is not strictly fixed — individual circumstances vary — but the principle is that earlier steps create the conditions under which later steps become possible. Skipping ahead tends not to work.

**The first move is removing or substantially reducing the load.** This is the step that the affected person is least likely to take, because the conditions that produced burnout typically include circumstances under which load reduction feels impossible. The literature, and the clinical experience of practitioners who treat burnout, is consistent on this point: continued operation at the load that produced the syndrome will not allow the syndrome to resolve, regardless of what else is done. The body and brain do not heal under the conditions that produced the injury. Whatever combination of leave, reduced hours, delegated responsibility, deferred commitments, or structural change is required to lower the load below the recovery threshold is the necessary first move, and there is little that subsequent moves can do if it is not made.

This step is uncomfortable to state plainly because it interacts with circumstances over which people often have less control than productivity literature implies — caring responsibilities, financial constraints, employment vulnerabilities. The honest acknowledgment is that for some people, the available reduction is partial rather than complete, and that partial reduction produces partial recovery. Even partial load reduction, however, performs better than no reduction. The pattern that performs worst is the one in which the affected person continues at full load while attempting to recover through evening and weekend interventions; the math of that approach does not work, and pursuing it tends to consolidate burnout further.

**The second move is restoring physiological baseline.** Burnout is not only psychological; it is a state in which the regulatory systems described in article 2.5.1 — the HPA axis, the autonomic nervous system, the immune and metabolic systems they coordinate with — have been operating outside their typical parameters for an extended period. Sleep is the master recovery process across most of these systems and is typically the first thing to address, because adequate sleep is a precondition for the cognitive and emotional work that comes later. The relevant techniques are the ones treated in detail in the Body pillar's article on sleep (Body 1.7.1) and in this pillar's article on circadian alignment (2.4.2): consistent timing, morning light, restricted late-evening light, protection of sleep from cognitive activation in the hours before bed.

Physical movement, perhaps counterintuitively given the exhaustion that defines burnout, is one of the more reliably restorative inputs in this phase. The relevant evidence base is substantial; movement of moderate intensity has measurable effects on HPA-axis regulation, mood, sleep quality, and the inflammatory profile that often accompanies chronic stress. Heavy training is contraindicated — the system does not have the capacity to recover from it — but walking, gentle yoga, or low-intensity cardiovascular movement performs reliably well. Nutrition matters in the broad-strokes way it always does and is treated in the Body pillar; the specific intervention for burnout is less about optimising and more about not undermining recovery through erratic eating or substance use.

**The third move is psychological detachment.** Sabine Sonnentag's research, introduced in articles 2.4.1 and 2.4.3, established detachment from work as the active ingredient distinguishing restorative non-work time from non-restorative non-work time. In burnout recovery, detachment becomes essential rather than optional. Time physically away from work that is spent in continued cognitive engagement with work — checking email, ruminating about deadlines, mentally rehearsing problems — does not produce recovery. The structural moves that support detachment include physical separation from work environments and tools, designated time-frames during which work-related input is not consumed, and engagement with activities that compete cognitively with work-related thought. The DRAMMA framework introduced in article 2.4.1 is a useful diagnostic for whether a given activity is genuinely competing for cognitive resources or merely passing time alongside work-related thought.

**The fourth move is human contact.** The cynicism dimension of burnout, which Maslach's research identified as a defensive distancing from the people one's work involves, tends to generalise outward across time. People in burnout commonly withdraw from relationships outside work as well, partly because those relationships require emotional resources the system does not have, and partly because the depersonalisation that began as a work-related defence becomes a more pervasive orientation. Reversing this requires deliberate engagement with relationships that supply support without demanding what the depleted system cannot meet. The relevant relationships are not necessarily the most intense ones; they are the ones that produce the felt experience of being known and accepted without requiring performance. People are not always able to identify which relationships in their life have this quality without examination, but the examination is worth doing.

**The fifth move is professional support.** This is the one that the popular discussion most often soft-pedals, and the empirical literature most consistently endorses. Cognitive behavioural therapy has the strongest evidence base for burnout-related psychological recovery, partly because the cognitive patterns that maintain burnout — particularly the rigid beliefs about productivity, worth, and obligation that often underlie it — are precisely what CBT's cognitive restructuring is designed to address. Other modalities, including ACT (treated in article 2.6.3) and various forms of psychodynamic therapy, have more variable evidence bases but produce real benefit for substantial proportions of those who engage with them. For burnout that has consolidated severely, particularly where depression is co-occurring, medical evaluation is appropriate; the pharmacological and psychotherapeutic interventions for depression apply when burnout has progressed to the point where the line between the two has blurred.

**The sixth move, and one of the most important, is structural rather than personal.** Burnout that resolved without changes to the conditions that produced it tends to recur. The temptation, on emerging from acute burnout, is to return to the previous arrangement with the assumption that one will manage it differently. This rarely succeeds; the conditions that produced the syndrome will, with high probability, produce it again. The post-recovery period is therefore the time in which honest reassessment of the work, the role, the relationships, and the broader life structure is most productive. What needs to change is not always obvious from inside the syndrome, and is often clearer in the period immediately after acute recovery, when the cognitive system is functional enough to think but the lived memory of the burnout is still vivid.

There are also things that consistently do not work, and naming them is a useful service. *Working harder* does not work; it is the move that produced burnout in the first place. *Productivity systems* do not work, beyond a brief novelty effect; the problem is not organisation, it is overload. *Pushing through* does not work, although it remains the most common response. *Single restorative interventions* — a meditation app, a vacation, a new exercise routine — do not work alone, although they may contribute when integrated into the broader sequence above. *Pretending the syndrome is something else* — calling it laziness, a phase to be ridden out — wastes the early-intervention window in which the trajectory is most easily altered.

A final point worth making is that recovery from burnout is not, in most cases, a return to exactly the previous self. The processes that produced the syndrome have left changes — sometimes adaptive, sometimes not — and the version of oneself that emerges is usually different from the one that entered. The post-traumatic growth literature treated in article 2.6.3 applies here in a more limited form: substantial proportions of people who recover from burnout report changes in priorities, in tolerance for certain kinds of demand, in clarity about what they want their life to look like. These changes are real, and they often inform the structural changes that prevent recurrence. They are not, however, automatic, and treating them as the silver lining of an experience that should not have been allowed to progress this far misrepresents the syndrome. Burnout is recoverable from. It is not, in any honest reading, a useful thing to have happened.

The state one is in when reading this article is information about a particular configuration of load, recovery, and relationship to work that has reached a boundary. The boundary, recognised, is the beginning of the work of restoring the system to one in which sustainable engagement is again possible. That work is gradual, rarely linear, and — based on the consistent finding across the literature — almost always achievable when the conditions that produced the syndrome are, at least partially, allowed to change.
