Two people receive the same email. It announces a high-stakes presentation in three days. The first person feels a sharpening — a clarification of what needs to happen — and produces some of their best work that week. The second person feels a contraction — a tightening that makes thinking harder — and produces work below their usual standard despite spending more hours on it. The objective stressor is identical. The relationship between stress and performance is not a single curve. It is at minimum a family of curves, and the question of where one falls on it is among the more practically consequential questions in performance psychology.

The classical model in the literature is the *Yerkes-Dodson law*, named for the 1908 paper by Robert Yerkes and John Dodson in which they reported that mice learned a discrimination task fastest at moderate levels of electric shock, and more slowly at both lower and higher levels. The finding produced an inverted-U relationship between arousal and performance: too little arousal and the system is under-engaged; too much and the system is overwhelmed; somewhere in between is a peak. The inverted-U has become one of the most widely cited curves in popular psychology, and it has also been one of the most frequently misapplied.

Three corrections to the casual reading are worth making before the framework can be used productively. The first is that Yerkes and Dodson's actual data did not show a single curve. They reported that the optimal level of arousal varies with task difficulty: simple tasks tolerate, and even benefit from, higher arousal, while complex tasks peak at lower levels and degrade more quickly as arousal rises. This is the part of the law most often dropped in popular invocations, and it is the part that does most of the explanatory work. The same physiological state that sharpens performance on a routine, well-rehearsed task can substantially impair performance on an unfamiliar one requiring flexible cognition. The second correction is that the original studies measured *arousal*, not stress in the modern psychological sense, and the two are related but not identical. Arousal can be produced by excitement, caffeine, threat, or anticipation of reward; the cognitive consequences of these are not all the same. The third correction is that the inverted-U is a population-average shape; individuals vary substantially in where their personal peak sits.

A more sophisticated framework, developed by physiologist Daniel Lieberman and others over the past two decades, draws on the underlying neurochemistry. Cognitive performance, particularly performance on prefrontal-cortex-dependent tasks, is sensitive to the levels of two neurotransmitters in particular: norepinephrine and dopamine. A 2011 review by neuroscientist Amy Arnsten in *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* summarised the evidence: at moderate levels, norepinephrine and dopamine support prefrontal function, enhancing focus, working memory, and cognitive flexibility. At low levels, prefrontal function flags. At high levels, particularly under acute stress, the prefrontal cortex is functionally taken offline — Arnsten described this as a *stress-induced reversion to more reflexive, habit-based behaviour*. The shift makes evolutionary sense. Under genuine threat, deliberation is too slow; reflexive, well-practised responses are favoured. The cost is paid when the situation actually requires deliberation rather than reflex, which is most of the time in modern life.

This neurochemical picture explains several features of the inverted-U that the original behavioural observation could not. It explains why high stress impairs novel and complex tasks more than familiar ones — novel tasks require the prefrontal capacity that high stress takes offline. It explains why moderate stress can enhance performance on the right kinds of task — the same neurotransmitter elevations that impair complex thinking improve simpler, well-practised performance. And it explains why people with stronger baseline executive function tend to tolerate higher stress before performance degrades — they are operating with more prefrontal capacity, and have more to lose before falling below the threshold of useful function.

There are also reliable individual differences in where the personal optimum sits. The *Trait Anxiety* literature, beginning with Charles Spielberger's work in the 1960s, established that some people consistently appraise more situations as threatening, and operate at higher baseline arousal, than others. People high in trait anxiety tend to peak earlier on the performance curve; what is moderate stress for the average person may already be past their optimum. The reverse pattern holds for those low in trait anxiety, who may benefit from higher stress before performance degrades. Neither pattern is more correct than the other; they are different positions on the same underlying curve. The practical implication is that universal recommendations about stress management — *reduce stress* or *embrace stress* — are unlikely to be uniformly correct. The relevant question is where one's personal optimum is, and how far the current situation is from it.

The challenge-versus-threat distinction introduced in the previous article is the lever that makes movement along the curve possible. Blascovich and Mendes' research has shown that the same situation can be appraised either way, with different physiological signatures and different performance consequences. *Challenge* appraisals — situations evaluated as demanding but within one's resources — tend to produce arousal patterns that fall within the productive range of the performance curve. *Threat* appraisals — situations evaluated as exceeding one's resources — tend to produce patterns that overshoot it. The same email about the same presentation produces a challenge profile in some people on some days and a threat profile on others, and the difference is not in the email.

This is also where the limits of the model should be acknowledged honestly. The performance curve is a useful heuristic, but it tends to imply that there is always an optimum and that it is reachable. Both implications are partial. There are situations of objective demand under which no level of appraisal will keep arousal in the productive range; there are also chronic conditions, including the kinds of allostatic load discussed in the previous article, that flatten the curve and reduce the height of the achievable peak. Stress management is not, in such cases, a matter of finer dial-tuning. It is a matter of changing the conditions under which the system is operating. The curve describes what the system can do given a particular configuration; the configuration itself is a separate question.

A useful framing for everyday application is this. The peak of the curve is not a place to live continuously — that is itself a form of chronic stress, and the system pays the cost. The peak is a place to be able to reach, reliably, when the situation calls for it, and to leave afterwards. Sustainable performance over years is closer to oscillation than to perpetual peak. The stress response is a tool. It performs well when used as one. It performs badly when activated continuously, and that pattern is, in the end, what most contemporary stress problems actually are.

The Yerkes-Dodson curve, properly understood, is not a prescription. It is a description of a relationship that has individual, task-specific, and configuration-specific variation. Working with it intelligently requires knowing roughly where one is on it, what the current task actually requires, and which direction adjustment should run.
