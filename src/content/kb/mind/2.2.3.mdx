There is a kind of small, quiet realisation that most people have at some point. It is the moment of noticing — usually after the fact — that *every* Sunday evening you feel a small dread you do not feel on other evenings, that *every* time you talk to a particular family member there is a flatness for the rest of the day, that *most* of your worst arguments with your partner happen on the days you skip exercise. The realisation has a particular flavour. It is the recognition of a pattern that was always there, that you were always inside, and that you somehow could not see until you stepped back far enough.

This is the territory of triggers and patterns. Both terms are, in popular usage, a little overloaded. In the technical literature they have specific meanings. A *trigger* is a cue — internal or external — that reliably evokes a particular emotional response, learned through repeated past association. A *pattern* is the shape of those responses across time, visible only when you have enough data points to see one. Triggers are local; patterns are longitudinal. Most people know they have triggers. Most people have no real picture of their patterns, because patterns are not directly observable from inside any single moment.

The mechanism by which triggers form is one of the oldest stories in psychology. Ivan Pavlov's experiments at the turn of the twentieth century — dogs salivating to the sound of a bell that had previously been paired with food — established the principle of *classical conditioning*: a neutral cue, repeatedly paired with an emotionally significant event, becomes able to evoke the emotional response on its own. The same mechanism operates in human emotional life, and it operates without permission. A particular tone of voice that, in childhood, preceded conflict can produce a flicker of anxiety in adulthood when heard from anyone, in any context. A song that played during a difficult breakup can flatten an otherwise fine afternoon ten years later. The cue does not have to be conscious. The response does not require agreement from the conscious mind to occur.

The neurobiology was mapped most influentially by neuroscientist Joseph LeDoux, whose research over several decades — synthesised in his 1996 book *The Emotional Brain* — traced how the amygdala learns to associate cues with threat, and how this learning is fast, durable, and often bypasses the slower, more deliberative cortical pathways. LeDoux's central finding is that the brain has what he called a *low road* and a *high road* for emotional processing. The low road runs from the senses to the amygdala almost directly, producing a rapid, embodied reaction before the cortex has finished interpreting what is happening. The high road runs through the cortex first and produces a slower, more contextualised response. Both are operating, all the time. The low road is faster, which means in many situations it gets there first — and the conscious mind arrives to find an emotion already in progress, and has to figure out what just happened.

The modern version of this picture comes from *predictive processing* theory, developed in different forms by neuroscientists Karl Friston and Andy Clark, among others. The brain, on this view, is not primarily a passive responder to the world. It is a continuous prediction engine, generating expectations about what is about to happen and updating those expectations against incoming sensory data. Most emotional triggers, in this framing, are not raw responses to stimuli. They are the activation of a learned prediction: *this kind of cue, in the past, has preceded this kind of outcome*. The body prepares for the predicted outcome before the actual outcome arrives. This is why triggers can feel out of proportion to the present situation — because the response is calibrated to the past, not to what is actually happening now.

What this means practically is that the gap between trigger and emotional response, however automatic it feels, is not empty. It contains something. Cognitive psychologist Aaron Beck, working in the 1960s and 1970s, described what he called the *cognitive chain*: situation → automatic thought → emotion. The crucial element is the middle one. Between the cue and the feeling is a flash of interpretation, usually too fast to register, that is doing most of the work. *He looked at me coldly* (the situation) → *he must be angry with me* (the automatic thought) → anxiety, defensiveness, withdrawal (the emotion). The same situation with a different automatic thought — *he looks tired* — produces a different emotion. Beck's central therapeutic insight was that the automatic thought is the leverage point. The situation is often beyond your control. The emotion is downstream of the thought. The thought is, with practice, something you can catch and examine. This insight became the foundation of cognitive behavioural therapy, which remains one of the most extensively validated psychological interventions in existence, and it returns explicitly in the article on cognitive reappraisal (2.5.3).

But noticing automatic thoughts in the moment is hard, and noticing them across moments is harder. This is where the distinction between triggers and patterns becomes important. A pattern is what becomes visible when many triggered episodes are laid next to each other. The pattern is not something any single episode can show you. It is a property of the data set.

This is also where memory fails. A great deal of research on what psychologists call *ecological momentary assessment* — the practice of sampling people's emotional states many times across days and weeks — has shown that retrospective accounts of how people felt in a given period are surprisingly inaccurate. People do not forget randomly. They forget in characteristic ways: peak emotional moments are remembered, mundane ones are not; the end of an experience disproportionately shapes the memory of the whole; current mood colours recall of past moods. A landmark series of studies by psychologist Arthur Stone and colleagues throughout the 1990s and 2000s consistently found that aggregated daily reports painted a noticeably different picture from end-of-week summaries by the same individuals. People who had reported moderate stress most days remembered the week as highly stressful, because the worst day was the most retrievable. People who had reported high but improving stress remembered the week as fine, because the last day was the most retrievable.

The implication is structural and matters beyond academic interest. If you want to see your own patterns honestly, you cannot reconstruct them from memory at the end of the month. The reconstruction is systematically distorted in the direction of whatever stands out. The only reliable way to see longitudinal emotional patterns is to capture small, time-stamped readings as they happen, then look at the aggregate later. This is precisely why Dolphin's daily logging is structured the way it is — small, repeated samples of mood and the situational features around it, accumulated until the patterns are visible by inspection rather than by introspection. The architecture is not about capturing every nuance in the moment. It is about avoiding the failure mode of trying to remember nuances later.

Once a pattern is visible, the next move is rarely complicated. *Sunday evening dread* responds to changing what Sunday evening looks like — a phone call with a friend, a planned activity, a different relationship to Monday's first hour. *Flatness after a particular relative* responds to deliberately scheduling something restorative afterwards, or shortening the visit, or noticing what specifically about the interaction is producing the flatness and addressing it. *Worse arguments on no-exercise days* responds to noticing the link and treating exercise as an emotional regulation tool, not just a fitness one. None of these is a profound intervention. They become available only after the pattern has been seen.

The harder cases are the ones where the trigger itself is loaded — where the pattern points toward something from much earlier, woven into the body's prediction of what to expect from people or situations. These are not always solvable by adjustment alone, and recognising when a pattern requires more than self-management is itself a form of self-knowledge. But the great majority of everyday emotional patterns are, in the end, reachable through observation, attention, and small changes. The rate-limiting step is not insight. It is the willingness to log what is happening, day after day, until the picture comes into focus.
