Clinicians who treat acute panic learn something early in their training that often takes longer to find its way into the popular literature: when a patient is in the middle of an intense activation episode, talking does not work. Not because the patient is unwilling, and not because the words are wrong, but because the cognitive system required to make use of words is, in that state, partially unavailable. The reappraisal techniques discussed in the previous article (2.5.3) depend on prefrontal capacity. Under sufficiently high acute activation, that capacity is reduced — Amy Arnsten's neurobiological work, introduced in article 2.5.2, has documented this consistently. The intervention has to enter the system somewhere else.

This is the territory of *grounding*: a family of techniques that operate not by changing how a situation is interpreted but by directly modulating the physiological state through which interpretation is happening. Where reappraisal is top-down — cognition modifying physiology — grounding is bottom-up — physiology modifying cognition. The two are complementary rather than competing. They apply at different points along the activation curve, and a serviceable regulation toolkit contains both.

The mechanism rests on a feature of human physiology that is often acknowledged in passing but rarely taken seriously: the relationship between bodily state and emotional state is bidirectional. The conventional folk model — emotions are mental events, the body responds — gets the directionality only half right. As the constructed-emotion framework in article 2.2.1 made clear, emotions are assembled in part from interoceptive signals, which means deliberate alteration of those signals changes the construction. A slowed breath does not just signify calm; it produces, through measurable downstream effects on the autonomic nervous system, the physiological substrate from which calm is partly constructed. The body, used deliberately, becomes a regulation tool.

The autonomic system at the centre of this is described in detail in the foundational Body article on the nervous system (Body 1.1.1). The summary version is that two branches of the autonomic nervous system operate in opposing balance: the sympathetic branch, which mobilises the body for action and dominates during stress, and the parasympathetic branch, which supports rest, recovery, and digestion. The relevant index for grounding purposes is *vagal tone*, a measure of activity in the vagus nerve, which carries the major parasympathetic outflow and which can be inferred non-invasively through *heart rate variability* — the small variations in time between successive heartbeats. Higher resting heart rate variability is associated, in a substantial body of research synthesised in Julian Thayer and Richard Lane's 2009 model of *neurovisceral integration*, with better emotional regulation, more flexible cognitive performance, and faster recovery from stress. The system that governs the body's calming response is therefore, in a useful sense, also the system that governs emotional flexibility, and many grounding techniques operate by deliberately raising vagal activity.

The most extensively researched physiological lever is *slow, extended exhalation*. Work from neuroscientist Jack Feldman's laboratory at UCLA, particularly research published in *Science* in 2017, identified a specific cluster of neurons in the brainstem (the pre-Bötzinger complex) that links breathing pattern to arousal state. Slow exhalation activates parasympathetic outflow through the vagus nerve, lowering heart rate, blood pressure, and sympathetic activation within seconds. The effect is measurable, replicable, and noticeable subjectively — it is also why nearly every contemplative tradition, evolved independently across cultures, includes some form of slow-exhale practice. A particular variant, the *physiological sigh* — two short inhales through the nose followed by a long exhale through the mouth — has been studied as one of the most efficient acute-activation downregulators available. A controlled trial published by Melis Yilmaz Balban and David Spiegel and colleagues at Stanford in 2023 found that five minutes of cyclic sighing produced larger reductions in subjective state anxiety than equivalent durations of meditation, with corresponding improvements in mood markers across a four-week trial.

Beyond breath, several other grounding techniques have accumulated meaningful empirical support, although the quality of evidence varies substantially across techniques and the popular literature frequently overstates the strength of the underlying findings.

The *5-4-3-2-1 sensory grounding* technique — naming five things one can see, four one can feel, three one can hear, two one can smell, one one can taste — is widely used in trauma-informed care and has the structural advantage of redirecting attention from internal arousal toward external sensory input, which engages perceptual and language systems and tends to reduce dissociation. The empirical literature on this specific technique is thinner than its widespread clinical use suggests, but it operates on a well-established principle: sensory engagement in the present moment competes for cognitive resources with rumination and threat-oriented attention, and the competition tends to favour the more concrete input.

*Cold exposure*, particularly to the face, engages what physiologists call the *mammalian dive reflex* — a coordinated parasympathetic response originally adapted to conserve oxygen during cold-water submersion, which produces rapid bradycardia (slowed heart rate) and reduced sympathetic outflow within seconds. The technique has a longer history in dialectical behaviour therapy than in popular wellness contexts, where Marsha Linehan's TIPP protocol (Temperature, Intense exercise, Paced breathing, Paired muscle relaxation) has used cold-water face immersion for acute distress regulation since the 1990s. The mechanism is robust; the broader claims about cold exposure for general well-being often extend beyond what the evidence supports.

*Body scanning* and *progressive muscle relaxation*, the latter introduced by physician Edmund Jacobson in 1929 and refined extensively since, operate by re-engaging interoceptive awareness while deliberately reducing skeletal muscle tension. The connection to the interoception research of Bud Craig (introduced in article 2.1.2) is direct. Acute activation tends to produce both sympathetic dominance and a pulling away from interoceptive signal — a kind of dissociation from the body. Deliberately scanning attention through the body, while simultaneously releasing held tension, tends to reverse both. The empirical literature on progressive muscle relaxation is robust and has been incorporated into nearly every cognitive-behavioural treatment protocol developed since the 1970s.

A final family of techniques involves *bilateral stimulation* — alternating left-right input through eye movements, tapping, or auditory cues. This is the foundation of EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing), developed by Francine Shapiro from the late 1980s, which has accumulated reasonable evidence for trauma processing although the specific mechanism remains debated. The component most relevant to acute grounding is simpler than the full clinical protocol — bilateral input appears to engage attentional resources in a way that reduces the intensity of currently-active emotional content.

Across these techniques a common pattern emerges. None of them changes the situation that produced the activation; none of them, used alone, addresses the underlying conditions that may need addressing. What they do is create a window — a period of reduced activation in which the prefrontal capacity required for actual problem-solving comes back online. They are not solutions. They are conditions for solutions to become possible.

This is the structural reason grounding belongs in the regulation toolkit alongside reappraisal rather than as a substitute for it. Reappraisal works when the cognitive system is online enough to use it. Grounding works when it is not, and produces the conditions under which it can become so. In practice, the sequence in acute regulation tends to run: grounding first, reappraisal afterwards. The body is settled enough that the mind can do its work.

A practical note worth stating directly. These techniques are most useful when they have been practised before they are needed. Attempting to use a slow-breathing technique for the first time during an acute panic episode is more difficult than using one that has already been rehearsed in calmer states many times. The skill is, like most skills, built in low-stakes conditions and deployed in high-stakes ones. Building it into a regular practice — daily breathing, regular body-scan, occasional cold exposure — is what makes it reliably available later. The available technique is the practised one.
