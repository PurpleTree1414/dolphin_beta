Your heart is racing slightly. There is a tight feeling in your chest. Your palms are warm. Are you anxious or excited? About to give a presentation, or about to ride a roller coaster? The physiological signature is, in both cases, almost identical. The difference between *anxious* and *excited* is not in the body. It is in what your brain has decided the body is signalling — and what it has decided depends on the situation, the words you have available, and what you have learned to expect from situations like this one.

This is not a small point. It is the central insight of the last forty years of research on what emotions actually are, and the answer is more interesting than the folk view we usually carry around.

The folk view — and the older scientific view — treats emotions as a small set of universal, hardwired responses. Anger looks the same in everyone. Fear has a recognisable face. Disgust feels distinct from sadness. The most influential version of this view came from psychologist Paul Ekman, whose research in the 1960s and 1970s appeared to show that six basic emotions — happiness, sadness, fear, anger, disgust, surprise — had matching facial expressions recognised across cultures. The implication, codified in his 1992 paper *An Argument for Basic Emotions*, was that emotions are pre-installed circuits, with each having a distinct biological "fingerprint": a particular pattern of neural activity, autonomic response, and outward expression. You could, in principle, look at someone's brain and physiology and read off which emotion they were having.

This view has not held up. Decades of careful neuroimaging work, summarised most thoroughly by neuroscientist Lisa Feldman Barrett, have failed to find consistent fingerprints. There is no single neural signature for fear that distinguishes it from anger. There is no autonomic pattern that reliably maps onto sadness. The same physiological state — heart pounding, breath quickening, attention sharpening — accompanies many different emotions, and the same emotion can occur with very different physiological patterns. Barrett's 2017 book *How Emotions Are Made* reframes the entire field around what is now called the *theory of constructed emotion*, and the central claim is straightforward: emotions are not pre-built responses that the brain detects. They are constructions the brain assembles, in real time, from three ingredients.

The first ingredient is interoception — the brain's reading of the body. The thread connecting the racing heart, the tight chest, and the damp palms is what neuroscientist Bud Craig called *the material me*: the constant, low-level sensing of the body's internal state, which the foundational article on body awareness in this knowledge base treats in detail (see Body 1.1.1). Interoception produces a stream of *affect* — broad, dimensional information about how the body is doing, captured along two axes: pleasant versus unpleasant, and high versus low arousal. Affect is not yet emotion. It is the raw input.

The second ingredient is *conceptual knowledge* — the categories and labels you have learned for emotional states. A person who grew up with a rich emotional vocabulary, in a household where feelings were named and discussed, has more conceptual tools to construct fine-grained emotions from a given pattern of affect. A person without those categories may experience the same physiological state as undifferentiated *bad* or *off*. The categories are not optional. They are part of the construction.

The third ingredient is *context*: where you are, what just happened, what you expect, what the people around you are doing. The same elevated heart rate is *anxiety* in a hospital waiting room and *excitement* on a roller coaster, because the brain uses context to decide which emotion is most likely.

The classic experimental demonstration of this dates from 1962, when psychologists Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer injected participants with adrenaline (without telling some of them what it was), then placed them in different social environments. Those put in a room with someone acting playful tended to interpret their physiological arousal as euphoria. Those placed with someone acting hostile tended to interpret the same arousal as anger. The body was identical. The construction was different.

This is where thought enters the picture, because constructed emotion is also constructed cognition. Psychologist Richard Lazarus, in a long career culminating in his 1991 book *Emotion and Adaptation*, demonstrated that emotions are not separable from the appraisals — the rapid, often automatic judgements — your mind makes about a situation. The same email reads as a routine question or as a personal attack depending on how you appraise it. The appraisal is partly conscious and partly automatic, and it shapes the emotion that follows. This is not a side comment about emotion. It is fundamental to how emotion works, and it is why thought and feeling are not two separate systems running in parallel. They are tightly looped: thoughts shape emotions, emotions shape thoughts, and the loop runs faster than introspection can usually catch.

Antonio Damasio's *somatic marker* hypothesis, laid out in his 1994 book *Descartes' Error*, fits neatly inside this picture. Damasio argued that the brain learns to associate bodily states with the outcomes of past situations — a tightening in the gut when contemplating a particular kind of decision, a small lightness in the chest when considering another — and that these somatic markers function as fast, embodied signals that guide reasoning before deliberate thought catches up. People with damage to brain regions that integrate bodily and cognitive signals — particularly the ventromedial prefrontal cortex — make poorer decisions even when their reasoning ability is intact. They have lost a layer of feedback that the rest of us run on without noticing.

Why does any of this matter, if the felt experience of emotion is the same regardless of how the science describes it? Because the constructed view, taken seriously, has a consequence the folk view does not: it offers more room to intervene. If emotions are pre-installed, hardwired, and given, then there is little to do but suffer them. If emotions are built — every time, from interoception plus concepts plus context — then each ingredient is, in principle, a place to influence the result. You can sharpen interoception, through attention to the body. You can build richer conceptual vocabulary, through learning to name what you feel. You can shift context, through changing where you are or who you are with. None of these eliminate emotion. None of them should. But they change the construction, sometimes dramatically.

This is the implicit foundation for almost everything that follows in this section and the next. The skill of *labelling* what you feel is not optional ornamentation; it is one of the three ingredients (covered in 2.2.2). The fact that the same trigger keeps producing the same emotion is not destiny; it is a learned construction that can, with attention, be revised (covered in 2.2.3). The technique of *cognitive reappraisal* — changing the appraisal that shaped the emotion — is one of the most studied and most effective regulation strategies available, and it is not magic. It is the construction process being interrupted before it finishes (covered in 2.5.3).

The folk view of emotions as fixed, given things you simply have to deal with is comforting but mistaken. Emotions are something the brain is doing, continuously, in collaboration with the body and the situation. That collaboration leaves room for influence — not total, not always, but more than people tend to assume.
