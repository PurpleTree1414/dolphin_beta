In the early 1970s, the social psychologist Christina Maslach was conducting interviews with workers in human-services professions — clinicians, social workers, nurses, lawyers serving low-income clients — for what she initially conceived as research on coping with emotional arousal at work. The interviewees, however, kept describing something that did not fit the framework she had brought to the project. They were not coping with arousal. They were depleted in a way that arousal could not produce on its own: emotionally hollowed out, increasingly cynical about the people they were meant to serve, and convinced that their work no longer accomplished anything. The word that several of them volunteered for the state was *burned out*. The term was already in informal use among workers in those professions. It was not yet a research construct.

Maslach's subsequent work over the next two decades, including the development of the *Maslach Burnout Inventory* (MBI) in 1981, established burnout as a measurable, multidimensional syndrome rather than a synonym for severe stress. The construct, as she defined it, has three components, and each is independently measurable. The first is *emotional exhaustion*: the sense of being depleted of emotional resources, of having nothing left to give to the demands of the work. The second is *depersonalisation* (or, in later revisions, *cynicism*): an emotional distancing from the people one's work serves, often expressed as detached, perfunctory, or even hostile responses to the very people one once cared about helping. The third is *reduced personal accomplishment* (later refined to *reduced professional efficacy*): the conviction that one's work is no longer effective, that one's competence has eroded, that nothing one does makes a meaningful difference.

The three dimensions are correlated but not identical, and the pattern of relative severity matters diagnostically. Emotional exhaustion typically appears first; cynicism develops as a defensive response to ongoing exhaustion; reduced efficacy tends to consolidate later as the cognitive consequence of the first two. Burnout that is detected at the exhaustion stage is more amenable to intervention than burnout that has progressed through all three. This sequencing is one of the more practically important findings in the literature, because it identifies a window — usually months — in which the trajectory can be altered before the full syndrome is established.

In 2019 the World Health Organisation included burnout in the eleventh revision of the International Classification of Diseases. The classification is precise and worth quoting in summary: burnout is described as a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, characterised by feelings of energy depletion, increased mental distance from one's job, and reduced professional efficacy. The WHO explicitly limits the term to occupational contexts and explicitly distinguishes it from depression, although the two share substantial features and frequently co-occur. The distinction is partly diagnostic and partly aetiological: depression is typically pervasive across life domains, while burnout is initially work-specific; depression involves global loss of pleasure, while burnout involves specific exhaustion of professional engagement. In practice, burnout untreated tends to migrate beyond work over time, and the line between advanced burnout and depression becomes harder to draw.

What this article picks up, beyond the basic taxonomy, is the question of detection: what the warning signs are, why they are reliably missed, and what an honest reading of one's own state actually looks like.

The signs cluster across four broad domains. Each is independently measurable; none is sufficient on its own; the presence of multiple signs across multiple domains is the diagnostic pattern.

The *cognitive* signs include difficulty concentrating, reduced working memory capacity, slower information processing, and what is sometimes called *cognitive fog* — the experience of thinking through a substance that resists. Tasks that were previously routine require disproportionate effort. Decisions that were previously straightforward feel exhausting. The kind of attentional control treated in articles 2.1.1 and 2.3.1 becomes measurably harder to deploy. Some of this is the expected consequence of chronic stress on prefrontal function (Arnsten's work, treated in 2.5.2); some of it is the consequence of the executive system being chronically engaged in defending against further demand rather than performing the work in front of it.

The *emotional* signs include increased irritability, decreased emotional resilience to small setbacks, emotional flattening (the sense that one is going through the motions without much affective engagement), and what Maslach's framework specifically captured: a hardening or cynicism toward the people one's work involves. Patients become cases. Students become problems. Colleagues become obstacles. The hardening is not a character development; it is a defensive narrowing in response to ongoing emotional load that exceeds available resources.

The *physical* signs include persistent fatigue that does not resolve with sleep, sleep disruption (most commonly difficulty falling asleep due to mental rumination, or waking unrefreshed despite adequate hours), elevated baseline arousal (a sense of being unable to fully relax), increased susceptibility to minor illness (the immune consequences of chronic cortisol elevation, treated in 2.5.1), and somatic symptoms — tension headaches, gastrointestinal disturbance, musculoskeletal pain — that have no clear medical cause but track closely with workload.

The *behavioural* signs include declining quality of work despite equivalent or greater effort, increased use of avoidance strategies (treated in 2.4.3 and 2.6.2), reduced engagement with relationships outside work, withdrawal from activities that previously produced restoration, and a pattern that is particularly diagnostic: doing the things that previously produced rest while no longer experiencing rest from them. The vacation does not feel restorative. The weekend does not feel like a weekend. The activity that used to recharge no longer does. This last sign is one of the more reliable indicators that the system has moved beyond ordinary fatigue into something more structural.

A particular failure mode in detection is worth naming directly. Burnout tends to be missed by the person experiencing it because the cognitive equipment required to notice it is the same equipment that is being degraded. The metacognitive monitoring discussed in article 2.1.1 — the capacity to step back and observe one's own state — is itself an executive function, and executive function is exactly what burnout impairs. The person in burnout is therefore the worst-positioned observer of whether burnout is occurring. People around them notice first, often by months. Self-recognition tends to lag.

This is also why retrospective signals are more reliable than introspective ones. The *Maslach Burnout Inventory* and its variants exist as instruments precisely because subjective state-reports in this domain are systematically distorted, in the direction the affected person finds least disruptive: it is not happening; it is just a hard week; the holiday will fix it. Time-stamped behavioural data — sleep quality across weeks, frequency of irritability with colleagues, output trends, time taken to recover from minor setbacks — is harder for the system to deny. This is the diagnostic case for the kind of longitudinal logging that Dolphin's architecture supports. The same retrospective-recall problem identified in article 2.2.3 in the context of emotional patterns applies, with greater consequences, to detecting the slow erosion of state that constitutes early burnout.

The earliest reliable warning signs, ahead of the fully consolidated syndrome, are in two places. The first is the recovery signal: how the system feels at the end of weekends, after holidays, after periods that should be restorative. Sustained insufficiency of restoration despite adequate input is one of the most informative early markers. The second is the *engagement* signal: the degree to which the work that one previously cared about still feels meaningful. The transition from caring to going-through-the-motions, when it persists across weeks rather than days, is among the more reliable predictors of the cynicism dimension consolidating.

What this article does not attempt to do is treat burnout once detected. That is the subject of article 2.7.3. What it does attempt is to make the syndrome legible — to name what is being experienced, to identify the warning patterns that distinguish it from ordinary fatigue, and to provide the framework against which one's actual state can be honestly evaluated. The recognition is the entry point. Without it, the trajectory tends to continue along its existing slope; with it, the question of what to do about it can at least begin to be asked.
