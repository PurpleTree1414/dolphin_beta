The modern empirical study of coping began in the same place, and at roughly the same time, as the modern empirical study of stress. When Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman synthesised the field in their 1984 book *Stress, Appraisal, and Coping*, the two concepts were inseparable for them. The question of how stressful a situation was, on their account, was partly the question of what resources a person had for handling it; and the question of what resources a person had was partly the question of how they characteristically coped. Coping, in this framing, is not what happens after stress; it is part of what determines how much stress actually occurs.

Lazarus and Folkman drew the most durable distinction in the field: *problem-focused coping*, aimed at altering the situation producing the stress, and *emotion-focused coping*, aimed at managing the emotional response to a situation that may not be alterable. Neither is more correct than the other, and the appropriate use of each depends on whether the situation is amenable to direct change. A demanding deadline that can be renegotiated calls primarily for problem-focused coping; the experience of grief calls primarily for emotion-focused coping. Difficulties arise when a person applies the wrong category — attempting to problem-solve a situation that cannot be solved, or attempting to manage feelings about a situation that genuinely needs to be changed. The cognitive flexibility to distinguish the two, treated in the next article, is a meaningful regulation skill in its own right.

The taxonomy was extended substantially by psychologist Charles Carver, whose 1989 *COPE Inventory* and its 1997 short form, the *Brief COPE*, identified fifteen distinguishable coping strategies through factor analysis of how people actually report responding to stress. The list, in something close to its standard form, runs as follows: active coping, planning, suppression of competing activities, restraint coping, seeking social support (instrumental), seeking social support (emotional), positive reinterpretation, acceptance, religion, focus on and venting of emotions, denial, behavioural disengagement, mental disengagement, alcohol/drug use, and humour. This is what people actually do when stressed, in roughly the proportions in which they do it.

The honest version of the literature, accumulated over the four decades since Carver's framework was introduced, is that not all of these strategies have equivalent long-term consequences. Some are reliably associated, across longitudinal studies, with better mental health outcomes, faster recovery, and lower rates of stress-related illness. Others are reliably associated with the opposite. The classification of coping into *adaptive* and *maladaptive* categories is therefore not a moral judgement; it is an empirical one, and one that the literature supports with reasonable consistency.

The strategies that show up consistently as adaptive are those that engage with the source of stress (active coping, planning), those that draw on relational resources (instrumental and emotional support-seeking), those that involve cognitive revision of the meaning of the situation (positive reinterpretation, acceptance), and those that mobilise physical or contemplative resources (exercise, meditation, religious or contemplative practice for those for whom it is genuine). These strategies share a common structural feature: they keep the person engaged with reality, either by changing what is, by changing how it is interpreted, or by recruiting capacity from other systems to handle it. The cost of the engagement is paid in the moment; the benefit is paid afterwards.

The strategies that show up consistently as maladaptive — denial, behavioural disengagement, substance use, rumination, self-blame — share an opposing structural feature. They reduce engagement with the source of stress, either by withdrawing attention (denial, mental disengagement), by chemically numbing the response (substance use), or by directing the response inward in non-productive ways (rumination, self-blame). The benefit of disengagement is paid in the moment, in the form of immediate relief; the cost is paid afterwards, in the form of accumulated unaddressed demand and compounded difficulty.

This is the central honest point of the article, and it explains why maladaptive coping is so persistent despite being maladaptive: it works in the short term. That is what makes it attractive. The person who reaches for the third drink at the end of the day is not making an irrational choice from inside the moment of reaching; they are choosing the option with the lowest immediate activation cost and the most reliable short-term effect on subjective state. The cost of the choice is loaded entirely onto the next day, the next week, the next year. Maladaptive coping is not characterised by people choosing things that feel bad. It is characterised by people choosing things that feel good now and bad later. The system is operating exactly as designed; it is only that the design was calibrated for a world with shorter feedback loops than the one most contemporary coping decisions are made in.

A related finding worth treating directly concerns *venting*. The folk belief that expressing anger or frustration aloud reduces it has been remarkably durable; the empirical literature has been arguing against it since at least the 1990s. A series of careful experiments by social psychologist Brad Bushman, summarised in a widely cited 2002 paper, found that activities promoted as cathartic — hitting punching bags, expressing anger verbally, ruminating aloud — tended to *increase* subsequent aggression and prolonged angry mood, rather than reducing it. The mechanism is consistent with the constructed-emotion framework treated in 2.2.1: behaving as though one is angry tends to reinforce the construction of anger, not dissipate it. Sharing emotional experiences with a trusted person in a structured, supportive context is something different from venting, and the evidence for that as a coping resource is substantial. But unloading hot emotion into the world, as a strategy, generally extends rather than concludes it.

A second cultural confusion is worth naming. *Self-care*, in its current popular usage, has stretched substantially past its origins in nursing and clinical psychology to include almost any activity a person engages in for themselves. The empirical question of whether a given act of self-care is restorative or avoidant — treated in detail in article 2.4.3 on rest versus avoidance — is the same question as whether it functions as adaptive or maladaptive coping. Genuine self-care that addresses the source of stress, that draws on resources the person actually has access to, and that produces durable improvements in baseline state is one of the more reliable adaptive strategies. Self-care as a label applied to whatever a person does to avoid engaging with what is taxing them is something different, and is structurally identical to the avoidance strategies catalogued above.

The practical move is not to identify a single best coping strategy and apply it universally. The literature is consistent that *coping flexibility* — the capacity to deploy different strategies appropriate to different situations — predicts outcomes better than reliance on any single strategy, however adaptive. Problem-focused coping for solvable problems, emotion-focused coping for unsolvable ones, social support when the relational resources are available, contemplative or physical strategies when they are not. The skill is partly in the techniques themselves and partly in the recognition of which one a given situation calls for. Single-strategy reliance, even on otherwise adaptive strategies, tends to produce the failure mode of bringing the wrong tool to a particular kind of demand.

A small developmental observation closes the article honestly. The coping repertoire most adults bring into stress situations was assembled, in significant part, much earlier — through observation, through what worked at home, through what was modelled in the relationships that mattered most. Some people inherit repertoires that serve them well; some inherit ones that do not. Neither is a verdict on the person, but the inheritance has consequences, and the maladaptive patterns that show up reliably in adult life often have origins that pre-date the adult's capacity to choose them. Recognising this does not absolve responsibility for revising the patterns. It does, sometimes, reframe what the work of revising actually is.

The repertoire one currently has is not the repertoire one is stuck with. New strategies can be deliberately learned, practised in low-stakes situations, and made available for higher-stakes ones. The coping vocabulary of a forty-year-old is not, in any necessary sense, the same as the coping vocabulary of the same person at twenty-five. What it is, by default, is whatever has been left in place by years of accumulated reinforcement. What it can become, with deliberate work, is something the person has chosen.
