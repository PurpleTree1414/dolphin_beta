The word *stress*, in its modern psychological sense, is younger than most people assume. It was introduced into the biological literature in 1936 by the Hungarian-Canadian endocrinologist Hans Selye, and it was, by his own later admission, partly the result of his imperfect English — he had meant something closer to *strain*, the engineering term for the internal response of a material to an external load. The metaphor stuck, but the slippage between *strain* (the response) and *stress* (the load) has produced confusion in the scientific literature ever since. Selye's central observation, however, was sound and has held up: animals subjected to a wide variety of noxious stimuli — cold, surgical injury, toxic doses of various substances — produced a strikingly uniform set of physiological responses, regardless of the specific stimulus. He called this the *General Adaptation Syndrome*, and proposed three stages: alarm, resistance, and exhaustion. The framework is now considered a partial simplification, but it identified the right phenomenon. There is a generic biological response to demand, and it operates broadly across kinds of demand.

The physiological mechanism underlying that response is treated in detail in the foundational article on the HPA axis in the Body pillar (Body 1.8.1). The summary version is that two systems coordinate the response. The faster system is the *sympathetic-adrenomedullary* axis, which operates on a timescale of seconds: the sympathetic nervous system activates, the adrenal medulla releases adrenaline, the heart accelerates, blood pressure rises, attention narrows, glucose is mobilised. The slower system is the *hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal* axis, which operates on a timescale of minutes to hours: the hypothalamus releases CRH, the pituitary releases ACTH, the adrenal cortex releases cortisol, and a sustained mobilisation of resources follows. Both systems evolved to handle threats that were short, physical, and resolved within minutes. The system performs well under those conditions. The problem is that the kinds of demand that activate the system in modern life are increasingly long, non-physical, and unresolved.

What this article picks up, where the Body article hands off, is the cognitive and emotional dimensions of that mismatch. Two questions are particularly important. The first concerns how stress is appraised — that is, how the cognitive system decides whether a given situation is stressful in the first place. The second concerns how acute and chronic stress diverge in their effects on cognition and emotion, and why the difference matters more than is usually acknowledged.

The appraisal question was the central concern of psychologist Richard Lazarus, whose work over four decades — culminating in the 1984 book *Stress, Appraisal, and Coping*, co-authored with Susan Folkman — established the modern psychological framework. Lazarus's central claim was that stress is not a property of the situation but of the relationship between the situation and the person's evaluation of it. The same external event — a demanding deadline, a difficult conversation, a public-speaking obligation — produces a stress response in some people and not in others, or in the same person on some days and not on others, because the appraisal differs. Lazarus identified two stages of appraisal. *Primary appraisal* asks: is this situation a threat to something I care about? *Secondary appraisal* asks: do I have the resources to handle it? When primary appraisal flags a threat and secondary appraisal returns insufficient resources, the stress response activates. When secondary appraisal returns adequate resources, the same situation may register as a *challenge* — accompanied by elevated arousal, but with a meaningfully different physiological and psychological profile. The threat-versus-challenge distinction has since been elaborated by psychophysiologists Jim Blascovich and Wendy Mendes, whose research has shown that challenge appraisals produce more efficient cardiovascular profiles, better cognitive performance, and faster recovery than threat appraisals to the same situation.

The implication is significant for what stress actually is. Stress is not the demand itself. It is the cognitive system's evaluation that the demand exceeds available resources. This means the same level of objective demand can produce very different stress responses depending on how it is appraised, and that appraisal is partly modifiable. This is the foothold for the regulation techniques covered in the third article of this section.

The second question — the divergence of acute and chronic stress — is where the cognitive and physiological pictures connect most consequentially. Acute stress, in the sense Selye originally studied, is short-duration and self-limiting. The system activates, the demand passes, the system returns to baseline. Cortisol rises, peaks within twenty to forty minutes, and falls back to baseline within an hour or two. Within bounds, this is not pathological; the cognitive effects of acute stress can include enhanced focus on the immediate situation, sharpened sensory perception, and improved memory consolidation for emotionally significant events. The system was, in evolutionary terms, designed for this.

What it was not designed for is *chronic* stress: stress that does not resolve, because the demand is ongoing or because the appraisal of threat persists past the actual threat. Under chronic activation, the same physiological responses that were adaptive in the acute case become measurably harmful. The neurobiologist Robert Sapolsky has spent much of his career on this question, and his book *Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers* — now in its third edition — remains the most accessible synthesis. The summary is that virtually every major physiological system shows degradation under chronic stress, including cardiovascular, immune, metabolic, reproductive, and — most relevantly for this article — central nervous system function. Chronic elevation of cortisol has been shown to reduce hippocampal volume in both animal models and longitudinal human studies, with corresponding deficits in declarative memory; to impair prefrontal function, including the executive control treated in articles 2.1.1 and 2.3.1; and to bias attention toward threat-relevant stimuli, producing a self-reinforcing loop in which a stressed brain perceives more threats and therefore stays stressed.

The transition from acute to chronic does not require continuous high-intensity stress. It requires only that recovery between stress episodes is incomplete. This is where the connection to the previous section becomes important. The article on mental fatigue and recovery (2.4.1) drew on Bruce McEwen's concept of *allostatic load* — the cumulative wear on regulatory systems that have been required to operate above baseline for extended periods. Allostatic load is, in effect, the integrated cost of incomplete recovery, and chronic stress is one of its primary inputs. The cognitive consequences are not separate from the physiological ones. The chronically stressed brain is genuinely a different cognitive system: with reduced executive function, narrower attention, biased perception, and impaired emotional regulation.

There is a useful but imperfect distinction in the literature between *eustress* — stress that produces growth, learning, and adaptation — and *distress* — stress that produces damage. Selye himself proposed the distinction, and it remains intuitively useful, although the boundary between the two is more porous than the terminology suggests. The most reliable way to tell which one is operating is not the intensity of the stress in the moment but the trajectory afterwards: did the system recover and adapt, or did it remain elevated and degrade? Stress that resolves and produces adaptation is the substrate of growth. Stress that does not resolve is the substrate of erosion. The same external demand can produce either outcome depending on appraisal, recovery, and duration.

This frames the project of stress management in terms that are sharper than the popular discussion typically offers. The goal is not to eliminate stress, which would be neither possible nor desirable. The goal is to keep the stress response operating in the acute mode it was built for, and to prevent the slide into the chronic mode it was not. That depends on three things, treated across the next two articles and beyond: appraisal that does not over-attribute threat (the foundation of reappraisal techniques in 2.5.3); calibrated demand that places stress in the productive range of the performance curve (the subject of 2.5.2); and recovery that genuinely completes the cycle (the subject of all of section 2.4).

The biological system is well-engineered for what it evolved to do. The mismatch is not in the system. It is in the conditions under which it now operates, and in the cognitive habits that keep it activated past the point where activation is useful.
