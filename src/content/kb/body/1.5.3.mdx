The previous two articles covered what to do during a strength training session and how to make those sessions progressively harder over time. This article zooms out one level. It is about the architecture of a training week — how many sessions, of what kind, in what configuration — and specifically about the question most users actually face: how do I fit strength training into a life that also includes cardiovascular training, work, family, and everything else?

The honest framing is that there is no single optimal schedule. The right architecture depends on goals, available time, recovery capacity, and what the user actually enjoys enough to do consistently. But several principles are well-supported by the research, and they narrow the space of reasonable options considerably.

## Frequency: how often to train

The question of training frequency for strength is one of the more studied topics in resistance training research. The general findings are clearer than the popular conversation might suggest.

For strength gains specifically, the most important variable is total weekly volume — the total number of working sets per muscle group across the week. Schoenfeld et al. (2016) reviewed the evidence on training frequency and concluded that, when volume is matched, training a muscle group two or three times per week tends to produce slightly better outcomes than training it once. Beyond three sessions per week, the additional benefit is small and may be offset by reduced quality of individual sessions due to accumulating fatigue.

For most users, this maps onto two practical configurations.

**Two full-body sessions per week.** Each session covers most of the major movement patterns. This is the minimum effective dose for general strength gains and works well for users with limited time, users primarily focused on aerobic training, and beginners building the habit. The two sessions should be separated by at least one day, ideally with at least one rest day between them.

**Three full-body sessions per week.** Each session covers the major patterns, but with slightly less volume per session than a two-day-per-week split. This is probably the most productive configuration for most beginners and intermediate trainees. The pattern is typically Monday/Wednesday/Friday or similar, with at least one rest day between sessions.

**Four sessions per week, split structures.** Upper/lower body splits or push/pull/legs splits become useful at higher training volumes, typically for trainees who have been training for at least a year and want to push more volume than full-body sessions can comfortably accommodate. For beginners, splits offer no clear advantage and can produce unbalanced development if the lifter prefers one part of the body to others.

**Five or more sessions per week.** Mostly relevant for advanced lifters with specific performance goals. For general health and fitness, this represents diminishing returns and increasing recovery demands.

The general principle: more frequency works for trainees who can recover from it, and stops working when recovery becomes the limit. Beginners almost universally do better at two or three sessions per week than at four or five — recovery, not motivation, is usually the rate-limiting factor.

## Volume: how much to do per session

Total weekly volume is the variable most strongly associated with strength and muscle adaptations in trained populations. The research literature generally suggests that 10 to 20 working sets per muscle group per week, distributed across two or three sessions, covers most of the productive range for general strength and hypertrophy goals (Schoenfeld et al., 2017).

For a beginner using a full-body program, this typically maps onto something like:

- 3 to 4 sets per major movement pattern per session
- 2 to 3 sessions per week
- 6 to 12 working sets per pattern per week

For exercise selection, this means each session might include three to four major lifts (squat, hinge, push, pull) at three sets each, plus one or two accessory exercises. The total session length lands in the 45 to 60 minute range.

A few notes on volume worth knowing:

**Volume scales with experience.** Beginners need less volume than intermediate or advanced trainees to produce adaptation. Twelve total weekly sets per major pattern can be plenty for a novice; an advanced trainee might need substantially more to continue progressing.

**There is an upper limit.** Volume that exceeds recovery capacity does not produce more adaptation; it produces accumulated fatigue, increased injury risk, and eventually reduced performance. Most research suggests this upper limit sits around 20 to 25 sets per muscle group per week for most trained individuals. Above this, returns diminish sharply.

**Quality matters more than quantity.** Twelve high-quality sets, performed with focus, good technique, and appropriate effort, produce more adaptation than twenty mediocre sets. This is one of the reasons time-strapped users can do well with relatively modest volumes — the quality of the work compensates for less of it.

## Combining strength training with cardio

A common practical question is how to fit aerobic work and strength training together in the same week. The short answer is: easily, with attention to a few specific concerns.

Most recreational adults can train both modalities in the same week without significant interference. Mixed-modality training — sometimes called "concurrent training" — produces broader adaptations than either modality alone: cardiovascular fitness, muscular strength, body composition improvements, and metabolic health. Wilson et al. (2012) reviewed dozens of studies on concurrent training and concluded that for most populations, the combined training stimulus produces additive benefits across most outcomes.

The specific concern in the research literature is the "interference effect" — the observation that very high-volume aerobic training can blunt strength and muscle gains from concurrent resistance training. The effect is real but is most pronounced at training volumes higher than most recreational users encounter. For users training two to three sessions of each modality per week, the interference is small enough to be a practical non-issue.

A few practical guidelines for combining modalities:

**Separate hard sessions of different modalities.** A hard run the day before a heavy lower-body lift compromises the lift. A heavy lower-body lift the day before a hard run compromises the run. Where possible, schedule hard sessions of each modality on different days, with easy or rest days between them.

**If both must be done in one day, prioritise the one that matters most for your goal.** Generally, perform the higher-priority modality first. For a user whose primary goal is strength, lift first and run later. For a user whose primary goal is endurance, run first and lift later. The first session benefits from full energy stores and undamaged muscle; the second session is performed at a deficit.

**Separate same-day sessions by at least six hours where possible.** This allows partial recovery between sessions and reduces the magnitude of the interference effect (Babcock et al., 2012).

**Lower-body strength and lower-body endurance interfere more than upper-body strength and lower-body endurance.** A heavy squat session and a hard run on consecutive days will produce more recovery cost than a heavy bench press session and a hard run. When scheduling, considering the muscular overlap between sessions matters.

**Easy aerobic work after strength training is generally fine.** Twenty to thirty minutes of easy walking or cycling after a strength session does not meaningfully compromise strength gains and can support recovery. Hard cardio after a hard lift is a different matter — both sessions tend to be compromised.

A reasonable weekly template that combines both modalities productively might look like:

- Monday: Full-body strength
- Tuesday: Moderate-intensity aerobic (30-60 min)
- Wednesday: Full-body strength
- Thursday: Easy walking or rest
- Friday: Full-body strength or HIIT
- Saturday: Long easy aerobic
- Sunday: Rest

This produces three strength sessions, two to three aerobic sessions, and adequate recovery, while comfortably hitting the 150-minute weekly aerobic recommendation and the two-to-three weekly strength recommendation.

For users with less time, a minimum effective configuration might be:

- Monday: Full-body strength
- Wednesday: Moderate-intensity aerobic or HIIT
- Friday: Full-body strength
- Plus daily walking and an active weekend day

Both configurations are productive. The right one depends on time, preference, and what the user can sustain.

## Home versus gym training

The choice of where to train is largely practical rather than physiological. Both can produce excellent results.

**Home training advantages.** No commute, no waiting for equipment, no scheduling around facility hours, low ongoing cost after initial setup, and complete privacy. Particularly suited to users with consistent at-home schedules, parents of small children, and people who find gym environments stressful.

**Home training challenges.** Limited equipment range constrains progressive overload over the long term. Most home setups eventually require either expanding equipment (more weights, more accessories) or accepting a ceiling on certain lifts. Bodyweight-only setups have higher ceilings than commonly assumed but eventually run out of progression for major lower-body lifts.

**Gym advantages.** Wider equipment range allows more sophisticated programming and continued progression for advanced lifters. Heavier loads safely accessible (with proper safety equipment and, for some lifts, a spotter). The social environment can be motivating for some users.

**Gym challenges.** Time cost of commuting, social friction, scheduling around facility hours, ongoing membership costs, and equipment availability during peak hours.

The decision should be made primarily on what the user will actually use consistently. A home gym that goes unused is worse than no equipment at all. A gym membership that produces three sessions per week is more valuable than home equipment that produces two sessions per month.

A reasonable middle path for many users is a modest home setup — adjustable dumbbells, a bench, a pull-up bar, perhaps resistance bands — supplemented by occasional gym visits when more sophisticated work is desired. This combines the convenience of home training with access to heavier loads when needed.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

The architecture of a training week determines how well the user's training schedule actually delivers on the Body metrics over time.

**Exercise frequency (0.20 of the Body score).** This is the metric most directly affected by programming choices. The user who can sustainably train four times a week (two strength, two aerobic) will accumulate higher exercise frequency over months than the user who attempts six sessions and burns out in three weeks. Programming for sustainability matters.

**HRV (0.25) and RHR (0.15).** The autonomic markers respond to total training stress and recovery balance. Programming that respects recovery — with rest days between hard sessions, adequate sleep, and sensible weekly volume — maintains or improves these markers. Programming that produces chronic accumulated fatigue degrades them. Many users see this directly when comparing weeks of moderate, well-recovered training to weeks of overreaching.

**Active calories (0.10) and steps (0.05).** Programming that combines structured training with daily movement (Section 1.3) tends to outperform programming that concentrates all activity in a few hard sessions. The user who walks daily and trains four times a week typically accumulates more total active calories than the user who hits intense workouts five times a week but sits the rest of the time.

**Sleep quality (0.25).** Training schedules that involve hard sessions late in the evening can interfere with sleep. Where possible, scheduling intense work earlier in the day improves sleep quality and the recovery that depends on it. This is a small intervention with consistent effects.

The general principle for using Dolphin's metrics to inform programming: the metrics that run on weekly rolling averages are telling you whether your training architecture is working. Persistent declines in HRV or sleep quality, combined with rising RHR, are signals that the current schedule is producing more stress than the body is recovering from. The right response is usually not more training but better recovery — a deload week, an extra rest day, or a reduction in weekly volume — until the metrics stabilise.
