The previous article was about the heart and what wearables tell you about it. This one is about the training that builds the heart up. It covers what aerobic training actually is at the cellular level, why it remains the single most evidence-rich form of exercise for long-term health, and how much of it you need.

The headline finding to know going in: more aerobic activity is associated with better outcomes across nearly every health metric researchers have measured, but the dose-response relationship flattens substantially after surprisingly modest amounts. The largest gains, in absolute terms, come from getting from no aerobic activity to some — not from going from "some" to "a lot." For most people, this is good news. The bar is lower than fitness culture sometimes implies.

## What aerobic training actually does

"Aerobic" means "with oxygen." Aerobic training refers to sustained activity at intensities low enough that your body can supply the working muscles with oxygen continuously, allowing them to produce energy through oxidative metabolism. The hallmark of aerobic effort is that you can sustain it for extended periods — minutes to hours — without producing the burn or breathlessness that comes from pushing past your aerobic capacity.

The adaptations to aerobic training happen at multiple biological levels. At the muscle, mitochondria — the cellular structures that produce energy aerobically — increase in number and density, particularly in the muscle fibres being trained. The capillary network supplying those muscles expands, improving oxygen delivery. The enzymes that drive fat and carbohydrate oxidation upregulate. Holloszy's foundational work in the 1960s and 1970s established this picture, and it has been refined but not fundamentally changed in the decades since (Holloszy & Coyle, 1984).

At the cardiovascular system, the heart's left ventricle expands in capacity, allowing more blood to be ejected per beat. The autonomic nervous system shifts toward parasympathetic dominance at rest — the adaptation that lowers resting heart rate and raises heart rate variability, as covered in 1.4.1. Endothelial function improves throughout the body, reducing vascular stiffness and improving the responsiveness of arteries to demand (Joyner & Green, 2009).

At the metabolic level, insulin sensitivity improves substantially. The body becomes better at using fat as a fuel during low-to-moderate intensity activity, sparing carbohydrate stores. Mitochondrial efficiency means more energy is produced per unit of oxygen consumed.

These adaptations together are what produce the well-known health outcomes. Lee et al. (2014) reviewed the literature and concluded that even modest amounts of aerobic activity reduce all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and several cancers, with effects that persist after adjustment for body weight and other lifestyle factors. The Cooper Institute's longitudinal data, summarised by Blair et al. across multiple papers, has shown that cardiorespiratory fitness — the trainable result of aerobic work — is one of the strongest single predictors of long-term mortality, often stronger than blood pressure, cholesterol, or smoking status individually.

The unifying principle is that aerobic training builds the body's capacity to do work efficiently, and that this capacity translates almost directly into healthspan. People with more of it live longer and live better.

## Intensity matters, but probably less than you think

A persistent debate in the popular fitness conversation is about whether aerobic training needs to be hard to be useful. The answer, on the evidence, is no — but it depends on what you are trying to achieve.

Aerobic intensities are usually classified into zones based on heart rate, perceived effort, or lactate response. The simplest practical taxonomy:

**Zone 2 / easy aerobic.** A pace at which you could comfortably hold a conversation. Heart rate is typically 60 to 70% of maximum. The work feels sustainable; you are not breathing hard. This is the foundation of endurance training and is where the largest mitochondrial adaptations happen.

**Zone 3 / moderate.** A pace at which you can speak in short sentences but not hold a flowing conversation. Heart rate is 70 to 80% of maximum. Common pace for moderate runs and rides; productive but more fatiguing.

**Zone 4 / threshold.** A pace at the edge of what you can sustain for around an hour. Heart rate 80 to 90% of maximum. This is the "tempo" intensity: hard enough that talking is difficult, sustained enough to drive specific adaptations.

**Zone 5 / VO₂max and above.** All-out efforts of seconds to a few minutes, covered in 1.4.3.

For general health, the largest body of evidence supports that moderate-intensity activity — Zone 2 to low Zone 3 — for most of your aerobic training produces nearly all the available benefit. The widely-cited public health guideline, originating with the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services and adopted by the WHO and most national bodies, recommends 150 minutes per week of moderate aerobic activity *or* 75 minutes per week of vigorous activity for adults (Piercy et al., 2018). These thresholds are derived from large prospective studies and represent roughly the point at which the dose-response curve flattens substantially.

A few caveats are worth noting.

**Higher intensities produce some additional adaptations.** Very high-intensity work (Zone 4 and above) drives larger increases in VO₂max — the upper ceiling of aerobic capacity — than moderate work alone. For people specifically training for performance, mixing moderate and high-intensity work produces better results than either alone. Section 1.4.3 covers this in detail.

**More is generally better, with diminishing returns.** Lee and colleagues (2014) found that mortality reductions continued past the 150-minute threshold but at a much slower rate. Going from 0 to 150 minutes per week is associated with substantial mortality reductions; going from 150 to 300 minutes adds further but smaller reductions; beyond about 300 minutes, the curve flattens further still.

**Excessive volumes may produce different trade-offs.** A small literature on extreme endurance training (multiple decades of marathon-distance training, for example) suggests that very high lifetime volumes may carry their own cardiovascular risks — particularly atrial fibrillation and certain forms of cardiac remodelling (O'Keefe et al., 2012). The relevant population here is small and unusual; for the typical recreational user, this is not a practical concern.

The honest summary is that for most people, the question is not whether their aerobic training is intense enough but whether they are doing any at all. The bigger lever for most users is consistency at moderate intensity, not hitting harder zones.

## How much, how often, and what kinds

The 150-minute threshold maps onto several practical configurations. The training literature on what produces good aerobic adaptation tends to converge on a few patterns.

**Frequency.** Three to five sessions per week is the range where most adaptation happens. Going below three reduces the cumulative training stimulus enough to slow adaptation; going above five for moderate-intensity work tends not to produce proportional additional benefit and may interfere with recovery.

**Duration.** Sessions of 30 to 60 minutes are the conventional range for moderate-intensity aerobic training. Shorter sessions still produce benefit, especially when combined with the movement-snack approach covered in 1.3.3. Longer sessions produce additional adaptations specific to endurance performance — fat oxidation capacity, glycogen storage, mental toughness — but for general health these are not necessary.

**Mode.** The mode matters less than the cumulative time and intensity. Walking, jogging, running, cycling, swimming, rowing, hiking, dancing, and most racquet sports all produce aerobic adaptation when sustained at appropriate intensities. The best mode is generally the one you will actually do consistently. People who hate running will not run consistently, regardless of how good running is for them.

**Progression.** The body adapts to any given training stimulus over weeks and months, after which further adaptation requires increased load — longer sessions, higher intensities, more frequent sessions, or some combination. This is the principle of progressive overload, covered more extensively in Section 1.5 in the context of strength training but applying equally to cardiovascular training.

A reasonable starting protocol for an untrained adult: three sessions per week of 30 minutes of moderate-intensity work, building over six to twelve weeks to four sessions of 40 to 60 minutes. By the end of this progression, the user has comfortably exceeded the 150-minute weekly threshold and built the foundation on which higher-intensity work or sport-specific training can sit.

## Combining cardio with strength

A common question is how aerobic training fits with resistance training. The short answer is: easily, most of the time, with one specific concern worth knowing.

Most recreational adults can train both modalities concurrently without issue. Mixed training — sometimes called "concurrent training" in the research literature — produces broad adaptations: improvements in cardiovascular fitness, muscular strength, body composition, and metabolic health, often more comprehensively than either modality alone (Wilson et al., 2012).

The specific concern is what is called the "interference effect": evidence that very high-volume aerobic training can blunt strength and muscle-mass adaptations from resistance training, when both are pursued at high intensity simultaneously. The effect is real but most relevant for athletes specifically optimising for either pure strength or pure endurance. For users training for general fitness or health, with moderate volumes of both, the interference is small enough to be a non-issue.

A few practical principles help when combining the two:

- Schedule hard sessions of either type with adequate recovery between them. A hard run the day before a heavy lower-body lift will tend to compromise the lift.
- If forced to do both in one day, separate them by at least six hours where possible. Where not possible, do the priority modality first.
- Volume balance matters. Someone training six days a week split evenly between cardio and strength will see different adaptations than someone training six days a week with one cardio session and five strength sessions.

This is covered in more practical detail in Section 1.5.3, on programming.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

Aerobic training is the most direct lever for moving the Body score upward, because it touches almost every metric the score tracks.

**Heart rate variability (0.25).** Sustained aerobic training over weeks to months raises HRV. The adaptation is mediated through improved parasympathetic tone — exactly the autonomic shift covered in 1.4.1. The effect is largest in people starting from low baseline fitness; trained individuals see smaller but still meaningful improvements with continued work.

**Resting heart rate (0.15).** Aerobic training is the single most reliable intervention for lowering RHR. The effect is structural (a stronger, larger left ventricle) and autonomic (greater parasympathetic dominance at rest). RHR drops typically appear within four to eight weeks of consistent training and continue to develop over months.

**Exercise frequency (0.20).** Aerobic sessions count directly toward this metric in most Dolphin-supported wearable integrations. Three to five aerobic sessions per week comfortably places the user in the productive range.

**Active calories (0.10).** Aerobic training, by definition, expends calories. Steady-state aerobic sessions are particularly efficient at this — moderate-intensity work for 30 to 60 minutes produces substantial caloric expenditure with relatively modest physiological cost.

**Sleep quality (0.25).** Regular aerobic training is associated with improved sleep quality across multiple studies (Kredlow et al., 2015), particularly when sessions are scheduled earlier in the day. Hard sessions performed too late in the evening can have the opposite effect; this is covered in Section 1.7.

The integration across metrics is what makes aerobic training so high-leverage in the Body pillar. A single intervention — going from sedentary to consistent moderate aerobic activity — moves five of the six Body metrics upward simultaneously. There is no other single change in this entire library that has comparable scope.
