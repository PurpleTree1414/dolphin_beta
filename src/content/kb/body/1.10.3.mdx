The previous article was about reading your wearable data. This one is about reading something the wearable cannot see: the subjective signals your body produces about its own state. The same body that registers as a number on a screen is also registering, internally, as energy, mood, recovery, motivation, and the dozens of small physical and psychological cues that fill an ordinary day. Both data streams matter. Neither is complete on its own.

The framing throughout is that the most useful approach combines the two. Wearable data is precise but limited — it captures certain physiological measures well and misses everything else. Subjective signals are imprecise but comprehensive — they integrate everything the body is experiencing, including the things the device cannot detect. Users who learn to read both, and to investigate when they disagree, make better decisions about training, recovery, and life adjustments than users who rely on either alone.

This article also covers when to actually change something — what the body's signals are telling you to adjust, and what the appropriate response looks like. Reading signals is only useful if it informs decisions.

## What subjective signals can tell you

The body produces many signals continuously. Most are subtle enough that most people do not consciously notice them. Some of the most informative ones, organised loosely by domain:

**Energy quality.** Not just the quantity of energy (tired vs. energetic), but the *kind*. Steady, sustained energy that supports focused work and engagement is different from the wired, jittery energy that comes from caffeine or stress. Different from the heavy, foggy energy of insufficient sleep. Different from the hollow energy of chronic underfueling. Each of these has a distinct subjective texture, and the differences are informative about what is actually happening in the body.

**Sleep that does or does not refresh.** A user can sleep 8 hours and wake feeling restored, or sleep 8 hours and wake feeling like the night did not happen. The difference is sleep architecture — covered in detail in Section 1.7 — but the subjective experience is the most directly accessible signal of it. Tracked over time, the sense of how restorative sleep is becomes a useful indicator.

**Mood and emotional reactivity.** How emotionally responsive are you to ordinary events? A normal range of response — frustration in frustrating moments, satisfaction in good ones, sadness when sad things happen — is the substrate of healthy daily function. Extreme reactivity, where small events produce disproportionate responses, often signals accumulated physiological stress or sleep debt. Flat reactivity, where nothing seems to produce strong response, sometimes signals depression, burnout, or chronic overreaching.

**Motivation and engagement.** The energy you bring to things you usually find engaging. A drop in motivation for activities that normally produce satisfaction — training, work, social connection — is one of the early subjective signals of accumulated burden, often appearing before the wearable data fully reflects it.

**Hunger and appetite signals.** Are you hungry when you should be? Satisfied when you have eaten? Or are these signals erratic, suppressed, or confused? Disrupted hunger signalling often accompanies sleep loss, stress, or undereating, and the disruption is informative.

**Physical sensations during exertion.** Does training feel sustainable, or does it feel like a struggle? Is recovery between intervals adequate, or are you starting each interval still recovering from the last? These signals provide real-time information about training stress that the data only catches afterward.

**Cognitive function.** Is your thinking sharp, or are you experiencing brain fog? Are you finding words easily, or struggling to articulate? Are you making more typos and small errors than usual? These are signals about brain function that often track with overall physiological state.

**Body sensations.** Tightness in the shoulders. Tension in the jaw. Subtle aches that come and go. These are partially trained signals — different users notice different things — but for users who develop interoceptive awareness (covered in 1.1.1), the body is producing signals constantly.

The unifying picture is that subjective signals are diverse, informative, and largely free. They cost nothing but attention. The challenge is that modern life trains us to ignore most of them, prioritising productivity over awareness of internal state.

## When the data and the body agree

In many cases, the wearable data and the subjective signals tell the same story. This is the easy case to interpret.

**Both showing recovery.** HRV stable or rising, RHR stable or falling, sleep quality good — and you feel well-recovered, energetic, motivated. Confidence in interpretation is high. The body is in good shape; continue what is working.

**Both showing accumulated stress.** Declining HRV, rising RHR, declining sleep quality — and you feel tired, irritable, less motivated, less recovered between training sessions. Confidence is high. The body is signalling that stress is accumulating; the right response is more recovery.

**Both showing illness onset.** Sharp HRV drop, sharp RHR rise — and you feel slightly off, scratchy throat, mild fatigue. Confidence is high. The body is fighting something; reduce demands and let immunity work.

These cases are mostly straightforward. Either trust both and continue, or trust both and adjust. The interpretation is not difficult.

## When the data and the body disagree

The interesting cases — the ones that teach you the most about how to read signals — are when the wearable data and your subjective experience disagree. Several patterns are worth knowing.

**Data looks bad, you feel fine.** HRV is below baseline, RHR is up, sleep score dropped — but subjectively you feel reasonable. Several explanations are possible:

- The wearable is detecting something you have not yet consciously felt. Common in the early stages of accumulated stress or impending illness. Trust the data; expect the subjective state to catch up within a day or two.
- A specific transient event affected the metrics without producing sustained subjective effects. Late dinner, glass of wine the night before, hot bedroom, particular movement during the night that the device misread. If a single day, and the trend is otherwise stable, the data is noise.
- Hardware issue. Strap displaced overnight, sensor problem, integration glitch. Worth checking the data quality before drawing inferences.

The decision rule: if the pattern persists for 2-3 days with consistent direction, trust the data. If it is one anomalous day, treat it as likely noise. If it conflicts with how you genuinely feel for over a week, something is going on that warrants more attention.

**Data looks fine, you feel bad.** Metrics are within normal range — but subjectively you feel tired, low-energy, unmotivated, or generally off. Possible explanations:

- The wearable cannot detect what is bothering you. Mental fatigue from cognitive work, emotional stress that has not yet manifested physiologically, mild dehydration, suboptimal nutrition, or low-grade illness in early stages all can produce subjective effects without yet showing up in the autonomic markers.
- Something the wearable does not measure. Joint pain, digestive issues, hormonal cycle effects in women, seasonal mood changes. These all affect subjective wellbeing without necessarily showing up in HRV or RHR.
- Adjustment to a new training stimulus. The body is adapting and the discomfort is part of the process; the metrics may not yet reflect the underlying load.

The decision rule: trust your subjective experience. If you feel bad, you feel bad — even if the wearable says everything is fine. The right response is similar to the data-says-bad case: rest, recover, address what you can identify, and watch for the data to catch up if there is something to catch.

**Conflicting trends over time.** Metrics gradually improving but subjective state declining (or vice versa). This is rarer but informative. Sometimes the body is recovering structurally while the user is psychologically depleted by something external. Sometimes the user feels great because they are adapting to a stress that is genuinely accumulating in the autonomic markers.

The decision rule: when conflicting trends persist for weeks, look beyond the immediate physiological state. Stressors, life events, motivational shifts, or relationship changes may be at play. The metrics describe the body; the subjective state often reflects more than just the body.

## What it means to read your signals well

Reading signals well is not a single skill. It is a collection of habits and capacities that develop with practice.

**Daily check-ins, briefly.** Spending 30 seconds in the morning attending to how you feel — energy, mood, body sensations, motivation — produces information that adds up over time. Done consistently for weeks, you start to notice patterns: "I always feel like this after a Tuesday HIIT session," "Sleep that ends naturally feels different from sleep ended by alarm," "Coffee after 2 p.m. produces this specific pattern of energy followed by crash."

**Tracking subjective signals alongside data.** A simple morning log — energy 1-10, mood 1-10, sleep quality 1-10, soreness 1-10 — adds subjective dimensions to the wearable data. Over weeks, patterns emerge: which days produce good subjective state, which produce bad, and how those correlate with the wearable measures and with what you did the day before.

**Reading transitions.** Often the most informative signals come at transitions — waking, the moments before a training session, the hour after a meal, the wind-down before sleep. The body produces particularly clear signals during these state shifts, and noticing what they tell you is more useful than constantly monitoring during steady states.

**Accepting noise.** Some days you will feel bad for no clear reason. Some days the data will look strange for no clear reason. Not every signal needs to be interpreted, and not every fluctuation needs a response. The skill is partly knowing which signals to take seriously and which to let pass.

**Avoiding rigid interpretations.** A pattern that looks like overreaching one week might look like normal training adaptation the next, and the difference is context — total life stress, sleep, nutrition, season, and dozens of other variables that the metrics do not fully capture. Reading signals well includes holding interpretations loosely.

## When to adjust: the practical questions

Reading signals is most useful when it informs concrete decisions. Several patterns and the appropriate responses are worth knowing.

**Single bad day, otherwise stable trends.** Probably noise. No adjustment needed. Train as planned, sleep well, expect things to look more normal tomorrow.

**Two to three consecutive bad days, no obvious cause.** Could be the start of something — accumulated stress, illness onset, recovery debt from previous demanding period. Reasonable response: reduce training intensity by 20-30%, prioritise sleep, watch for the pattern to either resolve or intensify.

**A week or more of clear deterioration patterns.** Time for more substantial adjustment. Reduce training volume by 30-50% for a deload week (covered in 1.6.1). Sleep aggressively. Consider whether life stressors can be addressed. Watch for normalisation; if it does not occur within 1-2 weeks of reduced training, look harder at what else might be driving the pattern.

**Persistent issues despite reduced training.** Time to consider whether something other than training is driving the picture. Stress, sleep environment, nutrition, illness, hormonal changes. Possibly time for clinical evaluation if the pattern is unusual or sustained.

**Sudden, severe changes.** Sharp HRV drop, sharp RHR rise, or sharp sleep quality decline that appears abruptly. Often the signature of acute illness onset, severe acute stress, or a specific event (hard travel, significant alcohol). Usually self-limiting if the trigger passes; reduce demands and let recovery happen.

**Subjective wellbeing declining despite stable data.** Worth investigating non-physiological dimensions. Mental fatigue, emotional load, life direction issues, relationship stress. The Mind and Lifestyle pillars cover these dimensions in detail.

The principle running through all of these is asymmetric response: when in doubt, more recovery is rarely a bad choice and frequently the right one. The cost of an unnecessary easy week is small. The cost of pushing through the early signs of overreaching, illness, or burnout is often substantial. Most users err on the side of doing too much; very few err on the side of doing too little.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

The Flow Score is a tool for making the kinds of decisions this article describes. Its design — 7-day rolling averages, multi-metric integration, persistent trends — is built to surface the patterns that matter and dampen the noise that does not.

Reading the Flow Score well means:

- Looking at trends rather than daily values
- Noticing when multiple metrics shift together (the most reliable signal) versus when only one moves
- Treating significant deviations as questions rather than verdicts
- Pairing the data with subjective check-ins for fuller information
- Acting on patterns when they are clear rather than waiting for unambiguous signals

Reading the Flow Score badly means:

- Reacting to single-day fluctuations as if they were significant
- Optimising the metric directly through tactics rather than supporting the underlying physiology
- Ignoring the data when it conflicts with planned activities
- Becoming anxious about every fluctuation
- Treating the data as a verdict on your worth or progress rather than information about current state

The metric is a tool. Used well, it surfaces information that helps you adjust. Used badly, it produces anxiety and distorted decisions. The skill of using it well is not separate from the skill of self-awareness; the two reinforce each other.
