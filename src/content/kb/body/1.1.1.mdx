You were probably taught you have five senses — sight, sound, touch, taste, smell. All of them point outward at the world. What is missing from that list is the entire category of sensing your body does about *itself*: whether it is tired, hungry, tense, recovered, hurting, or healing. This inward sense is what makes every wearable metric meaningful in real time. Without it, the numbers on a screen are just numbers.

This article is about that inward sense — what it is, why it quietly underwrites almost every other topic in this library, and how to read two of its most useful signals: the difference between hunger and its absence, and the far more consequential difference between pain and discomfort. It is placed first in the Body KB on purpose. Everything else assumes it.

## The three senses of the body

Neuroscience divides body sensing into three categories. The distinction comes from Charles Sherrington's work in the early twentieth century (Sherrington, 1906) and still structures modern research.

**Exteroception** is sensing the outside world — the five senses you already know.

**Proprioception** is sensing where your body is and how it is moving in space. It is what lets you touch your nose with your eyes closed, stay balanced on one leg, or catch a falling glass before you have consciously registered it falling. The signals come from receptors in your muscles (which detect stretch and tension) and joints (which detect position). Proske and Gandevia (2012) give the full review of the physiology.

**Interoception** is sensing the internal state of your body — heart rate, breathing, gut activity, temperature, hunger, thirst, fatigue, muscular tension, the vague but important sense of *how you feel overall*. Interoceptive signals travel mostly through the vagus nerve and a set of small, slow-conducting nerve fibres into the brainstem and insular cortex, where they are integrated into your conscious (and often pre-conscious) awareness of your body's state.

Of the three, interoception is both the most important for long-term health and the most neglected in typical fitness culture. It is what this research library leans on most.

## Why interoception underwrites so much

For a long time, interoception was treated as a background function — something your body does automatically, like blinking. The last two decades of neuroscience have complicated that picture considerably. Beginning with A. D. Craig's 2002 review in *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, and extended by researchers including Lisa Feldman Barrett and Hugo Critchley, a stronger claim has emerged: interoception may be the foundation of conscious feeling itself.

The idea goes like this. Emotions are not free-floating mental events that happen somewhere above the neck. They are interpretations your brain constructs from bodily states. The feeling of anxiety *is* your racing heart, tight chest, and shallow breathing, bound together and given a name. Antonio Damasio called these "somatic markers" (1994). Barrett's theory of constructed emotion (2017) pushes further: without interoception, there are no emotions at all — only undifferentiated arousal. This is not purely theoretical. In conditions where interoception is impaired — alexithymia (a reduced ability to identify one's own emotions), certain eating disorders, specific types of stroke — people tend to report flatter, less distinct emotional lives.

Four practical domains rest directly on this capacity:

**Knowing how you actually feel.** The Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness, or MAIA, is a validated questionnaire developed by Mehling and colleagues (2012) that measures eight facets of interoception, including whether you *notice* bodily sensations, whether you *trust* them, and whether you can *regulate* your attention toward them. Higher MAIA scores correlate with better emotional regulation and less rumination. Mindfulness-based training raises these scores (Farb et al., 2015), though the effects accumulate slowly rather than transform things overnight.

**Eating without rigid rules.** The hormones that regulate hunger — ghrelin, leptin, cholecystokinin, peptide YY — only work if you can feel them. Herbert and Pollatos (2012) found that people with higher interoceptive sensitivity regulate their food intake more effectively and maintain more stable weight over time. Chronic dieting appears to dull interoception, which is one reason why rigid food rules so often unravel.

**Catching problems before your wearable does.** Every metric in this library — heart rate variability, resting heart rate, sleep quality — is a lagging indicator of something interoception can tell you today. Trained athletes often sense impending overreaching a week or two before their HRV drops. The joint European and American consensus statement on overtraining (Meeusen et al., 2013) lists persistent, disproportionate fatigue as an early warning sign — but only if you notice it.

**Regulating stress in the moment.** Your nervous system does not announce when it has drifted into chronic sympathetic activation. Interoception is often the only way to catch that drift before it becomes your default. This is also why slow, diaphragmatic breathing works so well as a stress intervention: it gives interoception something detailed and slow to attend to, which crowds out the diffuse hum of arousal.

## Pain and discomfort are not the same signal

If you take one thing from this article, let it be this distinction.

Most overuse injuries in recreational athletes happen when someone reads a pain signal as discomfort and trains through it. A smaller but real group does the opposite — treats normal training discomfort as pain, avoids movement, and eventually deconditions themselves into genuine fragility. Neither group is careless. Both are misreading interoceptive signals they were never taught to separate.

The International Association for the Study of Pain updated its official definition in 2020: pain is "an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage" (Raja et al., 2020). Notice carefully what that definition does *not* say. It does not say pain equals tissue damage. Pain is a construction the brain makes, informed by nociception (the firing of specialized pain-related nerve fibres) but also by attention, memory, fear, belief, and context. This is why a torn ligament can go unnoticed in the middle of a match and resurface only after the adrenaline clears. It is also why chronic pain remains real even when the original injury has long healed.

Discomfort is a different category entirely. The burn of lactate accumulation. The ache of an unfamiliar movement. The breathless pressure of a hard interval. The deep soreness that shows up a day or two after novel training. These are signals of effort, not damage. Proske and Morgan (2001), and Cheung and colleagues (2003), traced delayed-onset muscle soreness to microtrauma in muscle fibres and the inflammatory repair process that follows. That inflammation is how the body rebuilds itself stronger. It is adaptive discomfort — not a warning to stop.

Two rough rules can separate them in real time:

- **Adaptive discomfort** tends to be diffuse, symmetrical (both sides where both sides worked), proportional to how hard you trained, and resolves within 24 to 72 hours. It often *improves* as you warm up and start moving.
- **Pain worth respecting** tends to be sharp or localized, persists or worsens as you keep moving, often appears after a specific moment you can identify, and may come with swelling, altered movement, or involuntary guarding.

Teaching people this distinction explicitly — rather than leaving them to guess — reduces both over-cautious avoidance and push-through-it behaviour (Bunzli et al., 2017; O'Sullivan et al., 2018). The middle path requires accurate interoception.

## What sharpens and what dulls body awareness

Interoception is not fixed. It is trainable, though not quickly.

What dulls it: chronic stress, probably through reduced insular sensitivity over time. Alcohol, which distorts both the signals and their interpretation. Sleep deprivation, which degrades the whole system's signal-to-noise. And, paradoxically, obsessive external tracking. When you train yourself to check a number on a screen instead of checking in with yourself, the internal channel gradually atrophies.

What sharpens it: deliberate attention practices — body scans, mindfulness meditation, breath awareness, yoga, tai chi. The evidence base (Farb et al., 2015) is real but best thought of as modest and cumulative rather than dramatic. More directly useful in daily life: practising interoception in specific contexts. Rate your perceived exertion during a workout. Notice your hunger level *before* you decide what to eat. Scan for tension during work breaks. Every act of checking in makes the next one easier.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

Every other article in this Body library will ask you to read a signal. Heart rate variability tells you something — but only in the context of how you actually feel. An elevated resting heart rate means something different depending on whether you can also sense poor sleep, mounting stress, or an oncoming illness. The entire Recovery section is about reading overtraining signals, which requires being able to notice them.

Dolphin's wearable integrations average data over a seven-day rolling window. That smoothing protects your Flow Score from day-to-day noise, but it also means the data is, by design, about a week behind what your body already knows. Users with strong interoception tend to catch problems before the numbers do. That is not a flaw in the metrics — it is the right division of labour between a machine and a body.

Body awareness is the meta-skill that makes every other Body metric legible. It is also what you will rely on most when you are travelling, sick, or off your usual routine — when the data looks wrong but something actually is wrong. Treat it as foundational, because it is.
