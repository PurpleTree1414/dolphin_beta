The previous article was about the importance of moving. This one is about the opposite — what happens when you do not move, particularly when you sit still for long periods, and what to do about the desk-based environment most modern work happens in.

The shift in research thinking on sedentary behaviour over the past 15 years has been substantial. For most of the twentieth century, the dominant model was simple: physical activity was good, and lack of physical activity was the absence of that good. The current evidence is more nuanced. Sitting is not just the absence of activity. It appears to be its own physiological state, with effects on metabolic and cardiovascular health that are partially independent of how much you exercise outside of it.

This has practical consequences. The runner who trains for an hour and then sits at a desk for nine hours is not protected from the harms of sitting just because they exercised. The variable that matters is not just total activity. It is the duration of uninterrupted sedentary time.

## The independent risk of prolonged sitting

The conceptual breakthrough came from work by Marc Hamilton, Genevieve Healy, Neville Owen, and several other researchers in the early 2000s, who began noticing that sedentary time and physical activity were behaving as separate variables in epidemiological data. People who sat for many hours daily showed elevated cardiometabolic risk even when they hit standard physical activity recommendations. The relationship was not simply about total energy expenditure. Something specific about prolonged sitting was producing dysfunction.

The evidence has accumulated steadily.

**All-cause mortality.** A pooled analysis by Ekelund et al. (2016) of more than one million participants found that prolonged sitting was associated with increased mortality, but with an important nuance: the risk was largely attenuated in people who accumulated 60 to 75 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity per day. In other words, plenty of exercise can offset most of the sitting risk — but this is more exercise than most people get, and the protection is incomplete.

**Cardiovascular and metabolic markers.** Healy and colleagues (2008) showed that people whose total sitting time was broken up frequently — even into small movement breaks — had significantly better metabolic markers than people who sat for the same total time in long unbroken blocks. The number of sitting interruptions mattered, not just the total. Subsequent work has consistently replicated this pattern (Dunstan et al., 2012).

**Insulin sensitivity.** Dunstan et al. (2012) ran a controlled experiment in which participants sat for nine hours after eating a meal. Some sat continuously; others stood up for two minutes every twenty minutes; others walked at light intensity for two minutes every twenty minutes. Both interruption groups showed substantially lower postprandial blood glucose and insulin responses than the continuous sitters. The interventions were tiny — two minutes of standing or walking. The metabolic effect was meaningful.

**Vascular function.** Sitting for as little as one to three hours produces measurable impairment in flow-mediated dilation, an indicator of vascular health (Restaino et al., 2015). The impairment reverses with brief movement breaks. The mechanism involves reduced shear stress on blood vessel walls, which the vascular system relies on for normal function.

The picture that emerges is that the human cardiovascular and metabolic systems are calibrated for intermittent movement. Long, uninterrupted stillness — particularly in the hours after eating — produces a stress response of its own kind, distinct from the stress of inactivity in general.

## What "sedentary" actually means

A note on terminology, because the word "sedentary" is used loosely in popular conversation.

In the research literature, sedentary behaviour refers specifically to waking activities performed in a sitting, reclining, or lying posture with low energy expenditure — typically defined as 1.5 metabolic equivalents (METs) or below (Tremblay et al., 2017). Sleeping is not sedentary. Standing is not sedentary, even though it involves little movement. What counts is the combination of low-energy-expenditure body position with wakefulness.

This distinction matters because some interventions targeted at sedentary time work primarily by changing posture (sit-stand desks, for example) without necessarily increasing total movement. The evidence suggests these interventions provide some benefit — standing produces slightly higher energy expenditure and engages postural muscles that sitting does not — but the effects are modest. The larger benefits come from interventions that produce actual movement, not just a different stationary posture.

## The desk environment

For most office workers, the dominant sedentary context is the desk. Several aspects of the desk environment shape how the body experiences eight or nine hours of work daily.

**Chair, screen, and keyboard ergonomics.** The traditional ergonomic guidelines — feet flat on the floor, hips slightly above knees, screen at eye level, elbows at roughly 90 degrees, wrists neutral — remain reasonable starting points but should be understood as ranges rather than fixed positions. The evidence base for specific ergonomic prescriptions is weaker than the confident tone of most ergonomic guidelines suggests (van Niekerk et al., 2012). What matters more than achieving any particular posture is *changing* postures across the day.

**Sit-stand desks.** Adjustable workstations have been studied extensively over the past decade. The evidence shows modest benefits: small reductions in lower back and shoulder discomfort, slight increases in energy expenditure, and improved subjective wellbeing in some studies, but with no consistent improvement in productivity (Karakolis & Callaghan, 2014). They are useful but not transformative. The biggest gains come from using them to alternate postures across the day, not from standing for long blocks (which produces its own discomfort, particularly in the lower back and lower limbs).

**Screen height and eye strain.** Screen positioning that requires sustained neck flexion or extension produces predictable musculoskeletal complaints over time. The general rule is that the top of the screen should sit at roughly eye level when seated upright, with the screen at about arm's length. Laptops, used as the only computer, force a compromise between neck position and wrist position; an external keyboard or monitor stand resolves this for sustained work.

**Keyboard and mouse position.** Repetitive strain conditions affecting the wrist and forearm — the broad category that includes carpal tunnel syndrome — are associated with sustained awkward postures and high-repetition input. Neutral wrist position, frequent breaks, and varying input devices reduce the cumulative load.

The honest summary on ergonomics is that no chair, desk, or workstation will protect you from the health effects of prolonged sitting. Ergonomics reduces specific local complaints — wrist pain, neck stiffness, lower-back ache — without addressing the underlying physiological problem of long uninterrupted stillness. The two interventions that actually move that needle are reducing total sedentary time and breaking it up frequently.

## What works in practice

The behavioural research on sedentary reduction has converged on a few interventions that consistently produce results.

**Movement breaks every 30 to 60 minutes.** A two- to five-minute walk, a flight of stairs, a quick stretch sequence, or simply standing and pacing during a phone call. The total time cost is small — perhaps 15 to 20 minutes across an eight-hour workday — and the metabolic and musculoskeletal benefits are well-supported. Loh et al. (2020) found that breaks of as little as one to two minutes every 30 minutes produced measurable improvement in glucose response.

**Walking meetings.** When a meeting is one-on-one, conducted by phone, or does not require shared screens, taking it walking adds substantial movement to the day at zero time cost. Some research also suggests creative thinking improves during walking compared to sitting (Oppezzo & Schwartz, 2014), though this effect is modest and depends on the type of task.

**Standing for routine activities.** Phone calls, brief reading, video calls that do not require detailed screen interaction. Most people can convert one to two hours of daily sitting to standing without changing their workflow.

**Active commutes.** Where possible, walking or cycling to work substitutes movement for sitting time and is associated with reduced cardiovascular risk independent of leisure-time exercise (Hamer & Chida, 2008).

**Interrupting at home, not just at work.** Most studies of sedentary behaviour focus on workplace sitting, but for many people the home is now the larger sedentary environment — particularly during evenings spent on couches and screens. Building movement breaks into evening routines (standing during ad breaks, walking after dinner, stretching while watching) addresses the largest single block of sitting time many adults experience.

The pattern across these interventions is that small, frequent disruptions of sitting produce larger benefits than longer blocks of more intense activity at fewer points in the day. Two minutes every thirty is more effective for metabolic health than thirty minutes every eight hours.

## Standing is not the answer either

A corrective note before closing. The cultural reaction against sitting has produced an opposite recommendation: stand all day, treat sitting as toxic. This is not what the evidence supports.

Prolonged standing has its own problems. It is associated with lower back pain, varicose veins, and lower-limb fatigue when sustained for many hours daily (Waters & Dick, 2015). The body is not adapted to continuous standing any more than it is to continuous sitting. The pattern that fits the human body best is the one our ancestors lived in — alternating sitting, standing, walking, squatting, and other postures across the day, rarely staying in any one for very long.

The right framing is variety. The best posture, as McGill suggests in the context of spine health, is the next one. A workday spent in the same chair for nine hours is the problem. A workday spent in the same standing position for nine hours is also a problem. A workday that alternates among them, with frequent movement breaks woven through, is the configuration the body's physiology was built for.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

The connection between sedentary behaviour and Body metrics is largely indirect, but real.

Steps and Active Calories are the obvious metrics: people who sit less, by definition, accumulate more of both. The intervention that reduces sitting time is usually the same intervention that raises these metrics — short, frequent movement breaks across the day.

The less obvious connection is to heart rate variability and resting heart rate (which together account for 0.40 of the Body score). Chronic sedentary behaviour appears to negatively affect these markers over time, partly through loss of cardiovascular fitness and partly through the metabolic consequences of long uninterrupted sitting. Even people who exercise regularly show measurable HRV differences based on their sitting time outside of training.

The Lifestyle pillar tracks Work-Life Balance as a metric, and there is a meaningful overlap. The work environment that produces nine hours of uninterrupted sitting is often the same one producing other lifestyle stresses. Interventions that improve daily movement often improve both pillars at once.

Finally, the same logic that applied to nutrition applies here: movement variety is substrate. The training you do in your scheduled workouts builds on a foundation of how you move during the day. A body that has been sedentary for ten hours is a body that responds differently to a 60-minute training session than a body that has been moving variably for ten hours. Both can train. They are not the same.
