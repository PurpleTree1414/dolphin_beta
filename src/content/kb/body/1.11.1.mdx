Most non-traumatic injuries in recreationally active people share a similar origin story. They are not single-event failures. They are the cumulative consequence of repeated suboptimal loading — weeks, months, or years of small errors and slight imbalances that finally exceed what the tissue can tolerate. The runner whose knee fails on a Tuesday morning has usually been running with subtle hip-control problems for years. The lifter whose shoulder finally gives way has usually been pressing with poor scapular control for hundreds of sessions. The desk worker with chronic low-back pain has usually been accumulating posture-related strain for years before any single moment "caused" the problem.

This is, in some ways, good news. It means that injury is largely preventable, not random. The two main levers that prevent it are **load management** — controlling the total demands placed on the body so they do not exceed recovery capacity — and **movement screening** — identifying patterns of movement that are accumulating damage before they produce visible problems. This article covers both, with attention to the practical implementation that fits within the consistency principles that run through the rest of this library.

The framing is that the user who never gets injured trains for decades. The user who repeatedly gets injured trains for months at a time, with months of forced rest in between, and accumulates dramatically less total adaptation across their lifetime. The decisive variable is not how hard you can train when healthy. It is how rarely you have to stop.

## What load management actually means

**Load** in training contexts refers to the total physical demand placed on the body — the sum of intensity, volume, and frequency across all training and life activities. Load management is the deliberate control of this total to keep it within what the body can productively handle.

The fundamental principle was established by exercise scientists working with elite athletes and has since been adapted for general populations. Tim Gabbett's work, in particular, popularised what is sometimes called the **acute:chronic workload ratio** (ACWR) — the ratio of recent training load (typically the past week) to longer-term training load (typically the past 4 weeks). The general finding from Gabbett's research and similar work (Gabbett, 2016) is that injury risk rises sharply when the acute load substantially exceeds the chronic load. In simpler terms: rapid increases in training are dangerous; gradual progression is safe.

The practical implication: the body adapts to whatever load it has been encountering recently. When new demands fall within the range it has prepared for, it handles them well. When new demands substantially exceed that range — a sudden increase of 30% or more in a single week — the tissues, the cardiovascular system, and the recovery infrastructure cannot keep pace, and small breakdowns accumulate.

A useful rule of thumb that has emerged from the literature is the **10% rule**: do not increase any single training variable (volume, distance, intensity, weight) by more than about 10% per week. This is a guideline rather than a strict prescription — some users tolerate larger increases comfortably, others need slower progression — but it captures the principle of gradual, body-paced progression.

A few practical applications:

**Mileage progression in running.** A runner who has been doing 25 km per week for several months can reasonably increase to 27.5 km the following week, then 30 km, then 33 km. A jump from 25 km to 40 km in a single week violates the principle and typically produces overuse injury within weeks.

**Strength progression.** Adding weight gradually (1.25-2.5 kg for upper body, 2.5-5 kg for lower body, per session at most) is consistent with the principle. Adding 10 kg to a previously comfortable lift in a single session is the kind of jump that produces injury.

**Returning from a break.** This is one of the most common contexts where load management gets violated. After 2 weeks off (sickness, holiday, life), the body has detrained slightly. Returning at full previous load on the first session is a substantial relative increase from the recent baseline of zero. The standard recommendation is to return at approximately 70% of pre-break load and progress back to baseline over 2-3 weeks.

**Race preparation.** Athletes preparing for events often increase training in the weeks leading up. Done gradually, this is appropriate. Done rapidly — cramming volume into the final 4 weeks — produces the highest injury rates of any common training pattern.

The principle is conservative by nature. It accepts that progress is slower under careful load management than it would be under maximum loading. The trade-off is that careful load management produces years of consistent training rather than months of training punctuated by months of recovery. Across a decade, the conservative approach produces more total adaptation. The aggressive approach produces more training streaks but also more injuries, and the average over years is worse.

## When the principle does not apply

A few corrective notes, because load management can be over-applied.

**The 10% rule is not a ceiling.** It is a guideline for progression. A user who has plateaued at a load they can handle comfortably is not gaining anything by limiting themselves to 1% increases. The point is not to progress slowly forever — it is to progress at a pace the body can accommodate.

**Total stress matters more than training stress alone.** As covered in 1.8.1, the body integrates training stress with life stress. A 10% training increase during a week of unusually high work or family stress may be too much, even though it would be fine in calmer circumstances. Load management is about total load, not just training load.

**The principle is most relevant in the first weeks of changing patterns.** The body adapts. Once a load pattern is established, the same load can be repeated indefinitely without problem. It is the *change* that creates risk, not the absolute level.

**Some changes are inherently larger than they appear.** Switching from a familiar exercise to an unfamiliar one (different squat variation, different running surface, different shoes, different training environment) can produce substantial new stress even when the obvious metrics — weight, distance, time — are unchanged. The body is encountering unfamiliar demands; the tissues respond accordingly. New training environments are one of the most common contexts for unexpected injury, even in experienced athletes.

## Movement screening: the briefest possible introduction

Beyond load management, the second lever for injury prevention is identifying patterns of movement that accumulate damage even at appropriate loads. This is the territory of **movement screening** — assessment of how the body actually moves through fundamental patterns, with attention to limitations or asymmetries that are likely to produce problems over time.

The most well-known systematic approach is the **Functional Movement Screen (FMS)**, developed by physical therapist Gray Cook and colleagues. The FMS uses seven movements to identify common limitations: deep squat, hurdle step, in-line lunge, shoulder mobility, active straight-leg raise, trunk stability push-up, and rotary stability. Each is scored, and the pattern of scores points to specific limitations the user can address.

The literature on whether FMS scores actually predict injury has been mixed. Bonazza et al. (2017) reviewed dozens of studies and concluded that low FMS scores do correlate with injury risk in athletic populations, though the predictive value is modest and the screening tool is not as deterministic as some advocates claim. The general principle the screen embodies — that the way you move predicts the problems you will develop — is well-supported. The specific tool has limitations.

For users without access to qualified screening, a few self-assessable patterns are worth knowing.

**Squat pattern.** Can you perform a deep, controlled bodyweight squat with heels on the ground, knees tracking over toes, and a neutral spine? Limitations are common — restricted ankle mobility, restricted hip mobility, poor trunk control — and predict knee, lower back, and hip problems over time.

**Hinge pattern.** Can you bend forward from the hips while maintaining a neutral spine, with most movement coming from the hips rather than the lower back? Difficulty here predicts lower back problems, particularly under load.

**Shoulder mobility.** Can you raise your arms overhead with your back flat against a wall, without pain or substantial restriction? Limitations predict shoulder problems and often track with poor posture-related thoracic mobility.

**Single-leg balance.** Can you stand on one leg for 30 seconds without obvious wobbling, and 60 seconds with eyes closed? Single-leg control reflects integrated hip, knee, and ankle stability — and asymmetries between sides predict injury on the weaker side.

**Toe touch.** Can you reach toward your toes from standing, with knees soft, without dramatic limitation? Tightness here is rarely "tight hamstrings" specifically — more often it reflects reduced hip mobility or protective patterns from chronic sitting.

These are not diagnostic tests. They are observations that, when limited, suggest areas worth working on before they become problems. The user who finds limitations in one of these patterns should consider it an early warning rather than a verdict, and incorporate work to address the underlying limitation.

## Common patterns worth addressing

A few movement and mobility limitations recur often enough across recreational populations to be worth specific attention.

**Restricted hip mobility.** Particularly internal rotation and extension. Common in users who sit much of the day. Hip mobility limitations propagate up to the lower back and down to the knees, producing problems in both. Addressing hip mobility through specific drills (hip CARs, 90/90 stretches, lunges with extension, deep squat holds) over weeks to months tends to reduce both hip-specific and adjacent issues.

**Limited ankle dorsiflexion.** The ability to bring the knee forward over the toes while the foot is flat. Limited dorsiflexion changes how the knee tracks during squats, lunges, running, and walking, producing chronic knee strain. Common in users who wear elevated heels or who have had previous ankle injuries. Addressed through specific calf and ankle mobility work, plus deliberate practice of full-range squat and lunge patterns.

**Poor scapular control.** The shoulder blades should move smoothly with the arms — protracting and retracting, rotating up and down — rather than locking in any single position. Common in desk workers and users who train heavily with chest-dominant patterns at the expense of pulling work. Addressed through balanced training (more pulling than most programs include), scapular stability work, and posture awareness.

**Weak posterior chain.** The glutes, hamstrings, and lower back muscles that resist forward bending and produce hip extension. Underdeveloped in sedentary populations and in users whose training emphasises anterior-chain patterns (squats, lunges, presses) at the expense of hinging. Addressed through deadlift variations, hip thrusts, Romanian deadlifts, and similar work.

**Poor deep core stability.** Not abdominal "strength" in the visible-muscle sense, but the deeper trunk stabilisation that protects the spine under load. Inadequate core stability is one of the most common contributors to lower back pain. Addressed through specific stability work — planks, bird dogs, dead bugs, Pallof presses, suitcase carries — rather than through high-rep abdominal flexion work.

The pattern across these is that addressing them requires consistent, low-load work over weeks to months. They are not solved by occasional stretching sessions or individual mobility classes. They are solved by integrating relevant work into ongoing training.

## How to actually apply this

A reasonable practical structure for incorporating load management and movement attention into ongoing training:

**Weekly load awareness.** Rough tracking of weekly training totals — sessions completed, distances run, weights lifted, intense vs. easy time. Not detailed accounting, but enough that significant changes are visible. Wearable data covers some of this; manual tracking covers what wearables miss.

**Conservative progression on new patterns.** When introducing a new training stimulus — a new exercise, a new training method, a return from break — start substantially below capacity and progress over 2-4 weeks rather than jumping to full load.

**Periodic self-screening.** Every few months, performing the basic patterns described above (deep squat, hinge, shoulder mobility, single-leg balance, toe touch) and noting whether they have improved, held steady, or declined. Persistent limitations or declines warrant attention.

**Addressing identified limitations.** Mobility and stability work for areas of weakness, integrated into warm-ups or as standalone short sessions. The most effective work is consistent and low-key — 5-10 minutes per day on relevant patterns is typically more useful than monthly hour-long mobility classes.

**Listening to early signals.** Persistent localised soreness, asymmetric discomfort, or specific patterns of stiffness are worth attending to before they become injuries. The pain-vs-discomfort distinction from 1.1.1 applies: discomfort is often training response; specific localised pain is often the early signal of accumulated load.

The principle running through all of these is that injury prevention is not separate from training. It is integrated into training. Users who treat mobility, stability, and load management as additions they do "if there is time" tend to neglect them. Users who weave them into ongoing practice tend to maintain the consistent capacity that allows them to keep training year after year.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

The metrics Dolphin tracks reflect injury prevention indirectly but consistently.

**Exercise frequency (0.20).** This is the metric most directly affected by injury prevention. Users who avoid the kinds of overuse injuries that interrupt training streaks accumulate dramatically more weeks of consistent training over months and years. The metric rewards consistency, and consistency requires not getting hurt.

**HRV (0.25) and RHR (0.15).** Sustained training without injury produces the cardiovascular adaptations that improve these markers. Repeated training-injury-recovery cycles do not — each forced break partially erodes the adaptations the previous training built.

**Sleep quality (0.25).** Pain and inflammatory recovery from injury degrade sleep quality. Users carrying chronic minor injuries often see their sleep quality affected without consciously connecting the two.

**Active calories (0.10) and steps (0.05).** Injuries reduce daily movement, sometimes for weeks at a time, before any return to normal training is possible. The downstream effects on activity metrics are substantial.

The unifying point is that injury is the largest disruption to consistent practice that exists. A single significant injury can erase 6-12 months of accumulated training adaptation, from which recovery takes months more. The user whose training never gets interrupted by injury accumulates more total adaptation across their life than the user whose training is repeatedly interrupted, regardless of how hard they train when healthy.
