The previous article covered the foundations of healthy breathing — what good breathing looks like at rest and why it matters for the autonomic nervous system. This article moves into something more specific: the trainable qualities of the respiratory system itself. Like the cardiovascular system or the muscular system, the respiratory system can be trained to function better. The training has two main dimensions: improving how well your body tolerates the natural rises in **carbon dioxide** that occur during exertion, and improving the actual strength of the muscles that produce breathing.

Both qualities matter for performance during exercise, for breathing quality at rest, and — interestingly — for autonomic regulation more broadly. A respiratory system that is well-trained does not just perform better. It tends to support better cardiovascular health, better stress recovery, and better sleep.

This is also the article where some of the popular interventions in this space (breath holds, hypoxic training, fancy breathing apps) get evaluated honestly. The science here is real but smaller than the marketing implies — and worth understanding clearly.

## What CO₂ tolerance actually means

The previous article touched on the fact that **carbon dioxide (CO₂)**, not oxygen, is the primary trigger for breathing in most circumstances. When CO₂ rises in your blood — for example, during exercise — receptors in your brainstem detect the change and signal your body to breathe faster and deeper to clear it.

The threshold at which your brainstem responds varies between individuals. Some people have receptors that fire at relatively low CO₂ levels — the brainstem signals "breathe more" almost as soon as CO₂ starts rising. Others have receptors that tolerate higher CO₂ before triggering the same response. This is what is meant by **CO₂ tolerance**: the amount of CO₂ your body can accumulate before the urge to breathe becomes overwhelming.

CO₂ tolerance matters for several reasons.

**It affects breathing patterns at rest.** People with low CO₂ tolerance tend to breathe faster than necessary, even when at rest. Their bodies are responding to CO₂ levels their physiology could comfortably handle if the receptors were less reactive. The result is chronic mild hyperventilation — breathing more than the body's actual metabolic demand. This can produce subtle but persistent symptoms: feeling slightly short of breath, occasional dizziness, anxiety that tracks with breathing patterns, and chronic mild sympathetic activation.

**It affects performance during exercise.** During hard effort, CO₂ rises rapidly. The athlete with higher CO₂ tolerance can sustain harder efforts before being forced to slow down by the urge to breathe. This is one mechanism (among several) by which trained endurance athletes sustain higher workloads than untrained people.

**It affects breath holds and underwater performance.** This is more specialised — relevant for swimmers, freedivers, and certain sports — but worth mentioning because it is where CO₂ tolerance is most directly tested.

**It interacts with anxiety and panic responses.** People with low CO₂ tolerance are more vulnerable to panic attacks and acute anxiety responses, because the same physiological cues that the body interprets as "danger, breathe more" can become entwined with psychological alarm responses. This is part of why breathing exercises are foundational interventions in anxiety treatment.

The good news is that CO₂ tolerance is trainable. The training involves deliberately exposing yourself to slightly elevated CO₂ levels through controlled breath holds and reduced-breathing exercises, allowing the brainstem receptors to gradually become less reactive.

## Training CO₂ tolerance

The standard approach involves gentle, progressive exposure rather than dramatic breath holds. The methods that have the best evidence base come from research on respiratory training in clinical populations and athletes (McConnell, 2013).

**The CO₂ tolerance test.** A common starting point is a simple measurement: sit comfortably, take a normal exhale, and time how long you can comfortably hold your breath without forcing it. The endpoint is when you feel the first definite urge to breathe — not when you cannot hold any longer, but when the urge clearly arrives. This is sometimes called the **BOLT score** (Body Oxygen Level Test), associated with breathing practitioner Patrick McKeown.

For reference: a BOLT score of 25 to 30 seconds is considered reasonable for general health. Below 20 seconds suggests low CO₂ tolerance. Above 40 seconds suggests well-developed tolerance, often seen in trained athletes. Most untrained adults score somewhere between 15 and 25 seconds.

The score itself is less important than its responsiveness to training. Users who do consistent CO₂ tolerance work typically see their score rise over weeks to months, alongside subjective improvements in breathing comfort and exercise capacity.

**Reduced-breathing intervals.** A simple training method involves breathing slightly less than your body's signal demands during low-intensity activity. For example, while walking at an easy pace, taking smaller breaths than feels fully comfortable, allowing CO₂ to rise modestly without producing distress. This is sometimes called "breathing reduction" or "controlled hypoventilation" in the literature.

**Box breathing patterns.** Patterns like 4-4-4-4 (inhale 4 seconds, hold 4 seconds, exhale 4 seconds, hold 4 seconds) introduce brief breath holds that mildly elevate CO₂. The pauses are short enough to be comfortable but long enough to produce gentle adaptation.

**Nasal-only breathing during light exercise.** As discussed in 1.9.1, nasal breathing produces slower air exchange than mouth breathing. During easy aerobic work, deliberately maintaining nasal-only breathing trains both the diaphragm and CO₂ tolerance. The user breathes more comfortably at slightly higher CO₂ levels than they would with mouth breathing.

A few important caveats.

**Breath holds during active exercise are different from breath holds at rest.** Holding the breath during heavy exertion creates substantial cardiovascular stress and is not appropriate as a routine training method for general users. Light breath holds during easy activity, or during seated practice, are the right starting point.

**Training should be gradual and comfortable.** The aim is mild, repeated exposure to slightly elevated CO₂, not breath-hold endurance contests. Users who push aggressively into discomfort can produce sympathetic activation that defeats the purpose of the training. The motto is: gentle, repeated exposure beats heroic single sessions.

**It is not appropriate for everyone.** People with cardiovascular conditions, panic disorder, or acute anxiety should approach CO₂ tolerance training cautiously and ideally under qualified guidance. The training works through controlled exposure to discomfort, which can be problematic in some clinical situations.

The available evidence on CO₂ tolerance training in athletes and general populations supports modest improvements in exercise capacity and breathing comfort with consistent practice (Romer & McConnell, 2003). The effects are real but not transformative — comparable to other forms of foundational fitness work that compound over months.

## Respiratory muscle strength

Beyond CO₂ tolerance, the actual muscles that produce breathing can themselves be trained. This is the less-popular but probably more practical side of respiratory training.

The primary muscles of breathing include:

**The diaphragm.** As noted in 1.9.1, the dome-shaped muscle below the lungs that contracts on inhalation. The diaphragm does most of the work of normal breathing.

**Intercostal muscles.** The muscles between the ribs that lift the rib cage during inhalation and lower it during exhalation.

**Accessory muscles.** Muscles in the neck and shoulders (sternocleidomastoid, scalenes, upper trapezius) that assist breathing during heavy exertion. These are not designed to be primary breathers; chronic activation of accessory muscles for normal breathing is a sign of dysfunctional patterns.

Like any muscles, the respiratory muscles can fatigue, weaken, or strengthen depending on the demands placed on them. The training literature on respiratory muscle strength has produced consistent findings (HajGhanbari et al., 2013).

**Inspiratory muscle training (IMT).** This typically involves breathing through a device that creates resistance to inhalation — making the diaphragm and intercostals work harder against the resistance. The standard protocol is something like 30 breaths against a moderate resistance, twice per day, for 6 to 8 weeks. The training has been shown to improve respiratory muscle strength, exercise endurance, and breathlessness ratings during exertion.

**Functional respiratory training.** Some training does not require special equipment. Practices like singing, certain wind instruments, and specific yoga breathing exercises produce respiratory muscle work. These are real but generally lower-intensity than dedicated IMT devices.

**Whole-body conditioning.** Regular cardiovascular training itself trains the respiratory muscles, because heavier breathing during exercise is exactly what those muscles do. Users who train cardiovascularly tend to develop reasonably strong respiratory muscles as a side effect.

The practical question is who benefits from dedicated respiratory muscle training versus who gets enough from general fitness work. A few populations do appear to benefit specifically:

- **Endurance athletes** seeking marginal performance improvements.
- **People with chronic respiratory conditions** like COPD, where dedicated training can reduce breathlessness substantially (Beaumont et al., 2018).
- **Older adults** with declining respiratory capacity, where targeted training can help preserve function.
- **Singers, public speakers, and wind musicians** for whom respiratory control is professionally relevant.

For most general-fitness users, dedicated respiratory training is probably unnecessary. The breathing work covered in 1.9.1 and 1.9.3 — slow breathing for autonomic effects, application during stress and sleep — produces most of the benefit available without specialised equipment.

## What about hypoxic training and altitude?

A separate line of "respiratory training" involves deliberate exposure to reduced oxygen levels — either through altitude (real or simulated) or through specific breathing protocols that reduce blood oxygen.

The evidence on altitude training for endurance athletes is reasonably positive but specialised. Living at altitude (or sleeping in altitude tents that simulate altitude) for 3 to 4 weeks tends to increase red blood cell production and modestly improve sea-level performance afterward. This is well-documented in elite endurance athletes (Levine & Stray-Gundersen, 1997). For recreational users, the effect is smaller and the practical complications usually outweigh the benefits.

Several popular breathing methods involve breath-hold protocols that produce intermittent **hypoxia** (reduced blood oxygen). The most prominent is the Wim Hof Method, which combines hyperventilation, breath holds, and cold exposure. The acute physiological effects are real and measurable — substantial autonomic activation, hormonal release, transient changes in inflammatory markers (Kox et al., 2014). The longer-term benefits and risks are less well-characterised.

The honest assessment is that intermittent hypoxia protocols are interesting research areas, but the practical benefits for general health are not yet clearly established beyond what conventional breathing practice and exercise produce. They are not dangerous for most healthy adults at moderate doses, but they are not the dramatic interventions some popular books imply. Users curious about them can experiment cautiously; users seeking dependable health improvements should focus on the more established interventions covered elsewhere in this library.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

CO₂ tolerance and respiratory muscle strength affect Dolphin's metrics indirectly but consistently.

**HRV (0.25).** Higher CO₂ tolerance allows for genuinely slower breathing at rest, which supports better autonomic balance and HRV. The user who breathes 12 times per minute at rest, comfortably, generally shows better HRV than the user who breathes 18 times per minute even though both feel "normal." Breathing rate at rest is one of the simpler reflections of underlying autonomic state.

**RHR (0.15).** Better respiratory function supports more efficient cardiovascular function, which tends to produce modest reductions in baseline resting heart rate over time.

**Sleep quality (0.25).** Users with strong CO₂ tolerance and good respiratory function tend to sleep with quieter, more diaphragmatic breathing. Snoring, obstructed breathing, and sleep-disordered breathing all impair sleep quality and are partially responsive to respiratory training (though clinical sleep disorders require proper medical evaluation).

**Exercise frequency (0.20) and active calories (0.10).** Better respiratory function supports more comfortable exercise, particularly aerobic work, which makes consistent training easier to sustain.

The integration here is the same point made elsewhere in this library: foundational physiological qualities support multiple metrics simultaneously. Investing time in CO₂ tolerance training pays off across the entire Body pillar, even though no single metric is dedicated to it.
