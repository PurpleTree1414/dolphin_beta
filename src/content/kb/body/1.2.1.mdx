There is a stubborn cultural assumption that eating less is always healthier than eating more — that the body is, broadly speaking, a problem to be restricted into shape. This assumption is wrong in ways that matter. For someone whose goal is sustained energy, sound recovery, and a body that adapts well to training, the more useful question is rarely "how do I eat less?" It is "do I have enough fuel to actually do what I am asking my body to do?"

This article is about that question. It introduces the concept of energy availability, walks through what happens when a body is chronically underfuelled, and ends with how to read your own hunger and fullness signals — because the long-term solution is rarely a calorie spreadsheet. It is a body whose own signals you can trust.

## What energy availability actually means

In sports science, "energy availability" has a specific definition. It is the energy left over for the body's basic biological functions after the energy cost of exercise has been subtracted. The clinical formula is energy intake minus exercise energy expenditure, divided by fat-free mass. The number that comes out, expressed in kilocalories per kilogram of fat-free mass per day, tells you how much energy your body actually has available to run everything that is not exercise: hormone production, immune function, bone remodelling, menstrual cycling, cognition, repair.

You do not need to calculate this yourself. The concept matters more than the arithmetic. The point is that exercise is not free. The energy you spend training is energy your body cannot also use for the other things it needs to do. If your total intake does not cover both, something has to give — and the body is unsentimental about what gives first.

The framework was formalised by Anne Loucks and colleagues in the late 1990s and 2000s and has since been refined by the International Olympic Committee under the term Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport, or RED-S (Mountjoy et al., 2018). RED-S began as a description of what happens to elite female endurance athletes — disrupted menstrual cycles, low bone density, suppressed thyroid function — but the underlying physiology applies to anyone who chronically eats less than they expend, regardless of sex or athletic level.

The threshold below which problems start to appear is roughly 30 kilocalories per kilogram of fat-free mass per day. A reasonably active adult sits comfortably above this. Someone training hard and eating like a bird often does not. The signs of having dropped below it are not subtle once you know to look for them.

## What happens when the body is chronically underfuelled

The body's first response to insufficient energy is intelligent triage. Non-essential functions get downregulated to free up energy for essentials. The trouble is that the body's definition of "essential" runs on evolutionary logic, and many of the things it considers non-essential are things you would prefer to keep.

**Hormone production drops.** Testosterone in men, oestrogen and progesterone in women, thyroid hormones in everyone. The downstream effects are slowed metabolism, blunted training adaptation, low libido, mood disturbance, and in women, irregular or absent menstrual cycles. De Souza et al. (2014) reviewed the evidence on functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea and concluded that energy deficiency is its primary driver — not exercise volume per se, but the gap between intake and demand.

**Recovery slows.** Protein synthesis requires energy. Tissue repair requires energy. The inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cycles that drive adaptation require energy. When energy is short, the body still does these things — but more slowly and less completely. Training adaptations stall. Soreness lingers. Small injuries take longer to heal.

**Bone density falls.** This is the consequence that gets the most attention in athletes and the one that is hardest to reverse. Chronic low energy availability suppresses the hormonal signals that drive bone remodelling. Over years, this can produce stress fractures in young athletes and accelerate osteoporosis later in life (Nattiv et al., 2007).

**Cognition and mood degrade.** The brain is metabolically expensive. Sustained underfuelling produces low mood, irritability, brain fog, and reduced executive function. Many people who have been dieting hard for months attribute these symptoms to stress or burnout when the actual cause is on their plate.

**Immune function weakens.** Athletes in chronic energy deficit get sick more often. Walsh et al. (2011) reviewed the literature on exercise immunology and concluded that energy availability is one of the strongest modifiable predictors of upper respiratory tract infections in trained populations.

The pattern is unified. None of these consequences are dramatic on day one. They are slow, cumulative, and easy to attribute to other causes — which is why chronic underfuelling so often goes unrecognised until something visible breaks.

## The other side: eating to support the work you do

The corrective is not "eat more" in some absolute sense. It is "eat enough for what you are actually doing."

For a sedentary office worker, energy needs are modest and easily met. For someone training three or four times a week, they are not. The most common mistake in recreationally active people is failing to scale intake to demand. They train more, but eat the same — and then wonder why their energy plateaus or their progress stalls.

A few patterns are worth knowing.

**Carbohydrate availability matters most around training.** Carbohydrate is the body's preferred fuel for moderate-to-high-intensity work. Burke et al. (2011) reviewed the evidence on carbohydrate periodisation and concluded that adequate intake before and after harder sessions improves training quality and recovery. The exact amount depends on session intensity and duration, but the principle is that hard training and low-carbohydrate eating are difficult to combine without paying a price somewhere.

**Protein supports adaptation, not just muscle growth.** The standard recommendation for active adults is roughly 1.6 to 2.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day, distributed across the day rather than concentrated in one meal (Morton et al., 2018). This range is associated with improved recovery, better body composition outcomes, and preserved muscle mass during periods of restricted calories. The function is broader than building muscle — protein is the raw material for nearly every tissue the body needs to repair and rebuild.

**Fat is the longer-term substrate.** Dietary fat supports hormone production, fat-soluble vitamin absorption, cell-membrane function, and steady energy supply during long-duration low-intensity activity. Very-low-fat diets, especially when sustained, tend to produce the hormonal disruptions described earlier. There is no precise required amount, but most evidence suggests fat should not drop below roughly 20% of total energy intake for active adults.

**Total calories matter more than the precise ratio.** This is worth saying clearly because the macronutrient debate gets disproportionate cultural attention. Most active adults will do well on a wide range of macronutrient distributions, provided total intake covers their actual demand. The debate about whether to favour carbs or fats matters far less than whether you are eating enough overall.

## Reading your own hunger and fullness signals

The longest-term solution to fuelling is not external — it is internal. The body has a sophisticated system for telling you when to eat and when to stop. The trouble is that many people have spent years overriding that system, either through restrictive dieting or through the kind of distracted eating where hunger and fullness signals never quite reach awareness.

The biology runs through several hormones. Ghrelin, secreted mainly by the stomach, signals hunger and rises before meals. Cholecystokinin, peptide YY, and GLP-1 are released by the gut during and after eating and signal fullness over a span of minutes. Leptin, secreted by fat tissue, signals longer-term energy status to the brain (Cummings et al., 2002). These signals are designed to keep intake matched to demand, and in people who have not aggressively dieted, they generally do.

Two practical interventions help reconnect with these signals when they have gone quiet.

The first is a hunger scale. Before deciding to eat, rate your hunger from one (ravenous) to ten (uncomfortably full). Decide what number you want to start eating at and what number you want to stop at. For most people, starting around three or four (clearly hungry but not desperate) and stopping around six or seven (comfortably satisfied but not stuffed) works well. The scale is not a rigid rule. It is a way of reintroducing awareness into a process that, for many adults, has become entirely automatic.

The second is reducing distraction. Tribole and Resch's work on intuitive eating (2020) and Mehling's interoception research (2012, cited in Article 1.1.1) converge on the same finding: people who eat while focused on the food perceive fullness more accurately than people who eat while working, scrolling, or watching screens. The signals are still there. The difference is whether they are noticed.

Internal signals are not perfect. They can be blunted by chronic dieting, sleep deprivation, alcohol, ultra-processed foods designed to override satiety, and high stress. They are nonetheless the most accurate intake-regulation system you have access to in the long run. Calorie tracking is a useful tool for short-term education — for understanding what foods contain, for example — but rare is the person who tracks calories happily for thirty years. Internal regulation, restored, is what scales.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

Energy availability is not a metric Dolphin tracks directly. But it is upstream of nearly every Body metric that is.

Heart rate variability, the most heavily weighted Body metric (0.25), drops in chronic energy deficiency. The autonomic nervous system reads sustained underfuelling as a stressor and responds with elevated sympathetic tone. The HRV decline is gradual and is often misread as overtraining when the actual issue is undereating.

Resting heart rate (0.15) shows the same pattern in the opposite direction. Chronically underfuelled bodies tend to show slightly elevated resting heart rates over time as the system runs in a more activated state.

Sleep quality (0.25) deteriorates when intake drops below demand. Hunger interrupts sleep onset. Cortisol rises overnight to maintain blood glucose. Sleep architecture shifts in ways that reduce restorative deep sleep.

And exercise frequency (0.20), the metric most directly under your control, becomes harder to sustain when the body is not receiving enough fuel to recover between sessions. You may force yourself through workouts on willpower for a while, but eventually performance drops, motivation fades, and frequency falls with it.

The connection works in the other direction too. The cleanest way to support every Body metric Dolphin tracks is to ensure your intake actually matches your demand — which, for most active people, means eating more than they think they need, more often than they think they should, and trusting their body's signals more than they currently do.
