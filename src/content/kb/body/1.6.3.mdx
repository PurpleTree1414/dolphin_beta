The previous two articles in this section have been about how to recover well. This one is about what happens when recovery fails — when training stress accumulates faster than the body can clear it, and the body starts producing the warning signals that tell you something needs to change.

There are three distinct phenomena to know about, on a spectrum of severity. Delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS) is the mildest and most common — a normal training response that most users experience routinely and that requires only modest adjustment. Overreaching is more substantial: a state of accumulated fatigue that, if caught early, resolves with rest, but if ignored, can progress into the most serious form, overtraining syndrome, which is severe enough that recovery can take weeks to months. Burnout is the psychological cousin of overtraining — distinct in origin but often overlapping in its physiological consequences.

The reason all three deserve treatment together is that they form a continuum. Mild soreness is normal training. Persistent soreness combined with declining performance is early overreaching. Sustained inability to recover, accompanied by mood and sleep changes, is overtraining. Knowing where on this spectrum you are is one of the most valuable training skills.

## DOMS: what it is and what it is not

Delayed-onset muscle soreness is the dull, distinctive ache that appears 12 to 48 hours after a training session involving novel or intense work, peaks somewhere around 24 to 72 hours, and resolves within a week. It is most pronounced in the muscles that did the work and is particularly associated with eccentric (lengthening) contractions — the muscle action involved in lowering weights, walking downhill, or absorbing landing impact.

The mechanism is partly mechanical and partly biochemical. Cheung et al. (2003) reviewed the evidence and described the process clearly: hard exercise produces small disruptions in the structure of muscle fibres, particularly in the structures that anchor contractile proteins to each other. The body responds with a localised inflammatory cascade — the same general process that responds to any tissue damage — which produces the swelling, sensitivity, and aching that we experience as soreness. Over the following days, the damaged fibres are repaired, often with slight additions to capacity that contribute to training adaptation.

A few important points about DOMS:

**DOMS is not a measure of training quality.** This is the most common misconception. Training that produces severe soreness is not therefore producing more adaptation than training that produces mild soreness. The relationship between soreness and adaptation is loose. Some highly productive training produces minimal soreness; some training that produces dramatic soreness produces only moderate adaptation. DOMS reflects mechanical disruption, not training value.

**DOMS adapts quickly.** The "repeated bout effect" is one of the more interesting phenomena in exercise science: a training session that produces substantial DOMS, when repeated within a few weeks, produces much less soreness the second time and progressively less with continued repetition. McHugh (2003) reviewed this effect and noted that the protective adaptation can persist for months after a single training bout. This is why beginners experience dramatic soreness early in a program and progressively less as they continue, and why returning from a layoff produces soreness even at familiar loads.

**DOMS does not require treatment.** A range of interventions have been studied for treating or preventing DOMS, and most produce small effects at best. The interventions covered in 1.6.2 — foam rolling, gentle stretching, easy movement, mild heat — modestly reduce subjective soreness. None substantially affects the underlying recovery timeline. The most reliable intervention is time.

**Training while sore is generally fine, but with judgement.** Training muscles that are mildly to moderately sore from a previous session typically does not impair performance significantly and does not delay recovery. Training muscles that are severely sore (to the point of impaired function) is usually unproductive — performance is reduced, technique is compromised, and recovery is extended. The honest test is usually whether you can perform the planned work with reasonable form.

The practical framing: DOMS is normal, particularly with new or unusually intense training. It is not something to fear or to chase. It is one signal among many that the body is doing the work training requires.

## When DOMS becomes a warning

There is a different category of soreness that is worth distinguishing from normal DOMS:

**Soreness that does not resolve.** Soreness that persists past 5 to 7 days, particularly in a specific area or joint rather than diffusely in a muscle, may indicate something more than normal training response. Persistent localised soreness can be early-stage tendinopathy, a small muscle strain, or another low-grade injury that has not yet announced itself clearly. The appropriate response is reduced training of the affected area, not pushing through.

**Soreness that intensifies rather than fades.** Normal DOMS peaks within 72 hours and steadily declines. Soreness that gets worse with continued training, or that requires longer recovery between sessions over time, is a signal that the training stress has exceeded recovery capacity.

**Sharp or specific pain.** This is the pain-vs-discomfort distinction from Article 1.1.1. DOMS is diffuse and aching. Sharp or localised pain is a different kind of signal and warrants the same caution: stop the activity that produces it, and consider whether something has been injured.

These distinctions matter because the appropriate response is different. Normal DOMS is managed with time, easy movement, and continuing reasonable training. Persistent or atypical soreness is a signal that something else is happening and that training adjustments are needed.

## Overreaching and overtraining

Beyond ordinary soreness lies a more substantial category: the state of accumulated fatigue that develops when training stress chronically exceeds recovery capacity. The terminology in this area has evolved over time, but the current consensus, articulated in the joint statement of the European College of Sport Science and American College of Sports Medicine (Meeusen et al., 2013), distinguishes three states:

**Functional overreaching (FOR).** Short-term accumulated fatigue, typically over days to a few weeks, that produces a temporary performance dip. After a period of reduced training (a deload, an unloading week, or simply easier sessions), performance returns and often improves slightly relative to baseline. This is sometimes deliberate — competitive athletes use planned overreaching followed by recovery to produce performance peaks. For recreational users, mild overreaching is common during especially demanding training periods and resolves naturally with sensible adjustments.

**Non-functional overreaching (NFOR).** A more substantial accumulated fatigue, typically requiring weeks to months of reduced training to resolve. Performance is reduced, mood may be affected, and the user does not recover with normal weekly rest patterns. This is the state where the body has crossed a line that requires deliberate intervention.

**Overtraining syndrome (OTS).** The most severe form, requiring months to years to fully resolve. OTS involves persistent performance decline, mood disturbance (often depressive), sleep disruption, hormonal disturbances, and often increased susceptibility to illness. This is rare in recreational populations but occurs more frequently in serious athletes pushing their training capacity.

The distinction between NFOR and OTS is mostly one of duration and severity — there is no clear biochemical line separating them, and the same underlying mismatch (training stress exceeding recovery) drives both. The practical implication is that overreaching is reversible with adjustment; full overtraining syndrome is much harder to recover from. Catching the pattern early is what prevents progression.

## The signs of accumulating overreaching

The early warning signs of overreaching are subtle and easy to misattribute. A few patterns recur:

**Persistent fatigue that does not respond to rest.** A weekend off does not produce the usual return of energy. Sleep, even when adequate, does not feel restorative. The trainee starts the week as tired as they ended the previous one.

**Performance decline despite consistent training.** Lifts that were comfortable now feel hard. Run paces that were sustainable feel like a struggle. The trainee is putting in the same work and getting less back.

**Mood and motivation changes.** Reduced enthusiasm for training, irritability, low mood, or general flatness. The mental side of training drains away before the physical side fully follows.

**Sleep disruption.** Difficulty falling asleep, more frequent night waking, or unrefreshing sleep despite adequate duration.

**Elevated resting heart rate and reduced HRV.** This is where Dolphin's data becomes particularly useful. Sustained shifts in these autonomic markers — RHR elevated 5 to 10 beats above baseline for multiple days, HRV consistently below the user's typical range — are an objective signature of accumulated stress. The 7-day rolling averages smooth out daily noise and reveal the trend.

**Increased illness susceptibility.** More colds, longer-lasting colds, frequent minor infections. The immune system is one of the systems most vulnerable to chronic overreaching.

**Emotional reactivity.** Disproportionate irritation, frustration with normal challenges, reduced patience for things that previously did not bother the trainee.

The reliability of any single sign is limited. Any of these can have other causes — a stressful work period, illness, family demands. What is more telling is the combination: multiple signs appearing together, persisting for a week or more, and not responding to a normal weekend's rest. That pattern is the autonomic and behavioural signature of training stress exceeding recovery.

## What to do when overreaching appears

The good news is that early-stage overreaching responds well to deliberate intervention. The bad news is that the natural impulse — to push harder, train through it, rely on willpower — is exactly the wrong response and reliably worsens the underlying problem.

The standard protocol when overreaching is suspected:

**Reduce training load substantially.** Cut volume by 30 to 50% for at least one week. For users in early-stage overreaching, a single deload week often produces visible improvement; for users further along, two to four weeks may be needed. The training stimulus during this period should feel easy, not just slightly reduced.

**Prioritise sleep.** Add an extra hour where possible. Improve sleep hygiene (covered in 1.7.2). Treat sleep as the primary intervention rather than an afterthought.

**Increase nutritional intake.** Many users in overreaching are also undereating. Ensuring adequate calories, protein, and carbohydrate supports the recovery the body is attempting.

**Reduce other stress sources where possible.** The body integrates total stress, not just training stress. Periods of high work or family demands are not the right time to also push training hard. Recognising the cumulative burden allows for sensible adjustment.

**Reassess before returning.** After a period of reduced training, assess subjective state, performance, and the autonomic markers (HRV, RHR). If these have returned to baseline, gradual return to normal training is reasonable. If they have not, the recovery period needs to extend.

A note on what does not work: rest alone, without addressing the contributing factors, often produces only partial recovery. The user who takes a week off but continues to sleep poorly, eat inadequately, and carry high life stress will not fully recover. Comprehensive recovery requires comprehensive support.

## Burnout: the psychological dimension

Burnout is technically a psychological construct distinct from overtraining, but they overlap substantially in practice. Burnout — the state of emotional exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced sense of efficacy that results from prolonged psychological demand — was originally described in occupational contexts but has been studied extensively in athletic populations as well (Gustafsson et al., 2017).

The relevant points for users of this library:

**Burnout can develop alongside overtraining or independently.** A trainee can be physiologically well-recovered but psychologically burned out — feeling no enthusiasm for training, going through motions, deriving no satisfaction from the work. Conversely, a trainee can be physiologically overreached but mentally still motivated, which is part of why overreaching often progresses: the desire to train remains even as the capacity to recover from it fails.

**The interventions for burnout overlap with but are not identical to those for overtraining.** Reduced training helps both. But burnout often requires additional changes: variation in the type of training (different modalities, different goals), changes in the social or environmental context of training, attention to whether the underlying motivations have shifted, and sometimes a longer break from structured training entirely.

**The Mind pillar covers more.** The fuller treatment of burnout, including the cognitive and emotional contributors, sits in the Mind pillar of this library. The Body-side intervention is largely about reducing the training-related contributions.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

The autonomic markers Dolphin tracks — HRV (0.25) and RHR (0.15) — are among the most reliable available measures of accumulated training stress. The 7-day rolling averages they use are specifically designed to capture the kind of trend that signals overreaching.

The pattern of overreaching in Dolphin's data is reasonably distinctive. Over a week or two, HRV declines below the user's typical range. RHR elevates several beats above baseline. Sleep quality declines. The metrics are correlated — they reflect a unified autonomic state — and the combination is more reliable than any single marker.

What the data cannot do is make the decision for you. The metrics describe the state of the system; they do not prescribe the response. The user who sees declining HRV and rising RHR has to interpret that pattern in context — has training been intense, has sleep been compromised, is illness developing — and choose the appropriate response. The most common error is dismissing the pattern as one bad week and continuing to train as planned. The data is usually correct; ignoring it tends to produce the kind of forced recovery the metrics were trying to help avoid.

Sleep quality (0.25) is particularly relevant here. Sleep is often the first metric to deteriorate in overreaching and the last to fully restore. A trainee whose sleep quality has been declining for two weeks, alongside autonomic changes, is showing the signature of accumulated stress regardless of whether other symptoms have appeared yet.

Exercise frequency (0.20) is the metric most paradoxically affected. Overreaching trainees often show stable or rising exercise frequency in the early phases — they are still training hard, sometimes harder, in pursuit of progress that is not appearing. The metric only drops when forced rest becomes unavoidable. By that point, the recovery requirement is much larger than it would have been if the warning signs had been respected earlier.
