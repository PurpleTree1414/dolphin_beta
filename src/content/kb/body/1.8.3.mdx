The previous two articles in this section covered the stress response and metabolic function. This article zooms out to the broader endocrine system — the network of glands and hormones that regulates much of what the rest of this library has been describing. The goal is not a comprehensive endocrinology textbook, which would be both impossible and unnecessary for this audience. The goal is to give users a working understanding of what their hormones are actually doing, how the major lifestyle inputs affect them, and what signs to look for when something is off.

The framing throughout is that the endocrine system is the integrator. Almost everything else this library covers — training, recovery, sleep, nutrition, stress — eventually expresses itself through hormonal signalling. When hormones are working well, they do their job invisibly. When they are not, the consequences ripple outward through nearly every system the body runs.

## What hormones actually do

Hormones are signalling molecules. The endocrine system uses them to send information from one part of the body to another, typically through the bloodstream. A gland in one location produces a hormone that travels through the circulation and acts on receptors in tissues elsewhere. The system is, in some ways, the body's slower and more sustained counterpart to the nervous system — slower in onset, but capable of producing effects that persist for hours, days, or longer.

The endocrine system's defining feature is that it is highly interconnected. Hormones rarely act in isolation. A hormone produced by one gland often regulates another, and feedback loops between glands maintain coordinated function. This is why hormonal disturbances tend to manifest as patterns rather than single-marker problems: when one system is dysregulated, the others usually shift in response.

For the practical purposes of this library, the hormones that matter most for users are those that respond to the lifestyle inputs they can actually control. These include:

**Cortisol.** Covered in detail in 1.8.1. The primary stress hormone, but also a daily-rhythm hormone with essential roles in waking, alertness, and metabolic regulation.

**Insulin.** Covered in 1.8.2. Regulates blood glucose; chronically elevated levels (insulin resistance) drive many of the metabolic dysfunctions characterising modern disease.

**Sex hormones.** Testosterone in men, oestrogen and progesterone in women. Influence not just reproduction but also muscle protein synthesis, bone density, mood, cognition, libido, sleep quality, and cardiovascular health.

**Thyroid hormones (T3, T4).** Regulate basal metabolic rate, temperature regulation, energy levels, and the speed of essentially every cellular process.

**Growth hormone.** Pulses overnight during slow-wave sleep; supports tissue repair, recovery, and body composition.

**Leptin and ghrelin.** Covered in 1.2.1. Long-term and short-term hunger signals respectively; central to appetite regulation.

**Melatonin.** Covered in 1.7.2. The primary sleep-related hormone; rises in the evening and falls toward morning.

What unifies these hormones is that they are all responsive to the same set of lifestyle inputs: sleep, exercise, nutrition, stress, body composition, and circadian alignment. The lifestyle inputs are not just "good for you" in some general sense. They are the actual variables the endocrine system uses to calibrate its output.

## How exercise shapes the endocrine system

Training is one of the most potent endocrine modifiers available without medication. Different forms of exercise produce different hormonal responses, both acutely and over time.

**Acute responses to a single training session.** A hard exercise session produces large transient changes in many hormones. Cortisol rises during the session and for some time afterward. Catecholamines (adrenaline, noradrenaline) rise sharply during the session and fall quickly. Growth hormone pulses during and after the session. Testosterone rises modestly during resistance training. The acute responses are temporary — typically resolving within hours to a day — and serve to mobilise resources during the session and support recovery afterward.

**Chronic effects of consistent training.** The interesting effects come from training repeated across weeks and months. The body's hormonal baseline shifts in response to consistent demand:

*Improved insulin sensitivity.* Possibly the most reliable hormonal effect of training. Both aerobic and resistance training improve the efficiency with which insulin signals tissues to take up glucose. The effect is durable provided training continues, fading within days to weeks if training stops.

*Maintenance of testosterone.* In men, regular moderate-to-vigorous exercise supports testosterone levels into older age. The effect is dose-dependent: insufficient training (sedentary lifestyle) produces declining testosterone with age more rapidly than would otherwise occur; excessive training (chronic energy deficit, overtraining) suppresses testosterone substantially. The middle range supports optimal levels (Hooper et al., 2017).

*Stabilisation of menstrual function.* In women, regular moderate exercise supports normal menstrual cycling. The same dose-response applies: excessive training combined with inadequate energy availability is one of the most common causes of menstrual disruption (covered in 1.2.1 under RED-S). Regular but appropriate exercise tends to support cycle regularity.

*Reduced chronic cortisol.* Regular moderate aerobic exercise reduces baseline cortisol over time and dampens the cortisol response to subsequent stressors. The mechanism appears to involve both autonomic improvements and direct effects on HPA axis regulation.

*Improved leptin signalling.* Body composition changes from training (more muscle, less fat) typically improve leptin signalling, which supports more reliable appetite regulation over time.

The unifying point: regular training is not a "stress" the body merely tolerates. Done at appropriate doses, it is a primary input the endocrine system uses to maintain healthy function. The user training consistently for years is, in a real sense, signalling their endocrine system to maintain youthful patterns of function longer than untrained adults typically do.

The dose matters. The same exercise that supports endocrine function at moderate doses can become an endocrine stressor at excessive doses, particularly when combined with inadequate fueling, sleep, or recovery. The signs of when training has shifted from supportive to disruptive are covered later in this article.

## How sleep shapes the endocrine system

If exercise is one major endocrine input, sleep is the other. As covered throughout Section 1.7, the effects of sleep on hormones are large, well-documented, and usually underestimated.

**Testosterone.** Most testosterone in men is produced overnight, particularly during REM sleep. Leproult and Van Cauter (2011) showed in a controlled study that one week of sleep restricted to 5 hours per night reduced testosterone in healthy young men by 10-15% — equivalent to 10 to 15 years of natural age-related decline. The effect is reversible with adequate sleep, but persistent if sleep restriction continues.

**Growth hormone.** The largest growth hormone pulses occur during slow-wave sleep, particularly in the first half of the night. Sleep restriction selectively reduces these pulses, blunting the body's primary recovery hormonal signal.

**Cortisol.** As covered in 1.8.1, cortisol follows a daily rhythm that depends on sleep timing. Sleep restriction or irregular sleep timing flattens this rhythm, with downstream effects on alertness, recovery, and metabolic function.

**Leptin and ghrelin.** Sleep restriction shifts the appetite hormones in ways that promote increased food intake. Spiegel et al. (2004) showed that two nights of restricted sleep increased ghrelin by 28% and reduced leptin by 18%, alongside a 24% increase in appetite ratings. This is part of the mechanism by which chronic sleep restriction contributes to weight gain.

**Insulin.** As covered repeatedly, sleep restriction substantially impairs insulin sensitivity. The effect is acute (single-night studies show measurable changes) and chronic (sustained sleep restriction is increasingly recognised as a contributor to type 2 diabetes risk).

**Thyroid hormones.** Sleep restriction can blunt thyroid hormone production. The effect is smaller than for testosterone or growth hormone but contributes to the general sense of low energy and slowed metabolic function that chronic sleep loss produces.

The pattern is consistent across hormones: sleep is when the endocrine system does much of its rhythmic work, and inadequate sleep degrades nearly every hormonal axis simultaneously. The user attempting to support hormonal health through supplements while chronically sleep-deprived is fighting an uphill battle. Sleep is the foundation; everything else builds on it.

## Sex hormone considerations across life stages

The major sex hormones shift across life in ways that produce predictable patterns and challenges. Worth knowing the broad outlines, both for users navigating their own changes and for understanding how the endocrine landscape shifts with age.

**Men: testosterone across the lifespan.** Testosterone in men typically peaks in the early 20s and declines gradually thereafter — roughly 1% per year on average from the late 30s onward (Travison et al., 2007). The decline is highly variable between individuals, with lifestyle factors (exercise, body composition, sleep, alcohol use, chronic stress) producing substantial differences in trajectory. Symptomatic low testosterone — including reduced libido, fatigue, mood changes, reduced muscle mass, and metabolic changes — affects a meaningful fraction of older men. Lifestyle interventions can substantially modify this trajectory; medical interventions are sometimes appropriate but require clinical assessment.

**Women: cyclic and life-stage hormone changes.** Premenopausal women experience ongoing cyclic variation in oestrogen and progesterone across the menstrual cycle, which produces predictable changes in energy, recovery, mood, and training capacity. Perimenopause — typically beginning in the 40s — produces increasingly variable hormone patterns and a range of symptoms (sleep disruption, mood changes, vasomotor symptoms, body composition changes). Menopause — defined as 12 consecutive months without menstruation — represents the long-term cessation of ovarian hormone production. The post-menopausal hormonal environment produces accelerated bone loss, cardiovascular risk changes, and altered body composition trajectories. Lifestyle interventions, particularly resistance training and adequate protein, become increasingly important during and after this transition (Watson et al., 2018, cited in 1.5.1).

**The intersection with training.** Hormone changes interact with training in important ways. The post-menopausal woman benefits from resistance training in part because the protective effects of oestrogen on bone and muscle have diminished. The man with declining testosterone benefits from resistance training because it preserves muscle and bone density that would otherwise be lost. The training prescription does not change dramatically — same patterns, same progressive overload — but the consequences of not training change. Earlier in life, the body forgives sedentary periods. Later in life, it does not.

**Hormone replacement and other interventions.** Both men and women may at some point consider medical hormone interventions. These are individual clinical decisions that require evaluation by qualified clinicians. The relevant point for this library is that lifestyle interventions and medical interventions are not mutually exclusive — they typically work additively. Even users on hormone therapy benefit from the lifestyle work this library covers; lifestyle work alone may or may not be sufficient depending on individual circumstances. The right answer is whatever produces good outcomes, often arrived at through experimentation and clinical guidance.

## Signs of hormonal health and dysfunction

Most users do not have access to comprehensive hormonal testing. What they do have is their own subjective experience and their wearable data. Worth knowing what general patterns suggest hormonal health and what patterns suggest something is off.

**Signs of generally healthy hormonal function.** Stable, adequate energy across the day. Reliable libido for the user's life stage. Consistent sleep that produces refreshment. Stable mood with normal range of emotional response. Stable body composition over time, responsive to changes in inputs. Normal recovery from training. Reliable appetite signalling. Cycle regularity (in premenopausal women).

**Signs that often point to hormonal dysfunction.** Persistent fatigue not explained by acute stress or sleep loss. Sustained low libido. Sleep disturbance not responsive to sleep hygiene interventions. Mood changes — particularly low mood, irritability, or anxiety — without clear life stressors. Stalled training progress despite consistent work. Body composition changes (particularly central fat gain) without changes in inputs. Loss of menstrual cycle in women not pursuing pregnancy. Recurrent infections. Significantly reduced cold tolerance, hair changes, or unexplained metabolic shifts.

These patterns do not reliably distinguish between specific hormonal conditions, and they overlap substantially with chronic stress, overreaching, and other states this library has covered. What they do indicate is that something has shifted and warrants investigation. The right response is typically:

**First, address the obvious lifestyle inputs.** Sleep, training balance, nutrition adequacy, stress management. Many "hormonal" symptoms resolve when the basics are corrected. The user who hasn't slept seven hours in a month is often not experiencing hormonal dysfunction so much as the consequences of one input being substantially neglected.

**Second, consider clinical assessment if patterns persist.** Blood work, conducted by a clinician familiar with the relevant patterns, can identify specific hormonal disturbances that lifestyle changes alone may not resolve. This is appropriate when patterns persist despite lifestyle correction, when symptoms are substantial enough to affect daily function, or when clinical risk factors (age, family history, specific symptom clusters) warrant assessment.

**Third, treat lifestyle and medical interventions as complementary.** Even when medical intervention is appropriate, the lifestyle work continues to matter. Hormone therapy, for instance, works better in users who also support their bodies with adequate sleep, nutrition, and exercise.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

The endocrine system underlies nearly every Body metric Dolphin tracks, but it does not appear directly. Hormonal balance shows up indirectly through the systems the wearables can measure.

**HRV (0.25)** is sensitive to autonomic balance, which is partly hormonally regulated. Chronic cortisol elevation, low testosterone in men, perimenopausal hormone variability, and several other endocrine patterns all tend to suppress HRV.

**RHR (0.15)** is similarly affected. Hormonal disturbances often shift the autonomic baseline toward sympathetic dominance, raising RHR.

**Sleep quality (0.25)** is bidirectionally connected to hormone function. Hormonal disturbances disrupt sleep; disrupted sleep degrades hormones. The two often improve together when addressed.

**Exercise frequency (0.20)** is sensitive to the energy and motivation that depend partly on hormonal health. Users in hormonal trouble often see exercise frequency decline before they consciously notice the underlying issue.

**Active calories (0.10)** and **steps (0.05)** reflect daily energy availability, which depends on hormonal function in ways that are subtle but real.

The integration here is the same point made throughout this section: the endocrine system is the integrator. Users supporting their bodies well across the lifestyle inputs (sleep, nutrition, training, recovery, stress management) tend to have hormonal systems that work well, which in turn supports good Flow Score outcomes. The hormones do not need to be measured directly. They show up in the metrics that the wearable can produce.
