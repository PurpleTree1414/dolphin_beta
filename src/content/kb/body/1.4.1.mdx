If the Body pillar has a single most important article, this is probably it. Two of the six metrics that make up your Body score — heart rate variability and resting heart rate — together account for 40% of the total. Both are produced by the same system: the heart and the autonomic nervous system that regulates it. If you do not understand what these numbers are measuring, you cannot reason about why your Flow Score moves the way it does, and you cannot make sense of most of the training advice that follows in this section.

The heart is, in some ways, an unusual organ. It is a pump that beats roughly 100,000 times per day for a typical lifetime — about 2.5 to 3 billion beats over 75 to 80 years. It is also a pump that almost no other organ in the body can do without. The cardiovascular system is the delivery infrastructure for nearly every other physiological process in this library: oxygen for muscles, fuel for the brain, hormones to their targets, immune cells to wherever they are needed, heat away from the core. Whatever else your body does, the cardiovascular system has to support it.

This article covers how the heart actually works, why HRV and resting heart rate are the two most useful windows into cardiovascular health that wearables can give you, and what that means for how you train and how you read your own data.

## The heart as an adaptable organ

The heart is, fundamentally, a muscle. Like every other muscle in the body, it adapts to the demands placed on it. The adaptations are visible at multiple levels.

**The chambers get bigger.** Trained endurance athletes show enlarged left ventricles compared to sedentary controls — the chamber that pumps oxygenated blood out to the body has more capacity per beat. This is a healthy adaptation, distinct from the pathological enlargement seen in heart disease. The classic study by Pluim et al. (2000), reviewing what they called "athlete's heart," documented this difference clearly: athletes' hearts are structurally different from non-athletes' hearts, and the differences predict better performance and lower long-term cardiovascular risk.

**The walls get more efficient.** Trained hearts contract more forcefully per beat. This is partly increased muscle mass and partly improved coordination of the contraction itself. The result is that each beat moves more blood — and the heart can therefore beat more slowly to deliver the same volume.

**The plumbing gets better.** Endurance training expands the network of small blood vessels supplying the heart muscle itself, and improves the function of the inner lining of arteries throughout the body — what is called endothelial function. Joyner and Green (2009) reviewed this evidence and concluded that endothelial improvements may be one of the largest single benefits of cardiovascular training, with effects on long-term disease risk that exceed what would be predicted from cardiorespiratory fitness alone.

**The autonomic nervous system rebalances.** This is the adaptation most directly relevant to HRV. Regular cardiovascular training shifts the balance of the autonomic nervous system toward parasympathetic dominance at rest — the "rest and digest" state — and improves the system's flexibility under stress. We will return to what this means in a moment.

These adaptations do not require elite-level training. The largest gains, in absolute terms, come from going from sedentary to moderately active. Blair et al. (1995), in a now-classic study following over 13,000 men across roughly eight years, found that the largest reduction in mortality came from moving out of the lowest fitness category — not from moving from "fit" to "very fit." The body's cardiovascular machinery responds remarkably to even modest doses of consistent aerobic work, and most of the available benefit is captured well below what would be considered athletic fitness.

## What resting heart rate actually measures

Resting heart rate (RHR) is the number of times your heart beats per minute when you are awake but at rest, after waking and before any significant activity or stimulants. The normal adult range is wide — somewhere between 60 and 100 beats per minute in conventional medical guidelines — but the useful window is narrower. For trained adults, RHR typically sits between 50 and 70 beats per minute. For highly trained endurance athletes, it can drop into the 40s or even high 30s.

What RHR is really measuring is the efficiency of your cardiovascular system at rest. A lower RHR means the heart is moving more blood per beat, requiring fewer beats to maintain circulation. This is partly a structural adaptation (a larger, stronger ventricle) and partly an autonomic one (greater parasympathetic tone, which slows the heart at rest).

The clinical significance of RHR is well-established. A long line of large prospective studies has linked higher RHR to higher cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, even within the "normal" range. Aune et al. (2017) pooled data from 87 studies covering over 2 million participants and found that each 10-beat-per-minute increase in resting heart rate was associated with roughly a 17% increase in all-cause mortality risk, after adjusting for other cardiovascular risk factors. This effect persisted across age groups, sexes, and baseline health status.

The mechanism is debated. Some of the relationship reflects underlying cardiovascular fitness (less-fit people have higher RHRs *and* higher mortality). Some reflects autonomic balance independently. Some likely reflects the cumulative wear of more total beats over a lifetime. The practical implication is that RHR is a useful, easy-to-measure marker of cardiovascular health, and that lower (within the normal range) is associated with better outcomes.

What can change your RHR? Cardiovascular training is the largest lever — sustained aerobic work over weeks to months can lower RHR by 5 to 15 beats per minute. Beyond that, RHR responds to a long list of inputs: stress, sleep quality, caffeine, alcohol, illness, hydration, and time of day. A single day's reading is noisy. A week's average is informative. A month's trend is reliable.

## What heart rate variability actually measures

HRV is the more sophisticated cousin of resting heart rate, and the more useful one for day-to-day training decisions.

The measurement is counterintuitive. A healthy heart does not beat at a perfectly steady rhythm. The interval between beats — measured in milliseconds and called the R-R interval — varies subtly from beat to beat, modulated by the autonomic nervous system. HRV is the statistical measure of this variation. Higher HRV means more variation between beats; lower HRV means more uniform timing.

The key insight is that more variation is, generally, a good thing. A heart that beats with high variability is responsive — its rate is being modulated moment-to-moment by parasympathetic input from the vagus nerve. A heart that beats with low variability is, in a sense, locked in. Either the parasympathetic nervous system is dampened, the sympathetic system is dominant, or both. This is the autonomic state associated with stress, fatigue, illness, and overtraining.

The full physiology is reviewed in Shaffer and Ginsberg (2017), but the core relationship is straightforward: HRV is a window into autonomic balance. People with consistently higher HRV show better cardiovascular health, better stress recovery, lower all-cause mortality, and better psychological wellbeing in large prospective studies (Jandackova et al., 2016).

A few practical points are worth knowing.

**HRV is highly individual.** Absolute HRV values vary enormously between people based on age, sex, fitness, genetics, and measurement method. A 25-year-old endurance athlete might routinely show HRV of 80 to 120 ms; a healthy 50-year-old might show 30 to 50 ms. Comparing your number to anyone else's is not meaningful. What is meaningful is how your number changes relative to your own baseline.

**Trends matter more than single readings.** A single day's HRV is noisy. It can drop substantially after one bad night of sleep, one alcoholic drink, one stressful meeting, or one hard workout. A 7-day rolling average — which is what Dolphin uses — smooths out this noise and produces a more reliable signal. Plews et al. (2013) showed that rolling averages of HRV produce stronger correlations with training adaptation and readiness than single-day measurements.

**HRV measures autonomic state, not fitness directly.** Two people with the same VO₂max can show very different HRVs. HRV is a window into how the autonomic nervous system is currently regulated — which is influenced by fitness but also by stress, sleep, illness, hydration, alcohol, and recent training load. A drop in HRV does not necessarily mean you have lost fitness; it usually means you are accumulating stress, recovering inadequately, or facing a stressor your nervous system has not adapted to yet.

**HRV trends respond to interventions.** Cardiovascular training over weeks to months tends to raise HRV. Chronic stress, poor sleep, and overtraining lower it. Reducing alcohol, improving sleep consistency, and building in adequate recovery between sessions all tend to raise it. Slow diaphragmatic breathing, covered in Section 1.9, is one of the few interventions that produces an acute rise in HRV within minutes — useful for stress regulation, though the long-term effect on baseline HRV is more modest.

## Why these two metrics together are so useful

RHR and HRV both reflect autonomic balance, but they tell you slightly different things, and their disagreements are often where the most useful information lives.

A typical "good" day looks like baseline RHR with normal-to-elevated HRV. The autonomic nervous system is balanced, parasympathetic tone is healthy, and the body is recovered.

A "stressed" day usually shows the opposite: elevated RHR (5 to 15 beats above your baseline) and reduced HRV. This pattern can be triggered by inadequate sleep, alcohol the night before, an oncoming illness, accumulated training stress, or psychological stress. The combination is more reliable than either marker alone.

A "fatigued but fine" day might show normal RHR but slightly elevated HRV — the autonomic state of someone who is tired but not stressed. This is a normal post-training pattern.

A "hidden problem" day sometimes shows normal RHR but substantially reduced HRV — the early signature of an autonomic shift that has not yet propagated to the more obvious markers. This is one of the contexts where HRV is most valuable: it can detect autonomic strain before RHR shifts visibly, often a day or two before you would notice symptomatically.

The combination is powerful because the two markers interpret each other. Persistent elevation in RHR with persistent reduction in HRV is the signature of the kind of accumulated load you should respect — whether that load is training, stress, or illness. Brief excursions in either are normal noise. Sustained shifts in both are signal.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

The Body score weights HRV at 0.25 and RHR at 0.15. Together that is 40% of your daily Body score, which is more than any other functional category. The metrics are weighted this way for two reasons.

The first is that they are, by far, the most reliable health indicators that consumer wearables can produce. Step counts and calorie estimates are useful but variable in accuracy across devices and contexts. RHR and HRV, when measured consistently with a worn device and averaged over rolling windows, produce signals that correlate with documented health outcomes in large studies. They earn their weight by being trustworthy.

The second is that they are integrative. They reflect not just what you did today, but how the cumulative load of your week — your training, your sleep, your stress, your nutrition, your hydration — has affected your autonomic state. A user who trains hard, sleeps well, eats enough, and manages stress will see this reflected in HRV and RHR trends over weeks. A user whose lifestyle is undermining their training will see it reflected in the same place, often before they consciously feel it.

This is why the Body pillar is structured the way it is. The articles in Sections 1.5 (Strength), 1.6 (Recovery), 1.7 (Sleep), 1.8 (Hormones and Metabolism), and 1.9 (Breathing) are all, in different ways, about inputs that show up downstream in HRV and RHR. The cardiovascular system is the integrator. Train it well, support it with sleep, recovery, and nutrition, and the numbers reflect the work. Strain it without supporting it, and the numbers will tell you that too.
