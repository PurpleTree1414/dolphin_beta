The previous article was about whether you are eating enough. This article is about what those calories are made of, and why it matters less than you have probably been told — but in specific, identifiable ways.

There are two arguments to make here, and they pull in slightly different directions. The first is that the cultural war between competing diets — low-carb, low-fat, paleo, plant-based, intermittent fasting — has produced more heat than light. The differences between most reasonable diets are smaller than the differences between any of them and the standard modern eating pattern they are reacting against. The second argument is that one variable does seem to matter consistently across the literature: the degree of food processing. This is the place where the evidence has shifted noticeably in the last decade and where most people have meaningful room to improve.

## The three macronutrients, briefly

Macronutrients are the three categories of food that supply energy: protein, carbohydrate, and fat. Each plays roles the others cannot fully replace.

**Protein** is the body's structural material. Every tissue you have — muscle, organ, skin, immune cells, enzymes, much of your hormonal system — is built from amino acids that come, ultimately, from protein in your diet. Protein also has the highest thermic effect of the three (the body uses more energy to digest it) and the strongest satiety signal, meaning it keeps you full longer per calorie than carbohydrate or fat. The recommended intake for active adults — 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilogram of body weight per day, distributed across roughly three to five meals — was set out in the previous article and is supported by the meta-analytic work of Morton et al. (2018).

**Carbohydrate** is the body's preferred fuel for moderate-to-high-intensity work. The brain uses about 120 grams of glucose per day under normal conditions, and your muscles store carbohydrate as glycogen for quick access during exercise. Despite a long-running cultural fight about whether carbs are inherently fattening, the evidence on this has been clear for some time: total energy balance, not carbohydrate quantity, is the primary driver of weight gain. The classic Hall et al. (2015) study — a tightly controlled metabolic ward comparison of a reduced-carb versus reduced-fat diet at matched calories — found that the reduced-fat diet produced *slightly* greater fat loss, the opposite of the popular claim. The differences were small enough that the practical takeaway is that carbohydrate quantity is not the meaningful variable.

What is meaningful is *which* carbohydrates. Whole-grain, fibre-rich, less-processed carbohydrate sources behave very differently in the body than refined ones, and we will return to this below.

**Fat** has been culturally rehabilitated since the low-fat panic of the 1980s and 1990s, and rightly so. Dietary fat is essential for hormone production, fat-soluble vitamin absorption, cell membrane function, and steady energy supply during long-duration low-intensity activity. The Hooper et al. (2020) Cochrane review on saturated fat concluded that reducing saturated fat does modestly reduce cardiovascular events, but the effect depends heavily on what the saturated fat is replaced *with* — replacement with unsaturated fats helps, replacement with refined carbohydrate does not. The PREDIMED trial (Estruch et al., 2018) showed substantial cardiovascular benefit from a Mediterranean diet rich in olive oil and nuts, even at relatively high total fat intakes. Fat phobia has aged poorly; what survives is a more nuanced picture about *which* fats.

The practical baseline: aim for protein at the higher end of the recommended range, get enough fat to support the body's structural and hormonal needs (not below ~20% of calories), and let carbohydrate fill the remaining energy. The exact ratio matters far less than the choice of food sources within each category.

## The food-quality argument

Here is where the picture becomes more interesting, and where popular nutrition culture has, in the last decade, started to converge on a position that early diet warriors largely missed. The variable that consistently predicts health outcomes across population studies is not how many grams of any particular macronutrient you eat. It is how *processed* your food is.

The classification that has gained the most traction is the NOVA system, developed by Carlos Monteiro and colleagues at the University of São Paulo. NOVA divides foods into four groups: unprocessed or minimally processed (whole foods); processed culinary ingredients (oils, butter, sugar, salt); processed foods (cheeses, breads, canned vegetables — recognisably-derived from whole foods with limited additions); and ultra-processed foods, or UPFs, which are industrial formulations of food substances and additives typically containing ingredients you would not find in a home kitchen.

The evidence linking ultra-processed foods to poor health outcomes has accumulated rapidly. A 2019 prospective cohort study from France (Schnabel et al., 2019), following nearly 45,000 adults, found that a 10% increase in the proportion of ultra-processed foods in the diet was associated with a 14% higher risk of all-cause mortality. Similar findings have been replicated in studies from the UK, Spain, and the United States, with consistent direction even after adjustment for total calories and macronutrient composition.

The strongest evidence is mechanistic, not just observational. Hall et al. (2019) ran a randomised crossover trial in which participants were given two-week diets matched for calories, sugar, fat, fibre, and macronutrients — but one period was made of ultra-processed foods and the other of unprocessed foods. Despite the matched composition, participants ate 500 calories more per day on the ultra-processed diet and gained weight. On the unprocessed diet, they spontaneously ate less and lost weight. The same calories on paper produced very different results in practice.

Several mechanisms appear to be at work. Ultra-processed foods are typically engineered to be eaten faster, which means satiety signals — which take 15 to 20 minutes to register — arrive too late to prevent overconsumption. They tend to be lower in fibre and protein per calorie, both of which contribute to fullness. They often combine fat, sugar, and salt in proportions that are rare in nature and that appear to override normal appetite regulation. And many contain emulsifiers, artificial sweeteners, and other additives whose long-term effects on the gut microbiome are still being characterised.

The practical implication is something like: at the level of food, what you eat matters more than the macronutrient distribution it produces. A diet built primarily from whole foods and minimally processed ingredients will tend to land in a reasonable macronutrient range automatically and will support health regardless of whether it is slightly higher in carbs or fats.

## A short, useful list

The following are not rules. They are the patterns that recur across the evidence base most consistently.

- Build meals around a protein source, vegetables, and a starch from a whole-food origin (potato, rice, oats, beans, whole-grain bread).
- Most fats should come from unprocessed sources: olive oil, nuts, seeds, fatty fish, avocado, eggs, dairy.
- Fibre intake matters and almost everyone in industrialised countries falls short. The recommended 25 to 38 grams per day for adults (Stephen et al., 2017) is achieved easily through vegetables, fruit, legumes, and whole grains; rarely through anything else.
- Drinks contribute calories without satiety signals. Sweetened drinks — including most fruit juices — are one of the highest-leverage targets for nutritional improvement in most modern diets.
- Ultra-processed snack foods are designed to be overeaten. This is not your willpower failing. It is product design working as intended.
- Cooking at home, even simply, shifts the food-quality variable in a direction the evidence consistently favours.

## What the evidence does not say

A few corrective notes, because the cultural conversation around nutrition is unusually full of overconfident claims in both directions.

The evidence does not support that any single food is poisonous in normal quantities. The "toxic" framing applied variously to seed oils, gluten, lectins, dairy, and red meat tends to outrun what controlled studies actually show. Some people have specific intolerances. The general population does not need to fear any of these foods on the basis of current evidence.

The evidence does not support that whole-food versions of foods are dramatically inferior to "superfood" alternatives. Blueberries are good for you. So are apples, carrots, oranges, and the cabbage in the back of your fridge. The marginal advantage of any specific antioxidant-rich food over a generally varied whole-food diet is small.

The evidence does not support extreme intervention as routinely necessary. Most healthy people do not need to fast for 18 hours daily, eliminate entire food groups, or follow rigid timing protocols to be well-nourished. These approaches sometimes work for specific people in specific circumstances, but the everyday baseline of "eat enough, eat mostly whole foods, get enough protein and fibre" covers most of the available benefit.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

Food quality and macronutrient sufficiency interact with your Body metrics in three identifiable ways.

The first is sleep quality (0.25 of the Body score). Diets heavy in ultra-processed foods are associated with poorer sleep across multiple studies (St-Onge et al., 2016). The mechanisms are not fully resolved but appear to involve blood sugar volatility overnight, lower fibre and tryptophan availability, and disrupted gut signalling. The same dietary changes that improve waking energy tend to improve sleep architecture.

The second is heart rate variability (0.25). Inflammatory dietary patterns — high refined carbohydrate, high ultra-processed food, low fibre, low omega-3 — are associated with reduced HRV in observational and intervention studies (Soares-Miranda et al., 2014). Conversely, Mediterranean-pattern eating tends to support HRV. The effects are modest but real, and they accumulate.

The third is exercise frequency (0.20), the most behaviourally proximate metric. Adequate protein intake is necessary for the recovery that makes consistent training possible. Adequate carbohydrate fuels the sessions themselves. Insufficient food quality eventually produces fatigue, illness, or stalled adaptation that interrupts training streaks.

Note that nutrition consistency itself is a Lifestyle metric, not a Body one. The Body Research library covers what nutrition does to the body. Whether you actually do it consistently — log meals, hit your targets, maintain the habit week over week — is the territory the Lifestyle pillar tracks. The two pillars are complementary, and the cleanest results come from understanding both: the science from this article tells you what to aim for; the Lifestyle research helps you stick to it.
