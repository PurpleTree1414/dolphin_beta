"Metabolism" is one of the most overused and least understood words in popular health conversation. It is invoked to explain weight gain ("my metabolism is slow"), to justify supplements ("metabolism boosters"), and to dismiss complaints ("you just need to speed up your metabolism"). Most of these uses are either wrong or substantially exaggerated. The actual physiology is more interesting than the marketing, and understanding it helps users reason about their own bodies more accurately.

This article covers what metabolism actually is, how energy balance really works, and the day-to-day manifestation of metabolic health that most users can actually feel: blood sugar regulation. The goal is to give you the working biology rather than the cultural shorthand.

## What metabolism actually is

In the strict biological sense, metabolism is the complete set of chemical processes that occur in your body to maintain life. Every cellular reaction — building proteins, breaking down fuel, replicating DNA, contracting muscles, transmitting nerve signals — counts as metabolism. The total energy these processes require is what physiologists call total daily energy expenditure (TDEE).

TDEE breaks down into roughly four components, the sizes of which were introduced in 1.3.1 in the context of NEAT:

**Basal metabolic rate (BMR).** The energy required to keep you alive at rest. The largest single component, typically 60 to 70% of TDEE. This includes everything from heart contractions to brain activity to maintaining body temperature to ongoing cellular repair. BMR scales primarily with body size and lean mass — bigger bodies use more energy at rest, and bodies with more muscle use more energy at rest.

**Thermic effect of food (TEF).** The energy cost of digesting and processing what you eat. Roughly 10% of TDEE on a mixed diet. Protein has the highest TEF (20-30% of its calories are used in digestion), followed by carbohydrate (5-10%), then fat (0-3%). This is one reason higher-protein diets are slightly more thermogenic per calorie consumed.

**Exercise activity thermogenesis.** Energy expended during deliberate exercise. Highly variable — anywhere from 0% in sedentary people to 25% or more in serious athletes.

**Non-exercise activity thermogenesis (NEAT).** Energy expended through everything else — walking, standing, fidgeting, household tasks, posture maintenance. Hugely variable between individuals (Levine 2002, covered in 1.3.1) and the most modifiable component for most people.

The popular notion of having a "fast" or "slow" metabolism usually refers to BMR, but the differences between individuals are smaller than commonly assumed. BMR varies by maybe 10-15% from population averages once you adjust for body size and lean mass. The much larger source of variation in TDEE is NEAT and exercise — what you do all day, not how efficiently your cells run while you sit still.

This matters because it changes the practical question. The user who wonders "is my metabolism slow" usually has a BMR within normal range. What is more variable, and more modifiable, is how much they move during the day, how much muscle mass they carry, and how much they exercise. These are levers under personal control. BMR is largely not.

## What "boosting metabolism" actually accomplishes

A whole industry exists around products and protocols that promise to "boost metabolism." Most of these claims do not survive controlled study. Worth being explicit about what works and what does not.

**Building and maintaining muscle mass.** This is the most reliable way to raise BMR. Muscle tissue uses more energy at rest than fat tissue (though the difference per kilogram is smaller than commonly claimed — roughly 13 kcal/kg/day for muscle vs 4 kcal/kg/day for fat). Adding 5 kilograms of muscle raises BMR by something like 50 calories per day. That is a real effect, but not transformative. The larger benefit of strength training for body composition comes from the work itself and the post-exercise metabolic effects, not from BMR changes.

**Protein intake.** As noted above, protein has the highest thermic effect of the macronutrients. Diets higher in protein produce moderately higher TEF — perhaps 80 to 100 additional calories per day at 30% protein vs 15% protein. Real, measurable, but again not transformative.

**Caffeine.** Acutely increases metabolic rate by 3 to 11% for several hours. The effect attenuates with regular use. Useful as a short-term tool; not a strategy.

**Cold exposure.** Activates brown adipose tissue and produces measurable increases in energy expenditure. The magnitude in adults is small — typically tens to low hundreds of additional calories per day under sustained cold conditions. Not a meaningful intervention for most users.

**Most "metabolism-boosting" supplements.** Green tea extract, L-carnitine, capsaicin, raspberry ketones, and similar products show small or inconsistent effects in controlled studies. The marketing implies dramatic effects; the evidence supports very modest ones at best.

**What actually moves the needle for daily energy expenditure:** moving more during the day (NEAT), exercising consistently, building and maintaining muscle, and eating enough that the body does not downregulate metabolic rate in response to chronic restriction. These are the same recommendations that appear throughout this library, and they remain the best answer to "how do I increase my metabolism."

## The myth of metabolic damage

A common popular concern is that chronic dieting "damages" metabolism in lasting ways. The reality is more nuanced.

The body does respond to sustained energy restriction by reducing energy expenditure. This is sometimes called metabolic adaptation or adaptive thermogenesis. The mechanisms include reduced NEAT (the body becomes more sedentary unconsciously), reduced thermic effect of food (less is eaten so less is digested), reduced BMR (the body becomes slightly more efficient), and shifts in hormones that further reduce expenditure.

Müller and Bosy-Westphal (2013) reviewed the evidence and concluded that metabolic adaptation is real but smaller than commonly assumed — typically 50 to 200 calories per day below predicted values during sustained restriction. The reduction is primarily a defence mechanism, not damage. Critically, when adequate intake is restored, metabolic rate returns to near-baseline within weeks to months. The changes are reversible.

The "damage" framing has become so embedded in popular conversation that it deserves explicit correction. The body is not permanently compromised by previous dieting. It does respond to chronic underfeeding by becoming more efficient, and that response can persist for some time after refeeding. But it is not a permanent or progressive condition. Users with histories of dieting can normalise their metabolic rate, support adaptation through training, and produce the body composition changes they want — provided they consume enough to support the work.

The more accurate framing is what the Female Athlete Triad and RED-S literature suggests (covered in 1.2.1): chronic underfueling produces real consequences, and continued underfueling makes those consequences worse. The intervention is not "rest your metabolism" but "eat enough."

## Blood sugar regulation: the day-to-day side of metabolism

Most users will not directly experience their resting metabolic rate. What they will experience is blood sugar — the moment-to-moment regulation of glucose in the bloodstream that determines how energy feels across the day.

Blood glucose is tightly regulated. After a meal containing carbohydrate, blood glucose rises. The pancreas releases insulin, which signals tissues (primarily muscle and liver) to take glucose out of the bloodstream and use or store it. As glucose returns to baseline, insulin levels fall. Between meals, the liver releases stored glucose to maintain steady levels. The system is built to keep blood glucose in a relatively narrow range despite variable eating patterns.

When the system works well, this happens invisibly. When it does not, the result is the experience most users can readily identify: energy spikes and crashes, post-meal fatigue, cravings, difficulty concentrating, mid-afternoon energy slumps.

The factors affecting blood sugar regulation are reasonably well-understood:

**The food itself.** Different foods produce different glycaemic responses. Refined carbohydrates (white bread, sugar, sweetened beverages) produce sharp, large blood glucose rises followed by sharp drops. Whole foods with fibre, fat, and protein produce more gradual rises and more stable post-meal glucose. The glycaemic index ranks foods by their typical blood glucose response, though the response varies between individuals (Zeevi et al., 2015).

**The combination of foods.** Eating carbohydrate alone produces a sharper response than eating the same carbohydrate alongside protein, fat, and fibre. Combining nutrients in a balanced meal flattens the glucose curve substantially. This is one of the practical reasons whole-food meals tend to produce more stable energy than single-component snacks.

**Movement after meals.** Even brief post-meal walks substantially reduce post-meal blood glucose spikes (Reynolds et al., 2016, cited in 1.3.3). The mechanism is straightforward: muscle takes up glucose during contraction, reducing the amount that has to be cleared through insulin signalling. Ten to fifteen minutes of walking after a main meal is one of the highest-leverage simple interventions in this entire library.

**Sleep.** As covered in 1.7.1, even short-term sleep restriction substantially impairs insulin sensitivity (Donga et al., 2010). Poor sleep makes the glucose system less efficient, producing larger spikes and crashes from the same foods.

**Stress.** Cortisol elevation raises blood glucose directly and impairs insulin sensitivity. Chronic stress (covered in 1.8.1) produces more volatile glucose patterns even at the same food intake.

**Exercise patterns.** Regular exercise improves insulin sensitivity broadly. Training muscles take up more glucose for the same insulin signal than untrained muscles, producing more stable blood sugar across the day.

**Body composition.** Higher muscle mass and lower visceral fat both improve insulin sensitivity. The interventions in this library that affect body composition (training, adequate protein, daily movement) thus indirectly support blood sugar regulation.

The practical implication is that blood sugar regulation is not just about avoiding sugar. It is the integration of nearly everything else the Body pillar tracks — sleep, stress, training, daily movement, food quality, food combinations. Stable energy across the day is a signal that all of these are working reasonably well together.

## What "metabolic health" actually means

In recent years, "metabolic health" has become a useful umbrella term in clinical and research contexts. The term refers to the constellation of factors that determine how well the body's energy and substrate-handling systems function.

The most widely-used clinical definition comes from O'Hearn et al. (2022), who described metabolic health as having all five of the following: optimal blood pressure, optimal blood glucose, optimal blood lipids, optimal waist circumference, and no use of medication for any of these conditions. By this strict definition, only 12% of American adults are metabolically healthy. The figure is shocking but consistent with the broader literature on metabolic disease prevalence.

A few features of metabolic health are worth knowing:

**It is largely independent of weight.** A user can be lean and metabolically unhealthy (sometimes called "thin on the outside, fat on the inside" or TOFI), or substantially overweight and metabolically healthy. Weight is correlated with but not synonymous with metabolic health. Body composition (the proportion of muscle vs fat, and the distribution of fat) matters more than total weight for metabolic outcomes.

**It is responsive to lifestyle interventions.** Metabolic dysfunction is one of the most reliably reversible chronic conditions. The same lifestyle interventions that show up throughout this library — adequate movement, strength training, sufficient sleep, whole-food eating, stress management — produce measurable improvements in metabolic markers within weeks to months. The Diabetes Prevention Program demonstrated this at scale: lifestyle intervention reduced progression to type 2 diabetes by 58% in pre-diabetic adults (Knowler et al., 2002).

**Visceral fat is uniquely relevant.** Fat stored around abdominal organs (visceral adipose tissue) is more metabolically active and more harmful than fat stored under the skin (subcutaneous adipose tissue). Waist circumference is a reasonable proxy for visceral fat and predicts cardiovascular and metabolic outcomes more accurately than total body weight (Ross et al., 2020).

**Blood markers reflect deeper biology.** The standard metabolic markers — fasting glucose, HbA1c (a 3-month average of glucose), fasting insulin, triglycerides, HDL cholesterol — are not arbitrary numbers. They reflect the integrated function of the metabolic system, and the cutoffs that separate "healthy" from "unhealthy" are based on prospective studies of long-term outcomes.

For users of this library, the practical implication is that metabolic health is an outcome of consistent practice across the rest of the Body pillar. The interventions that improve sleep, training, recovery, and daily movement are the same interventions that improve metabolic markers. The link runs in both directions: better metabolic health supports better outcomes in every other Body domain, and good practice in those domains produces better metabolic health.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

Metabolic function does not appear directly as a Body metric in Dolphin's Flow Score. There is no dedicated "metabolic health" score, in part because the relevant biomarkers (glucose, lipids, insulin) require blood testing rather than wearable measurement.

The metrics Dolphin does track, however, reflect metabolic health in several indirect ways:

**HRV (0.25)** is partly mediated by autonomic balance, which is partly affected by metabolic function. Insulin resistance, chronic blood sugar volatility, and visceral adiposity are all associated with reduced HRV in research populations.

**RHR (0.15)** tends to be higher in metabolically dysfunctional states, partly through chronic sympathetic activation.

**Sleep quality (0.25)** and metabolic health are mutually reinforcing. Poor sleep impairs metabolism; poor metabolism degrades sleep. The two improve together when addressed simultaneously.

**Exercise frequency (0.20)** is the single most reliable lifestyle lever for metabolic health. Users who maintain this metric over months tend to see metabolic improvements that show up in clinical measurements.

**Active calories (0.10)** and **steps (0.05)**, which reflect daily movement, are similarly tied to metabolic function. NEAT, covered in 1.3.1, is one of the most modifiable components of total daily energy expenditure.

For users who do have access to clinical metabolic markers — through annual physicals, employer health programs, or direct lab ordering — these can serve as a longer-term integration of how well their Flow Score practice is actually working at the biological level. The wearable metrics provide weekly feedback; the clinical metrics provide quarterly or annual confirmation that the practice is producing the intended biological changes.
