If you ask most people what counts as exercise, they will describe a workout — a 45-minute session at the gym, a long run, a yoga class. The cultural definition of physical activity has narrowed, over the last few decades, to mean these structured, dedicated sessions. Everything that happens outside them is treated as neutral background.

The research tells a different story. The hours you spend walking, standing, moving around the house, climbing stairs, carrying groceries, and pacing on phone calls add up to substantially more energy expenditure than your scheduled training does — for most people, several times more. This non-exercise activity is also more closely tied to long-term health outcomes than scheduled exercise alone. The implication is that the most underrated lever in the Body pillar is not what you do at the gym. It is what you do in the other 23 hours.

This article covers two threads. The first is the physiological concept of non-exercise activity thermogenesis, or NEAT — what it is and why it matters. The second is the role of walking specifically, which is the single most evidence-rich form of daily movement and the foundation on which most other Body work is built.

## What NEAT actually is

Total daily energy expenditure breaks down into roughly four components. Basal metabolic rate — the energy your body uses to keep you alive at rest — accounts for the majority, typically 60 to 70%. The thermic effect of food, the energy cost of digesting and processing what you eat, is about 10%. Exercise, meaning structured workouts, accounts for somewhere between 0 and 30% depending on the person. The remaining slice — sometimes large, sometimes small — is everything else: walking, fidgeting, standing, taking the stairs, carrying things, climbing, and the thousand small movements of an active day. James Levine and colleagues at the Mayo Clinic gave this category the name non-exercise activity thermogenesis, or NEAT, in a series of studies in the early 2000s (Levine, 2002; Levine et al., 2005).

The striking finding from Levine's work is how variable NEAT is between individuals. In one classic experiment, participants were overfed by 1,000 calories per day for eight weeks. Some gained almost no fat. Others gained substantial amounts. The single best predictor of who stayed lean was not metabolic rate, age, or sex. It was how much their NEAT spontaneously increased in response to the extra food — through more fidgeting, more standing, more incidental movement (Levine et al., 1999). The body's defence against weight gain runs partly through tiny, unconscious behavioural adjustments that vary enormously from person to person.

NEAT also varies across the day, the year, and the lifespan. Active jobs, walkable neighbourhoods, hobbies that involve standing or moving, and a household where movement is woven into daily life all push NEAT upward. Desk jobs, car-dependent commutes, screen-heavy leisure time, and labour-saving devices push it downward. Levine estimates the gap between high-NEAT and low-NEAT lifestyles can reach 2,000 calories per day — more than three hours of moderate exercise.

The practical consequence is straightforward. For most people, raising daily NEAT is more accessible, more sustainable, and produces a larger total energy expenditure than adding more workouts. Workouts are still valuable for reasons that have nothing to do with calories — cardiovascular adaptation, strength, technical practice, mental health. But NEAT is the larger lever for daily energy balance and metabolic health.

## Why daily movement matters beyond calories

If NEAT were just a calorie story, it would be worth knowing about. It is not just a calorie story.

The literature on incidental daily movement has expanded considerably over the past two decades, and several findings have proven robust enough to consider settled.

**More daily movement, lower mortality.** A series of large prospective cohort studies has found that higher daily step counts are associated with lower all-cause mortality, with a dose-response relationship that flattens but does not disappear at higher activity levels. Paluch et al. (2022) pooled data from 15 cohort studies covering nearly 50,000 adults and found that mortality risk decreased progressively up to roughly 6,000 to 8,000 steps per day in older adults and 8,000 to 10,000 in younger adults, with smaller additional gains beyond. The often-cited "10,000 steps" target turns out to be roughly correct for younger adults, though it originated in a 1960s Japanese marketing campaign rather than from research.

**Movement helps independent of structured exercise.** Saint-Maurice et al. (2020) followed adults using accelerometers and found that the protective effect of daily movement appeared even after controlling for time spent in structured exercise. The two are complementary: people who exercise regularly *and* move a lot during the day fare better than people who do either alone.

**Sedentary time is its own risk factor.** A separate body of research, summarised in Section 1.3.2, has shown that prolonged uninterrupted sitting carries risks beyond simply having a low total step count. Movement matters partly because it breaks up sitting.

**Cardiometabolic markers respond to daily movement.** Insulin sensitivity, blood lipid profiles, blood pressure, and inflammatory markers all improve with increased daily activity, even at intensities far below what would qualify as "exercise" (Dunstan et al., 2012; Healy et al., 2008). The body's metabolic machinery is calibrated for movement; depriving it of movement produces predictable dysfunction over years.

The unifying interpretation is that the human body did not evolve to sit still for ten or twelve hours daily. Hunter-gatherer populations like the Hadza of Tanzania, studied extensively by Pontzer and colleagues, average somewhere between 12,000 and 18,000 steps per day as part of ordinary life, with substantial portions of moderate-intensity movement (Pontzer et al., 2012). This is the level of activity our physiology was built to expect. The modern sedentary baseline of 4,000 to 6,000 steps for many office workers represents a drastic departure, and the body responds accordingly.

## Walking as the foundation

Among forms of daily movement, walking deserves its own section. It is the most studied, the most accessible, the easiest to sustain, and the most resilient to the things that interrupt other forms of training.

The evidence base for walking is large and consistent.

**Cardiovascular benefit.** Lee and colleagues (2014) followed adults across multiple cohorts and found that even modest amounts of daily walking — 75 minutes per week of moderate-paced walking — were associated with substantial reductions in cardiovascular mortality. Higher amounts produced larger reductions, with diminishing returns after about 300 minutes per week.

**Weight maintenance.** The National Weight Control Registry, which has tracked thousands of people who maintained substantial weight loss for years, finds that the most common shared behaviour among long-term maintainers is high daily walking volume — typically 60 minutes or more per day (Wing & Phelan, 2005). Walking turns out to be the most sustainable form of physical activity for the long timescales weight maintenance requires.

**Mental health.** Walking, particularly outdoors, has well-documented effects on mood and cognition. Bratman et al. (2015) found that 90 minutes of walking in a natural setting reduced rumination and self-reported negative thoughts more than walking in an urban setting; both reduced them more than no walking at all. The effects are not dramatic, but they are consistent across studies.

**Cognitive function in ageing.** Erickson et al. (2011) randomised older adults to either walking or stretching for one year. The walking group showed measurable increases in hippocampal volume — a part of the brain typically shrinking with age — alongside improvements in memory function. The stretching group did not. Walking, plain and unglamorous, is one of the few well-supported interventions for age-related cognitive decline.

**Joint and bone health.** Walking is sufficient mechanical loading to maintain bone density and joint health in most adults. It is also low-impact enough that it can usually be sustained through minor injuries that would interrupt running or other higher-impact activities.

A useful frame is that walking is the "default" form of movement the body is most adapted to. Other forms of training add specific adaptations on top — strength, speed, sport-specific skills — but walking provides the substrate. People who walk consistently rarely lose the basic physical capacity to live well. People who do not walk consistently lose it surprisingly quickly.

## How to actually move more

Given that most people sit too much and walk too little, the question is what to do about it. A few patterns recur in the behavioural literature.

**Make movement the default for short trips.** If a destination is under a kilometre or two, walking is often faster than driving once you account for parking. Substituting walking for short car or transit trips can add several thousand steps per day with no time cost.

**Build walking into existing routines.** Phone calls taken on a walk. Meetings conducted while walking. Coffee fetched from a slightly farther café. The most sustained NEAT increases come from movement that is woven into things you were going to do anyway, not from movement scheduled as a separate activity.

**Set the environment up for it.** People who place their water bottle one room away rather than within reach, who keep their walking shoes by the door, who take a bag rather than ordering delivery — all leverage what behavioural psychologists call choice architecture. The decision to move is easier when the path of least resistance leads through movement.

**Track without obsessing.** Step counts on phones and wearables provide a useful feedback loop. The goal is not to hit an arbitrary number but to be aware of trends. Most people who track their steps for a few weeks discover their actual baseline is lower than they thought, and adjustments follow naturally.

**Aim for cumulative, not heroic.** Three short walks of ten minutes each appear to produce roughly the same cardiovascular benefit as one walk of thirty minutes (Murphy et al., 2002). Building movement into the day in pieces is often easier to sustain than carving out a long block, especially during busy weeks.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

Two of the six Body metrics are directly affected by daily movement. Steps (0.05 of the Body score) is the most obvious — it is, literally, what this article is about. Active Calories (0.10) is closely related: most active calories for most people come from cumulative daily movement, not from training sessions.

Even the lower-weighted metrics like steps still matter considerably for the Flow Score. The Body pillar treats sustained, daily activity as foundational to nearly everything else it measures. The user who walks 8,000 to 10,000 steps daily but never trains formally will tend to have better cardiovascular markers, better metabolic health, and better recovery from any training they do undertake than a user who hits intense workouts twice a week but is otherwise sedentary.

The other connection is indirect but important. Resting heart rate (0.15) and heart rate variability (0.25), which together account for 40% of the Body score, both respond to consistent daily aerobic activity. The cardiovascular adaptations that lower resting heart rate and raise HRV come from sustained low-to-moderate intensity work — exactly what daily walking provides. Many people pursuing better HRV concentrate on hard interval training and miss that the larger lever, especially for sedentary starters, is simply moving more during the day.

The final point is one of leverage. Going from sedentary to moderately active — say, from 4,000 to 8,000 daily steps — produces some of the largest health gains available in the entire Body pillar. The effect dwarfs what you can achieve by going from "good" to "excellent" further up the curve. If you are starting from a low base, daily movement is probably the highest-leverage place to begin.
