The previous article was about the practices that make individual nights of sleep better. This article is about the structures that make those practices sustainable — the routines that sit between knowing what to do and actually doing it consistently. It also covers a specific tool that, when used well, can extend the recovery sleep provides: the strategic nap.

The framing is that good sleep is largely a behavioural problem, not an information problem. Most users already know broadly what improves sleep — they just do not do it consistently. The role of routines is to remove the decision-making cost from things that should not require thought every night. The role of strategic napping is to provide a recovery tool for the days when the night-time sleep was not enough.

## Why routines matter

The case for sleep routines rests on several different observations.

**Routines reduce decision fatigue.** Every night that requires conscious decisions about when to start winding down, what to do during the wind-down, when to put the phone away, and when to go to bed becomes a small willpower test. Over weeks and months, willpower runs out, and the user defaults to whatever takes the least effort — which tends to be exactly what undermines sleep. A routine that has been performed many times becomes automatic, requiring far less effort to execute.

**Routines train the body's clock.** The circadian system, covered in 1.7.1, calibrates itself to predictable patterns. Doing similar activities at similar times each evening signals to the body what is coming, allowing the gradual physiological transitions that prepare the body for sleep — falling core temperature, rising melatonin, parasympathetic dominance — to happen in their natural sequence. Erratic evenings produce erratic physiology; consistent evenings produce consistent sleep onset.

**Routines provide signals to associate with sleep.** The brain learns to associate specific cues with specific states. A consistent set of pre-sleep activities — the same lighting, the same location, the same kind of activity — eventually produces sleepiness as a conditioned response. This is one of the foundations of cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia: rebuilding sleep-supporting associations that have been eroded by erratic patterns or stimulus control problems (Edinger & Carney, 2014).

**Routines allow consistent timing.** As covered in 1.7.2, consistent sleep timing is one of the most strongly evidence-supported interventions. A bedtime routine makes consistent timing achievable in practice, not just in principle.

The honest framing is that routines do most of the work. Users who attempt to apply sleep recommendations on a night-by-night basis, deciding fresh each evening what to do, almost always fall short. Users who establish a routine they actually like enough to follow rarely struggle.

## What an effective bedtime routine looks like

The specifics matter less than the consistency. A wide range of routines work, provided they share a few features.

**Long enough.** A useful routine generally needs to last at least 30 minutes, and often closer to 60 to 90 minutes for users transitioning from active days. The body cannot drop into sleep instantly from a state of high stimulation. The routine creates the gradient.

**Progressively lower stimulation.** The general arc moves from mildly active to passive. A user who works on a laptop until 11:00 and gets into bed at 11:05 has not given their nervous system a chance to transition. A user who closes the laptop at 10:00, has a slow walk and gentle conversation until 10:30, reads quietly with dim lights until 10:45, and is in bed by 11:00 has built a graded transition the body can follow.

**Minimal blue and bright light.** The last hour before bed should be in dim, warm light. This is the strongest single environmental signal that "sleep is coming," and it directly supports the natural rise in melatonin. Dimmable bulbs, lamps with warm-spectrum bulbs, or candlelit environments produce this naturally.

**Limited stimulating content.** This includes most screen-based content (work, social media, intense entertainment), but also covers stimulating conversations, news, or anything that produces emotional activation. The goal of the routine is parasympathetic transition; emotional activation produces the opposite.

**Minimal food, alcohol, or caffeine.** The last meal generally completed at least 2 to 3 hours before bed. No caffeine in the second half of the day. No alcohol within 3 hours of bed.

**Comfortable physical preparation.** A warm shower or bath an hour or two before bed produces a temperature trajectory that supports sleep onset — body temperature rises during the bath and then drops as it normalises afterward, which the brain reads as a sleep signal (Haghayegh et al., 2019). Some light stretching, particularly of areas held tight during the day (hips, lower back, shoulders) can support both physical and mental relaxation.

**A consistent terminus.** The routine ends at the same approximate time most nights. The body learns to expect sleep at this hour.

A reasonable example routine for a user targeting 11:00 p.m. bedtime:

- 9:30 — Last work or screen-based task ends. Lights dimmed in primary living areas.
- 9:30 to 10:00 — Whatever transitional activity fits the user's life: walk, conversation, household tasks performed slowly.
- 10:00 to 10:30 — Warm shower or bath. Light stretching afterward.
- 10:30 to 10:55 — Reading (preferably printed material in dim light), gentle music, journaling, or similar low-stimulation activity. Phone in another room or on a charger across the bedroom.
- 10:55 — In bed. Lights out by 11:00.

This is a template, not a prescription. The components matter less than the consistency. A user who hates baths can substitute another transitional activity. A user whose schedule requires earlier or later timing can shift everything proportionally. What matters is that the same approximate sequence happens at the same approximate times most nights.

## When the routine breaks

Real life will routinely violate the routine. Travel, illness, family demands, social events, work crises — all produce nights where the practiced sequence is not possible. This is normal and not a problem. The question is what happens after.

The single most important principle for routine maintenance is that one disrupted night is normal and recoverable; a sequence of disrupted nights becomes the new pattern. The goal is not perfect adherence to any given night's routine but consistent return to the routine after disruptions.

A few practical principles:

**The morning after disruption matters more than the night itself.** A late night out is recoverable. A late night out followed by sleeping in until noon shifts the entire week's pattern in ways that take days to recover from. Maintaining roughly the normal wake time after a disrupted night limits the damage.

**Travel adjustments are worth planning.** For trips crossing time zones, gradual pre-trip shifts (going to bed and waking 30 to 60 minutes closer to the destination time, starting a few days before travel) reduce jet lag substantially. Light exposure timing on arrival is the most powerful tool for re-entrainment.

**Don't compensate by dramatically extending sleep.** "Catching up" on sleep over the weekend has been studied repeatedly and produces only partial recovery from accumulated sleep debt (Pejovic et al., 2013). The deficit accumulates; the recovery is incomplete. Better to maintain consistent sleep most nights than to alternate restriction and recovery.

**The routine is not the goal.** The goal is consistent good sleep. The routine is the tool. Users who become rigid about specific routine elements — to the point of distress when they cannot perform them — are missing the point. The routine should be tight enough to be effective and loose enough to survive normal life.

## Strategic napping

Naps are one of the more controversial topics in popular sleep advice, often presented as either universally beneficial or universally counterproductive. The actual evidence is more nuanced and depends heavily on timing, duration, and the user's overall sleep state.

The basics of nap physiology are well-described. A short nap (10 to 30 minutes) typically remains in the lighter stages of sleep, allowing the user to wake feeling refreshed rather than groggy. A longer nap (60 to 90 minutes) typically includes a full sleep cycle, with substantial slow-wave sleep and possibly REM. The intermediate range (30 to 60 minutes) is the most problematic — long enough to enter slow-wave sleep but too short to complete it, often producing sleep inertia (the disorientation and grogginess that comes from waking from deep sleep).

A few well-supported practical principles:

**Short naps (10 to 30 minutes) restore alertness.** Multiple controlled studies have shown that brief naps produce measurable improvements in alertness, cognitive performance, and mood in the hours afterward (Lovato & Lack, 2010). The effect is comparable to or larger than caffeine and persists longer. Users who can take a short nap during the day often perform better in the afternoon and evening than users who power through.

**Naps before 3 p.m. tend not to disrupt night-time sleep.** Naps later in the afternoon — particularly within 6 hours of bedtime — can reduce sleep drive and delay sleep onset that night. The earlier the nap, the less it interferes with night sleep.

**Naps cannot fully substitute for night sleep.** Short naps produce some restoration but do not include the full cycling through sleep stages that overnight sleep produces. The recovery is partial. Users using naps to supplement chronic insufficient night sleep will not fully compensate.

**Naps support recovery from short-term sleep loss.** A useful application is recovery from a single bad night. A 20-30 minute nap the next day produces measurable recovery in alertness and cognitive function.

**Naps may interfere with night sleep in some users.** Individual variation is real. Users with insomnia or difficulty falling asleep at night often benefit from avoiding naps entirely, because the daytime sleep reduces sleep drive at night. Users without insomnia generally tolerate strategic napping well.

**The "coffee nap" is real.** Drinking a small coffee immediately before a 20-minute nap produces a particularly potent alertness boost on waking — the caffeine takes 20 to 30 minutes to reach peak effect, arriving approximately when the user wakes (Reyner & Horne, 1997). Useful for situations requiring sustained alertness afterwards (long drives, demanding afternoon work).

## Practical nap recommendations:

- For most working adults: 20 minutes, before 3 p.m., when sleep drive is high (typically the early afternoon dip in alertness).
- Set a timer or alarm to avoid sleeping longer than intended.
- A relatively quiet, somewhat darkened space supports nap onset.
- For users who struggle to nap, even a quiet rest period in a horizontal position with eyes closed produces some restorative benefit, even without sleep onset.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

The routines and napping practices in this article support Sleep Quality (0.25 of the Body score) directly, with downstream effects on the rest of the Body metrics.

For users tracking sleep quality on Dolphin, a few specific patterns are worth watching:

**Improvement after routine establishment.** Users who establish consistent bedtime routines typically see Sleep Quality improvements within a few weeks. The improvement is usually visible in increased sleep efficiency (more time asleep relative to time in bed) and more consistent sleep timing across days.

**Disruption signals.** Sleep Quality drops following late nights, alcohol consumption, or schedule disruptions. The drops are usually visible in the data immediately and recover within a few days when normal patterns resume.

**Long-term consistency rewards.** The metric rewards consistent good sleep more than occasional excellent sleep. The 7-day rolling averages Dolphin uses are designed to capture this — the user who sleeps adequately every night scores higher than the user who alternates great nights and disrupted ones, even at the same total weekly sleep.

**Naps and the metric.** Most consumer wearables count naps in their daily sleep total to varying degrees. Whether or not a 20-minute nap is fully captured by the device, the underlying physiological recovery is real. Users supplementing slightly insufficient night sleep with short naps generally show better HRV and RHR than users with the same insufficient night sleep and no daytime recovery.
