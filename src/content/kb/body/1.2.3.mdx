This article finishes the nutrition fundamentals by covering two questions that get either overcomplicated or completely ignored in popular advice. How much water should you actually drink? And does it matter when you eat?

The answers, in short: hydration matters more than most people realise but less than the fitness industry implies, and meal timing matters in a few specific contexts that are worth knowing about — though it is a secondary lever compared to total intake and food quality.

## Hydration: the basics, with the caveats

The body is roughly 50 to 60% water by mass in adults. Almost every physiological process — circulation, thermoregulation, joint lubrication, digestion, kidney function, neurotransmission — depends on adequate fluid availability. Water is also the most underrated lever in daily energy. Mild dehydration, well below the level at which you would consciously feel thirsty, produces measurable drops in cognitive performance, mood, and physical capacity (Armstrong et al., 2012; Ganio et al., 2011).

The "eight glasses a day" rule has no specific scientific basis, despite its persistence. The actual evidence base is more nuanced.

Total water needs vary by body size, activity level, climate, diet (foods contribute substantially to fluid intake), and individual physiology. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA, 2010) sets adequate intake at roughly 2.0 litres per day for women and 2.5 litres per day for men, including water from food. The U.S. Institute of Medicine recommends 2.7 and 3.7 litres respectively. Both numbers include all sources — food, beverages, water itself — and both are broad averages.

A more useful approach than counting glasses is reading the body's own signals. Two work well in practice.

The first is urine colour. Pale yellow indicates adequate hydration; darker yellow suggests insufficient fluid intake; consistently clear may indicate slight overhydration. Armstrong's group at the University of Connecticut has validated urine colour against more invasive measures of hydration status and found it accurate enough for everyday use (Armstrong et al., 1994). Most people find it more reliable than thirst, especially in older adults whose thirst signal weakens with age.

The second is thirst itself, with one important caveat. By the time you feel thirsty, you are already mildly dehydrated. That is not a problem — the body has tolerance — but it does mean that during exercise, hot weather, or work that disrupts your normal drinking pattern, waiting until you feel thirsty is too late to maintain optimal hydration. Drinking modestly and steadily through the day generally outperforms drinking large amounts in response to felt thirst.

A few practical points are worth knowing.

**Caffeinated drinks count.** The widespread belief that coffee or tea dehydrates because of their diuretic effect has not held up in controlled studies. Killer et al. (2014) gave participants moderate amounts of coffee daily and found no measurable difference in hydration status compared to water. Caffeine has a small, transient diuretic effect that is more than offset by the fluid in the drink itself.

**Alcohol does dehydrate, meaningfully.** Alcohol suppresses antidiuretic hormone, increasing urine output beyond the fluid in the drink. This is one of the proximate mechanisms of hangover and a clear reason why training the morning after drinking tends to feel harder.

**Sweat losses can exceed intuition.** During hard exercise in warm conditions, sweat rates of 1 to 2 litres per hour are common, and elite endurance athletes sometimes lose more. For most recreationally active people, replacing fluids during exercise is straightforward — drink a few hundred millilitres before, sip steadily during, and finish replacing afterwards based on how thirsty you remain. For longer or hotter sessions (over 60 to 90 minutes, or above ~25°C ambient temperature), adding electrolytes — particularly sodium — becomes meaningful. Plain water alone, in large enough quantities, can produce hyponatraemia (low blood sodium), which has caused deaths in marathon runners (Hew-Butler et al., 2015).

**Overhydration is real and worth respecting.** Drinking far above your body's needs does not confer benefits and can dilute electrolytes. The right target is "enough" — not "as much as possible."

The bottom line is that hydration is a small daily habit with an outsized effect on how you feel. Most adults can solve it by keeping water visible and accessible, drinking with meals, drinking before, during, and after exercise, and using urine colour as a quick check.

## Meal timing: where it matters and where it does not

The cultural conversation around meal timing has become unusually noisy. Intermittent fasting, time-restricted eating, breakfast-skipping, late-night eating warnings, six small meals a day, three large meals — every variant has its advocates. The actual evidence is much more boring than the debate suggests.

Three findings recur consistently across the controlled literature.

**Total intake matters more than timing.** When calories are matched, most timing patterns produce similar weight and body composition outcomes. Lowe et al. (2020) ran a randomised trial of 16:8 time-restricted eating versus standard three-meal patterns at matched calories and found no significant differences in weight loss or metabolic outcomes. This does not mean timing has no effect — it means the effect is smaller than what total intake produces.

**Protein distribution matters for muscle protein synthesis.** Distributing protein across roughly three to five meals containing 20 to 40 grams each appears to produce more total daily muscle protein synthesis than concentrating the same total in one or two meals (Areta et al., 2013; Schoenfeld & Aragon, 2018). The effect is most relevant for people training for muscle gain or maintenance during ageing, both of which depend on the body's ability to repeatedly trigger protein synthesis.

**Fuelling around training improves training quality.** Eating carbohydrate and protein in the hours before a hard session improves performance during it. Eating protein within a few hours after improves recovery from it. The "anabolic window" — the idea that protein must be consumed within 30 minutes of training — has largely been overturned (Aragon & Schoenfeld, 2013); the relevant time scale is hours, not minutes. But the principle that fuelling around training matters more than fuelling between training is solid.

Beyond these, the timing patterns most commonly debated produce smaller effects than the debate implies.

**Breakfast.** The widespread belief that skipping breakfast harms metabolism or causes overeating later in the day has not survived controlled study (Levitsky & Pacanowski, 2013). Some people perform better with breakfast; some perform better without. The choice should be based on individual preference and how each pattern fits with training and work demands, not on metabolic claims that the evidence does not support.

**Late eating.** A large 2022 controlled study (Vujović et al., 2022) found that eating later in the day was associated with increased hunger, reduced energy expenditure, and changes in fat tissue gene expression that favoured fat storage. The effect was modest but consistent. The mechanism appears to involve circadian alignment — the body's metabolic machinery is calibrated to handle food differently across the day. The practical implication is mild: very late, large meals are probably not optimal, but the difference is not large enough to justify rigid rules.

**Intermittent fasting.** Time-restricted eating works for many people, primarily because it tends to reduce total intake without explicit tracking. The metabolic benefits beyond calorie reduction appear to be modest. Trepanowski et al. (2017) compared alternate-day fasting to standard caloric restriction and found similar weight loss but somewhat higher dropout in the fasting group. Whether intermittent fasting suits you depends largely on whether you find it easier to stick to than other patterns.

The honest summary is that meal timing offers small refinements once the larger variables — total intake, food quality, protein sufficiency — are in order. Worrying about timing before those are settled is putting effort in the wrong place.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

Hydration and meal timing influence the Body metrics in three identifiable ways.

The first concerns heart rate variability (0.25 of the Body score). Acute dehydration produces measurable drops in HRV (Castro-Sepúlveda et al., 2018) — a wearable showing reduced HRV after a hot, undertraining day may be reading dehydration rather than overtraining. Adequate hydration is part of what keeps HRV interpretable.

The second concerns sleep quality (0.25). Eating large meals close to bedtime is associated with poorer sleep quality and altered architecture (St-Onge et al., 2016). The mechanism appears to involve digestive activity competing with the physiological processes of falling asleep. Most people do not need a strict cutoff time, but pushing larger meals at least two to three hours before bed is a low-cost intervention with consistent benefit.

The third is more diffuse: how you feel through the day. Mild chronic dehydration manifests as the kind of low-grade fatigue, reduced motivation, and headaches that get attributed to work stress, screen time, or general life — and that drag down both training and recovery quality without ever being specifically diagnosed.

This article closes Section 1.2 on nutrition. The central message across all three articles is that nutrition's role in the Body pillar is best thought of as substrate. It does not appear directly in any wearable metric, but every metric runs better when intake matches demand, when calories are made of mostly whole foods, and when fluids are adequate. The next section, 1.3 Daily Movement, turns to the metrics nutrition supports — Steps, Active Calories, and the daily activity that builds the foundation for training.
