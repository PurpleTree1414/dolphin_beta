The previous article covered what to do in your first months of strength training: which patterns to learn, which exercises to choose, how to organise them. This article covers what makes those exercises continue to produce adaptation over months and years, and what makes them safe enough to keep doing for that long.

The two topics belong together because they are in tension. Progressive overload — the gradual, deliberate increase of training stress over time — is what drives adaptation. Without it, the body has no reason to keep getting stronger. But progressive overload pursued without attention to technique is one of the most reliable ways to develop the kinds of injuries that interrupt training for weeks or months. The skill of strength training, in the long run, is balancing these forces — pushing the body enough to grow, not so much that it breaks.

## What progressive overload actually is

The principle is straightforward. The body adapts to the stress placed on it. When that stress is repeated unchanged for too long, the body adapts to it and stops adapting further — the same workout produces the same result indefinitely. To continue improving, the stress has to gradually increase.

The principle was articulated formally by Soviet sports scientists in the mid-twentieth century but reflects an observation as old as physical training itself: bodies respond to demand. The Greek wrestler Milo of Croton, according to legend, trained by lifting a calf each day until it grew into a bull. The story is a parable rather than a training program, but its principle is correct: gradually increasing load produces gradually increasing capacity.

The modern research literature supports several specific forms this principle can take. Schoenfeld and colleagues (2017) reviewed the variables that drive resistance training adaptation and found that all of the following can contribute to progressive overload, in roughly descending order of importance for muscle and strength gain:

**Load.** Adding weight to the bar, the dumbbells, or the load being moved. The most direct form of progression and, for strength specifically, the most important.

**Repetitions.** Performing more repetitions at the same load. Useful when adding load is not yet feasible.

**Sets.** Adding additional sets of the same exercise. Increases total volume without changing intensity.

**Frequency.** Training the same movement more often per week. Most beneficial when the lifter is recovering well from current frequency.

**Range of motion.** Performing exercises through a fuller range of motion as joint mobility allows.

**Technical complexity.** Progressing from simpler variations to more demanding ones — bodyweight squat to goblet squat to barbell back squat, for example.

**Tempo.** Slowing down the eccentric (lowering) phase, pausing at specific points, or otherwise manipulating how the movement is performed in time.

**Rest periods.** Reducing rest between sets to increase metabolic stress (though this typically reduces load capacity per set, so it is more useful for hypertrophy than maximum strength).

For most users in the first one to two years of training, the simplest forms of progression — adding load and repetitions — produce the most adaptation per unit of effort. The more sophisticated forms become useful when basic progression slows or stalls.

## How to apply progressive overload in practice

The most common way beginners apply progressive overload is what is sometimes called "linear progression" — adding small amounts of weight to each lift every session, or every week, for as long as it works. This works for a long time in beginners, often six to twelve months, before progress slows enough that weekly increments become unrealistic.

A reasonable structure for a beginner:

- For each main lift, attempt to add a small amount of weight (typically 1.25 to 2.5 kg for upper body lifts, 2.5 to 5 kg for lower body lifts) when the previous session's prescribed sets and reps were completed with reasonable form and effort.
- If the prescribed sets were not completed, repeat the same weight next session and attempt again.
- If the same weight has been failed across multiple sessions, reset slightly lower and rebuild — sometimes called a "deload" of that specific lift.

This works because the early gains in strength training are largely neurological. The body is not building substantially more muscle in the first weeks; it is learning to use the muscle it already has more effectively — recruiting more motor units, coordinating muscles better, reducing inefficient co-contraction. These adaptations are rapid and respond to almost any consistent stimulus. Linear progression captures them efficiently.

After this novice phase, progress slows, and the structure of progression has to change. Intermediate lifters typically progress over weeks rather than sessions — adding load over the course of a four to six week training block, then deloading, then starting another block at a slightly higher baseline. Advanced lifters may progress over months, with much smaller increments and more sophisticated periodisation. Most users in this library will be in the novice or early intermediate phase for years.

A useful concept that comes from the strength training literature is the principle of "intent." The intent to push hard in a given session — to attempt a load you are not certain you can complete, to grind out a difficult repetition, to seek incremental progress — is what drives adaptation. Repeating the same easy workout indefinitely does not produce strength gains, even if the work itself feels productive. Progressive overload is, in essence, the formalisation of intent.

## Technique: what it actually means

"Use good form" is the most common piece of technique advice in strength training, and it is also the least useful, because what constitutes good form depends on the lift, the body, and the goal. A more practical framing is that good technique has four properties:

**The intended muscles do the work.** A squat is supposed to be primarily a lower-body lift, with the trunk stabilising and the legs producing the force. When the lower back rounds and the hips fail to drive the lift, the squat has been hijacked by the wrong muscles. The same applies to every other lift: the prescribed pattern depends on specific muscles producing the force.

**Joints stay in their efficient positions.** Knees track in line with toes. The lumbar spine maintains a neutral position under load (neither rounded nor extreme arched). The shoulder blade moves with the arm during pressing and pulling. These are not arbitrary aesthetics — they reflect the architecture of the joints involved. Loading joints out of their efficient positions concentrates stress in places not designed to bear it, producing the kinds of soft-tissue injuries that interrupt training.

**Breath is controlled.** During heavy lifts, holding the breath momentarily through the most demanding portion of the lift (the Valsalva manoeuvre) increases intra-abdominal pressure and helps stabilise the spine. This is normal and protective. Holding the breath through an entire set, or breathing shallowly into the chest throughout, is not.

**The movement is repeatable.** A single repetition can be made to look acceptable by force of effort even if the underlying coordination is poor. A set of ten repetitions exposes the truth — by rep seven or eight, the lifter is moving how they actually move, not how they aspire to move. Form should not collapse over the course of a set. When it does, the set is over.

The empirical case for prioritising technique rests on the long arc of training, not on any single session. McGill and colleagues' decades of work on lower-back biomechanics have shown that most non-traumatic back injuries result not from one bad lift but from cumulative micro-damage over thousands of repetitions performed with poor mechanics (McGill, 2007). The runner whose knee fails on a Tuesday morning has usually been running with subtle hip-control problems for years; the lifter whose shoulder finally gives way has usually been pressing with poor scapular control for hundreds of sessions. Pathology, in this view, is the integral of small errors over many repetitions.

The corollary is that technique investment is highest-leverage early. The patterns established in the first hundred sessions tend to persist. A beginner who learns clean movement and reinforces it carefully has a much easier path forward than a beginner who picks up rough patterns and has to fix them later under load. This is also why the first weeks of strength training should prioritise form over load — not because beginners are fragile, but because the patterns being learned will be repeated tens of thousands of times.

## Effort, RIR, and knowing how hard to push

A useful concept that has become standard in modern strength coaching is "reps in reserve," or RIR. The idea is to estimate, for each working set, how many additional repetitions you could have performed before failing. A set ended with two reps in reserve means you finished believing you could have done two more; a set taken to failure was performed at zero RIR.

The empirical case for RIR is that training to absolute failure on every set produces more fatigue per unit of adaptation than stopping a few repetitions short. Helms and colleagues (2016) reviewed the evidence on training to failure and concluded that, for most lifters, working with one to three reps in reserve on most sets — and only occasionally taking sets to failure — produces equivalent or better long-term outcomes with less accumulated fatigue.

For practical purposes, this means most working sets in a training program should feel hard but not all-out. A set of eight where the lifter could have done ten or eleven is too easy. A set of eight where the lifter could not have done a ninth is too hard for routine use. A set of eight where the lifter could have managed one more repetition with maximal effort, and a second only with great difficulty, is the productive zone.

This is also where progressive overload becomes self-correcting. A weight that previously produced an eight-rep set with two reps in reserve will, after a few weeks of consistent training, allow nine or ten reps with the same RIR. That is the cue to add load — not because of an arbitrary calendar, but because the body has adapted to the previous stress and is ready for slightly more.

## Common mistakes and how they limit progress

A few patterns recur often enough in beginner strength training to be worth naming.

**Adding load too fast.** Progressive overload works only when the lifter can maintain technique under the new load. Adding weight aggressively, without form catching up, eventually produces either an obvious failure (a missed lift) or, more commonly, a quiet degradation of pattern that accumulates damage over time. The remedy is patience: small, consistent increments rather than ambitious leaps.

**Confusing soreness with progress.** Delayed-onset muscle soreness, covered in 1.6.3, is not a measure of adaptation. Programs that produce maximal soreness are not producing maximal adaptation; they are producing maximal disruption, which is not the same. Productive training often produces moderate soreness in the early weeks, less soreness as the body adapts, and only occasional severe soreness when something genuinely new is introduced.

**Changing programs too often.** Strength training is, in some ways, boring. The same lifts, performed slightly heavier, week after week. Beginners often abandon programs prematurely, attracted by novel routines that promise faster results. The truth is that almost any reasonable program produces good results when followed consistently for six to twelve months. The lifter who completes a year of a basic program will be substantially stronger than the lifter who tries six different programs over the same period.

**Neglecting weak patterns.** The lifts that feel hardest and least satisfying are usually the ones the body needs most. The hinge for people who sit all day, the overhead press for people with poor shoulder mobility, the single-leg work for people with hip imbalances. Programs that allow trainees to favour their preferred movements over their needed movements produce uneven development and predictable injuries.

**Skipping warm-ups.** Working sets at meaningful loads should be preceded by lower-load preparatory sets. The standard pattern is two to three sets of the same exercise at progressively heavier loads before the first working set. This both reduces injury risk and improves performance on the working sets themselves.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

Progressive overload and technique do not appear directly in any Body metric, but they are upstream of one critical concern: the sustainability of training frequency.

Exercise frequency (0.20 of the Body score) rewards consistent training. The user who lifts twice a week for two years accumulates substantially more total adaptation than the user who lifts five times a week for three months and then spends the rest of the year recovering from a strain. The mathematics here is unforgiving — a single significant injury can erase six months of training frequency on the metric.

Progressive overload, applied with attention to technique, is what makes long-term consistency possible. The lifter who progresses gradually and maintains form can train for decades. The lifter who progresses too aggressively or with poor mechanics will, eventually, force themselves into the kinds of breaks that interrupt the metric and undo the underlying adaptation.

This is why we placed technique in this article alongside progressive overload, rather than treating them separately. They are two halves of the same problem: how do you keep getting stronger over the long arc of life. The answer is not by training harder than your body can sustain. It is by training in a way that compounds — adding small amounts of stress, allowing the body to adapt, repeating the cycle for years.
