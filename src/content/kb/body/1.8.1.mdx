The body has a coordinated system for responding to challenges that threaten its balance — the **hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis**, almost always shortened to **HPA axis** in the research literature. The name comes from the three structures it links together: the hypothalamus (a small region deep in the brain that monitors the body's state), the pituitary gland (a pea-sized gland just below the hypothalamus that controls many of the body's hormones), and the adrenal glands (small glands sitting on top of each kidney that produce, among other hormones, **cortisol**). The HPA axis is the physiological substrate of what we colloquially call "stress." When the system works well, it mobilises resources to meet acute demands and then returns to baseline. When it works poorly — when activation becomes chronic, or when recovery between activations becomes inadequate — it produces a cascade of effects that show up across nearly every Body metric Dolphin tracks.

This article covers the physiology of the stress response: what the HPA axis actually is, how cortisol behaves across a normal day, and what chronic activation does to the body's recovery capacity. The behavioural and cognitive side of stress management — meditation, cognitive reframing, mindfulness practices — sits in the Mind pillar. The two are complementary. This article gives you the biology; the Mind articles give you the techniques. Both are necessary for users dealing with sustained stress.

## What the HPA axis actually is

The HPA axis is a coordinated three-organ system, and it is worth walking through how it actually works because the cascade is the foundation of everything that follows.

It starts in the **hypothalamus** — the small region of the brain that monitors the body's internal state. When the hypothalamus detects a stressor (real or perceived), it releases a small signalling molecule called **corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH)**. Don't get bogged down in the name; the relevant point is that CRH is the first chemical messenger in the chain. It travels a short distance to the **pituitary gland**, which sits just below the hypothalamus.

The pituitary responds to CRH by releasing a second signalling molecule into the bloodstream: **adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)**. The "adrenocorticotropic" part of the name simply describes what it does — it travels to the adrenal cortex (the outer layer of the adrenal glands) and stimulates it. ACTH reaches the **adrenal glands**, which sit on top of the kidneys, and stimulates them to produce **cortisol** — the hormone most closely associated with the "stress response."

The whole sequence — hypothalamus releases CRH, pituitary releases ACTH, adrenal glands release cortisol — is what HPA axis means. It is a cascade designed for speed. From the initial stressor to peak cortisol release takes roughly 15 to 30 minutes — fast enough to mobilise resources for an acute challenge, slow enough that the response is graded and proportional rather than reflexive.

Cortisol does several things at once. It raises blood glucose, providing rapid fuel for muscles and brain. It increases blood pressure and cardiovascular tone (the readiness of the heart and blood vessels to respond to demand). It suppresses non-essential functions — digestion, reproduction, immune surveillance — to prioritise immediate demands. It heightens attention and vigilance. In the short term, this is exactly what the body needs to deal with a genuine challenge.

The HPA axis works in parallel with a faster system: the **sympathetic nervous system**, which is the part of the nervous system that drives the immediate "fight or flight" response. It produces its effects through **adrenaline** and **noradrenaline** (the same chemicals you might know as the things produced when you get a sudden scare). The sympathetic response peaks within seconds. Cortisol provides the longer-acting, sustained response that follows. Together they constitute the body's full physiological stress response.

The opposite system — the one that promotes recovery, digestion, and rest — is called the **parasympathetic nervous system**. Healthy daily function involves regular shifting between these two states: sympathetic activation when responding to demands, parasympathetic dominance during recovery, sleep, and after meals. This is one of the most important concepts in this entire library, because heart rate variability — 25% of your Body score — is largely a measure of how well-balanced these two systems are.

Sapolsky's *Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers* (Sapolsky, 2004) remains the most accessible comprehensive treatment of this system. The core argument of that book — that the human stress response evolved for acute physical threats and handles them well, but is poorly adapted to the chronic psychological stressors of modern life — has been repeatedly supported by subsequent research and remains the central frame for understanding why "stress" is a meaningful health variable.

## The daily cortisol rhythm

A common misconception about cortisol is that it is uniformly "bad" — a stress hormone that should be kept low. The reality is more interesting. Cortisol follows a strong daily rhythm, with normal fluctuations that are essential for healthy function. You need cortisol to wake up. You need it to feel alert in the morning. You need it to keep blood sugar stable between meals. The problem is not having cortisol — it is having too much, at the wrong times, for too long.

The pattern is well-described. Cortisol levels are typically low in the late evening and early in sleep. They begin rising in the second half of the night, well before waking. The **cortisol awakening response** — a sharp 50% rise in the 30 to 45 minutes after waking — is one of the most reliable hormonal patterns in human physiology (Clow et al., 2010). This morning surge is part of what produces normal alertness and metabolic readiness for the day. It is also what makes the first hour after waking a real biological event, not just a behavioural one.

Across the day, cortisol gradually declines, reaching its lowest point in the late evening, when other systems — particularly the parasympathetic nervous system and the sleep-promoting machinery — take over. The full curve is roughly: low overnight, sharp rise on waking, gradual decline through the day, low again in the evening. This rhythm is what allows the body to shift between activation and recovery in a coordinated way.

The pattern matters because it means cortisol is not just about stress. It is also about timing. Healthy cortisol function looks like a robust rhythm with appropriate peaks and troughs. Disrupted cortisol function looks like flattened rhythm, blunted morning rises, elevated evening levels, or any pattern in which the daily oscillation has been compromised.

A few specific patterns of cortisol disruption recur in the research literature:

**Chronically elevated cortisol.** Cortisol levels remain higher than appropriate across the day. Often associated with chronic psychological stress, sustained sleep restriction, or active disease processes. The user feels "wired but tired" — activated but not productively so.

**Flattened rhythm.** The daily oscillation is reduced — morning peaks lower, evening troughs higher. Associated with chronic stress, depression, certain chronic illnesses, and accelerated cellular aging in some studies (Sephton et al., 2000). The user often reports waking unrefreshed and feeling "off" all day.

**Reverse rhythm.** Evening levels exceed morning levels. The most disrupted pattern, and the one most strongly associated with poor health outcomes. Often seen in shift workers and individuals with severe chronic stress. The user feels exhausted in the morning and wired at night — the opposite of how the system should run.

**Blunted morning response.** The cortisol awakening response is reduced or absent. Associated with burnout, depression, post-traumatic stress, and certain forms of chronic fatigue (Heim et al., 2000). The user struggles to feel alert in the morning despite adequate sleep.

Most consumer wearables do not measure cortisol directly. But the autonomic markers Dolphin tracks — HRV and resting heart rate — reflect the same underlying system that cortisol regulates. Users with healthy HPA function tend to show good autonomic balance. Users with chronic HPA dysregulation tend to show suppressed HRV and elevated resting heart rate. The wearable cannot tell you your cortisol number, but it can tell you whether your autonomic state looks like a system that is recovering well, or one that is stuck in chronic activation.

## What chronic stress actually does to the body

The acute stress response is adaptive. The chronic stress response is, in nearly every domain that has been studied, harmful. The mechanisms are now reasonably well-characterised.

**Cardiovascular consequences.** Chronic cortisol elevation contributes to elevated blood pressure, vascular dysfunction, and increased cardiovascular disease risk. The mechanisms include direct effects on blood vessel tone, indirect effects through metabolic disruption, and the cumulative cost of repeated sympathetic activation. Steptoe and Kivimäki (2012) reviewed the evidence and concluded that chronic psychological stress is an independent cardiovascular risk factor of comparable magnitude to several established traditional risk factors like high cholesterol or smoking.

**Metabolic consequences.** Chronic cortisol elevation impairs insulin sensitivity (the body's responsiveness to the hormone that controls blood sugar), promotes the accumulation of fat around the abdominal organs, and worsens blood lipid profiles. The pattern of "stress weight" — particularly weight gain around the abdomen — is partly a cortisol effect. Chronic stress is increasingly recognised as a meaningful contributor to type 2 diabetes risk (Hackett & Steptoe, 2017).

**Immune consequences.** The acute cortisol response suppresses certain immune functions, redirecting resources to the immediate stressor. This is adaptive in the short term — suppressing inflammation while you escape a threat is sensible. Sustained over time, it impairs the immune system's ability to monitor for problems, increases susceptibility to infection, and may contribute to autoimmune dysregulation. Cohen et al. (2012) showed in controlled experimental studies that chronic stress measurably increases susceptibility to viral infection — participants under chronic stress, given identical exposure to a cold virus under controlled conditions, became sick at substantially higher rates than less-stressed controls.

**Cognitive consequences.** Chronic cortisol elevation is associated with reduced volume of the hippocampus (a brain region central to memory), impaired memory consolidation, and increased anxiety reactivity. The hippocampus has particularly high concentrations of cortisol receptors, which is part of why this region is especially vulnerable to chronic stress (Lupien et al., 2009). The effect is bidirectional: chronic stress impairs cognitive function, and impaired cognitive function reduces the resources available to manage stress.

**Reproductive consequences.** The HPA axis suppresses the reproductive system when it is heavily activated. Acutely, this is sensible — reproduction is metabolically expensive and is appropriately deprioritised under threat. Chronically, it contributes to disrupted menstrual cycles in women, reduced testosterone in men, and reduced fertility in both. The female athlete triad and Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport, both covered in 1.2.1, intersect with this physiology.

**Sleep consequences.** Chronic stress disrupts sleep architecture (the structure of how sleep cycles through its stages), reduces total sleep time, and increases night-time arousal. Sleep disruption further dysregulates the HPA axis, producing a self-reinforcing loop that is one of the most common patterns in chronic stress states. Section 1.7 covered the sleep side of this loop; this article covers the hormonal side.

**Recovery consequences.** This is where the chronic stress story becomes most directly relevant to the Body pillar. Chronic HPA activation impairs nearly every recovery process the body performs — muscle repair, glycogen resynthesis (the rebuilding of carbohydrate stores in muscle), autonomic recovery, sleep architecture, immune restoration. A user who is training hard and recovering well has a very different physiological state than a user who is training hard and chronically stressed. The training stimulus may be similar; the adaptation will not be.

## The training-stress connection

This article sits between Section 1.6 (Recovery) and Section 1.9 (Breathing) for a reason. Stress integrates with everything else in the Body pillar.

The body does not distinguish well between sources of stress. The HPA axis responds to physical demands (training), psychological demands (work pressure, relationship conflict), environmental demands (noise, temperature extremes), and metabolic demands (illness, undernutrition) through largely the same physiological pathways. **Total stress is what the body responds to; the source matters less than the cumulative burden.** This is one of the most important practical concepts in the entire Body pillar.

This has practical implications for training. The user who trains hard during a period of high work or family stress is not adding training stress to a fresh system. They are adding training stress to an already-activated HPA axis that is producing all the effects described above. The same training session that produces good adaptation in a well-recovered, low-stress user can produce overreaching in a high-stress user — even if the workout itself is identical.

Several practical implications follow:

**Training intensity should match recovery capacity, not the calendar.** A planned hard session on a week of unusually high non-training stress is not the same physiological event as the same session on a normal week. Adjusting training to current stress levels — which means sometimes reducing training when life demands are high, even if you "feel fine" — is a feature of sustainable training, not weakness.

**The autonomic markers reflect total load.** HRV declines from chronic stress look similar to HRV declines from overtraining, because they reflect the same underlying autonomic state. The right response is similar in both cases: more recovery, not more training.

**Stress reduction supports training adaptation.** Users sometimes treat stress management as an addition to their training schedule — something to "fit in" if time permits. The more accurate framing is that stress management is part of training. Adaptations to the work happen during recovery, and recovery is impaired by chronic stress. The user who adds 10 minutes of evening relaxation practice to their day is not subtracting training time. They are improving the conditions under which the rest of their training works.

## What supports HPA recovery

The full treatment of stress-management techniques sits in the Mind pillar — cognitive reframing, mindfulness practices, behavioural activation, structured rest. The Body-side interventions are largely about supporting the physical conditions for HPA recovery.

A few practices have particularly strong evidence for supporting parasympathetic recovery (the body's shift toward rest-and-digest mode) and HPA normalisation:

**Sleep, prioritised aggressively.** The first and largest intervention. Most chronic stress patterns improve substantially when sleep is consistently adequate. Section 1.7 covered this in detail.

**Slow, diaphragmatic breathing.** This is the topic of Section 1.9.3 and one of the few interventions that produces immediate, measurable parasympathetic activation and HRV elevation. Even a few minutes of slow breathing produces autonomic effects that, repeated regularly, support HPA recovery over time.

**Regular moderate physical activity.** Paradoxically, regular moderate aerobic activity reduces cortisol responses to subsequent stressors and supports better HPA regulation overall — provided the training itself is not the stressor. The same exercise that supports recovery when adequate can become an additional stressor when excessive. The signal is whether training is producing positive adaptations or chronic fatigue.

**Time in natural environments.** Several lines of evidence support the cortisol-lowering effects of time spent in nature. Hunter et al. (2019) showed that even 20 to 30 minutes of contact with natural environments produces measurable reductions in salivary cortisol (cortisol measured in saliva, the standard non-invasive way to track it). The mechanism is debated — partly visual, partly auditory, partly the absence of typical urban stressors — but the effect is consistent.

**Adequate, well-timed nutrition.** As covered in 1.2.1, chronic energy deficiency is itself an HPA stressor. The body reads "not enough food" as a survival threat and responds with elevated cortisol. Eating enough to match demand supports HPA function in ways that supplements and protocols cannot replace.

**Social connection.** Quality social relationships are among the most reliable buffers against the physiological effects of stress (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2015). The Body and Lifestyle pillars cross over here: Social Connection is a Lifestyle metric, but its physiological consequences show up in Body markers.

Notable for what is *not* on this list: most "anti-stress" supplements have weak evidence bases. Adaptogens (a category of plant-based supplements claimed to help the body resist stress), certain herbal preparations, and various stress-reduction products show small or inconsistent effects in controlled studies. The basic interventions above produce most of the available benefit; supplemental interventions are largely peripheral.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

The HPA axis is upstream of nearly every Body metric Dolphin tracks. Users in chronic stress states show characteristic patterns:

**HRV (0.25)** declines as parasympathetic tone is suppressed and the body sits in chronic sympathetic activation. The pattern is often more reliable than subjective stress reporting — users who claim they "feel fine" sometimes show clear HRV evidence of chronic activation.

**RHR (0.15)** rises as sympathetic tone dominates. Sustained elevations of 5 to 15 beats above baseline are common signatures of chronic stress.

**Sleep quality (0.25)** degrades as evening cortisol elevation interferes with the natural transition into sleep. The sleep-stress loop becomes self-reinforcing if not addressed.

**Exercise frequency (0.20)** typically holds up early in chronic stress periods (the user is still training) but eventually declines as accumulated fatigue forces breaks.

**Active calories (0.10)** and **steps (0.05)** decline somewhat, often before the user consciously recognises lower energy.

The pattern that emerges across the Flow Score data is reasonably distinctive: declining HRV, rising RHR, declining sleep quality, persistent exercise frequency in the early phase, eventually broader declines. This is the autonomic and behavioural signature of chronic stress — and it is, in many users, indistinguishable from the signature of overtraining, because it produces the same underlying physiological state.

The right interpretation depends on context the wearable cannot fully capture. If training has been intense and life otherwise stable, the pattern likely reflects training stress. If training has been moderate but life demands have been high, the pattern reflects life stress. Often it is both, with the cumulative load exceeding what either source alone would produce.

The actionable response is similar in either case: reduce the source that can be reduced. For training-driven patterns, that means more recovery and less training intensity. For life-driven patterns, it means stress-management interventions, often in coordination with the Mind pillar's articles. For combined patterns, it means addressing both — and accepting that this is sometimes a season for survival rather than progress.
