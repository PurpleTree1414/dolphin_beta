The previous two articles covered the foundations: how breathing actually works, how it connects to the **autonomic nervous system** (the part of the nervous system controlling automatic functions), and how the trainable qualities of the respiratory system can be improved over time. This article is about applications. Specifically, the three contexts where breath control produces the largest practical effects in daily life: managing acute and chronic stress, supporting athletic performance, and improving recovery and sleep.

The honest framing is that breathing is one of the few interventions where a small daily investment produces effects that show up in the data and in subjective experience relatively quickly. Five minutes of slow breathing per day, repeated consistently, is not a dramatic time commitment. The returns — measured in HRV, sleep quality, stress recovery, and general wellbeing — are real and visible within weeks.

This article covers what to actually do, in each of the three contexts.

## Breathing for stress

Stress, in the physiological sense covered in 1.8.1, involves a shift toward **sympathetic nervous system** dominance — the activating "fight or flight" branch of the autonomic system. The body reads a stressor (real or perceived), the **HPA axis** activates, **cortisol** rises, breathing becomes faster and shallower, heart rate increases, and the body mobilises resources for action. In an acute crisis, this is appropriate. As a chronic state, it is the source of nearly every "stress-related" health problem covered earlier in this library.

Slow breathing is one of the few interventions that can deliberately reverse this shift, on demand, within minutes. The mechanism, covered in 1.9.1, runs through the **vagus nerve** — the largest nerve in the **parasympathetic nervous system** (the recovery, "rest and digest" branch). Slow exhalation directly stimulates the vagus, sending parasympathetic signals to the brainstem and shifting the body toward recovery mode.

Several specific breathing patterns have evidence behind them for stress reduction.

**Resonant breathing (around 6 breaths per minute).** Covered in 1.9.1 as the rate that produces the largest HRV response in most adults. The standard pattern is roughly 4 seconds inhale, 6 seconds exhale, repeated for 5 to 10 minutes. The longer exhale is the key element — exhalation is what activates the vagus nerve most strongly, and an exhale-dominant pattern produces a stronger parasympathetic shift than an even one. This is the most evidence-supported general breathing practice for autonomic regulation, with substantial research from Lehrer and colleagues over the past two decades (Lehrer et al., 2020).

**Box breathing (4-4-4-4).** Equal periods of inhale, hold, exhale, hold — typically 4 seconds each. This is the technique used by U.S. Navy SEALs and various other tactical organisations, and the popularity is partly cultural. The actual evidence base is more limited than the resonant breathing literature, but the practice produces a similar autonomic shift through slow, controlled breathing with breath holds. It is particularly useful in acute stress contexts because the rhythmic structure provides a focal point for attention that can be steadier than counting alone.

**Extended exhale breathing.** A simpler variant: just make exhales noticeably longer than inhales. A 1:2 ratio (inhale 4 seconds, exhale 8 seconds, for example) produces strong parasympathetic activation. This is a useful "minimum viable" breathing practice for users who find more elaborate patterns hard to remember.

**The physiological sigh.** A quick double inhale followed by a long, slow exhale — a pattern the body produces naturally during certain stressful or emotional moments. Recent research from Andrew Huberman's lab and others has suggested that this pattern produces particularly fast acute stress reduction, more rapidly than slower-paced breathing exercises (Balban et al., 2023). One to three repetitions take less than a minute and produce measurable effects on subjective stress and physiological markers. This is potentially the highest-leverage breathing intervention available for acute moments — when you have one minute, not ten.

## Practical application:

- For acute stress in the moment: 1 to 3 physiological sighs, or 2 to 3 minutes of extended-exhale breathing. Useful before a meeting, after a difficult conversation, in traffic, in transitions between activities.
- For daily preventive practice: 5 to 10 minutes of resonant breathing (roughly 6 breaths per minute) once or twice per day. The morning and evening are common choices, but any consistent time works.
- For sustained chronic stress: integration of breathing practice with other interventions covered in 1.8.1 — sleep prioritisation, time in nature, social connection, and the cognitive interventions covered in the Mind pillar.

The honest framing is that breathing is not a complete stress-management toolkit. It is one tool — a fast, reliable, always-available one. For chronic stress, breathing alone is rarely sufficient. For acute stress, it is often the most useful intervention available in real time.

## Breathing for performance

The performance applications of breathing are different from the stress applications. Where stress breathing is about producing parasympathetic dominance, performance breathing is often about something more nuanced: managing the autonomic state needed for the task, maintaining respiratory function under demand, and using breath control as an attentional anchor during difficult moments.

Several patterns recur in athletic performance contexts.

**Breathing during strength training.** During heavy lifts, momentary breath holds are protective. The **Valsalva manoeuvre** — forcefully holding the breath against a closed glottis (the back of the throat) during the most demanding portion of a lift — increases pressure inside the abdominal cavity, which stabilises the spine and protects the lower back. This is the breath pattern most experienced lifters use intuitively for their heaviest sets.

For lighter sets — anything in the 10-rep-plus range — sustained breath holds become impractical and counterproductive. The standard pattern is to exhale through the demanding (concentric, lifting) portion and inhale through the easier (eccentric, lowering) portion. The general rule is "exhale on effort," with the concentric and eccentric phases coordinated with breath.

**Breathing during endurance work.** Most endurance athletes settle into a stable breathing rhythm coordinated with their movement — for example, running with a 3:2 pattern (3 strides inhaling, 2 strides exhaling) for moderate paces, shifting to 2:1 or 2:2 for harder efforts. The specific rhythm matters less than having one. Coordinated rhythmic breathing reduces the "panic" response that can occur when breathing feels chaotic and helps maintain efficiency at sustained pace.

For users training in the easier zones, nasal-only breathing is feasible and produces some benefits over time (covered in 1.9.2). At hard intensities, mouth breathing is necessary and forcing nasal-only breathing becomes counterproductive.

**Pre-performance breathing.** Different sports call for different pre-performance autonomic states. Some require activation (sprinting, contact sports, explosive efforts). Others require calm focus (precision sports like archery, golf, shooting). Breathing patterns can shape this:

- For activation: shorter, faster breathing, particularly with emphasis on the inhale, tends to support sympathetic readiness.
- For calm focus: slow, exhale-emphasised breathing supports the parasympathetic dominance that often accompanies optimal precision performance.

The classic example is the slow exhale that many archers and snipers perform immediately before releasing — it slows heart rate and stabilises the body for the demanding moment.

**Recovery between high-intensity efforts.** During interval training, the period between hard efforts is when recovery happens. Slow, deliberate breathing during these recovery periods accelerates the return toward parasympathetic dominance, allowing more complete recovery before the next interval. This is one reason coaches often emphasise active, controlled recovery breathing rather than just "resting" between hard efforts.

The honest framing is that performance breathing techniques produce modest but real benefits in most cases. They are unlikely to transform a moderate athlete into an elite one. They are likely to help any athlete perform marginally better, train more comfortably, and recover slightly more quickly between sessions.

## Breathing for recovery and sleep

The recovery and sleep applications of breathing connect closely to the autonomic-shift mechanism covered in 1.9.1. Sleep onset, in particular, depends on the body's transition from sympathetic to parasympathetic dominance — and breathing is one of the most direct ways to support that transition.

Several specific applications are worth knowing.

**The 4-7-8 pattern.** A pattern popularised by Andrew Weil and others: inhale for 4 seconds, hold for 7 seconds, exhale for 8 seconds. The extended hold and very long exhale produce strong parasympathetic activation. The pattern is somewhat demanding (the holds and long exhales take practice) but effective for sleep onset in users who tolerate it. Several rounds at the start of the wind-down routine, or while in bed, can help shift the autonomic state toward sleep.

**Slow nasal breathing during the wind-down.** The simplest application: just breathing slowly through the nose during the last 30 to 60 minutes before bed, while reading or doing whatever low-stimulation activity ends the day. No specific count or rhythm needed — just slower, fuller, nasal breathing in place of the chest-dominant patterns most people default to during stressful days. Over time, this becomes a learned cue that the body associates with the transition into sleep.

**Body scan breathing.** A practice that combines slow breathing with attention to physical relaxation. Lying in bed, working slowly through different parts of the body — face, neck, shoulders, arms, chest, abdomen, hips, legs — releasing any held tension while maintaining slow breathing. This is one of the foundational practices in **progressive muscle relaxation** (a structured technique for releasing physical tension) and **mindfulness-based interventions** for sleep.

**Breathing for night-time wake-ups.** Users who wake during the night and struggle to return to sleep often benefit from brief breathing practice when this happens. The acute autonomic shift back toward parasympathetic dominance — produced by 5 to 10 minutes of slow breathing — supports return to sleep more reliably than lying awake trying to force sleep through willpower.

**Breathing during morning awakening.** A brief breathing practice on waking can support the smoother transition into the day. This works in the opposite direction from sleep practice — slower breathing here is less about sympathetic suppression and more about gently activating awareness without producing the harsh sympathetic spike that accompanies anxious morning rumination. A few minutes of slow breathing, before reaching for the phone or jumping into the day's tasks, sets a calmer baseline.

## Practical sleep applications:

- For routine sleep support: incorporate slow nasal breathing into the last 30 minutes of the evening wind-down. Combine with the broader sleep hygiene interventions covered in Section 1.7.
- For acute sleep difficulty: 4-7-8 breathing or extended-exhale patterns when sleep is not coming, ideally while sitting up briefly rather than thrashing in bed (consistent with the **stimulus control** principles covered in 1.7.2).
- For chronic sleep difficulty: breathing as part of a broader behavioural approach — typically alongside cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) when chronic insomnia is involved.

## Putting it all together

The unifying picture across this section is that breathing is a uniquely accessible intervention. It is:

- **Always available.** No equipment, no cost, no scheduling required. You have access to it any time.
- **Fast-acting.** Effects appear within minutes, often within seconds in the case of the physiological sigh.
- **Cumulative.** Brief daily practice over weeks produces baseline shifts that persist between practice sessions.
- **Compatible with everything else.** Breathing practice does not conflict with any other intervention in this library; if anything, it supports them.

For users looking for a minimal-viable breathing practice that produces most of the available benefits with modest time investment, the recommendation is something like:

- **Once daily**: 5 to 10 minutes of resonant breathing (6 breaths per minute) at any consistent time.
- **In acute stress moments**: 1 to 3 physiological sighs, or 1 to 2 minutes of extended-exhale breathing.
- **As part of evening wind-down**: slow nasal breathing during the last 30 minutes before sleep.

This adds up to perhaps 10 to 15 minutes per day, distributed naturally through normal daily transitions. The effects, applied consistently, show up in HRV data, sleep quality, stress recovery, and subjective wellbeing within a few weeks.

For users who want to invest more — competitive athletes, people with chronic stress or sleep issues, those interested in respiratory training — the additional practices covered in 1.9.2 (CO₂ tolerance training, respiratory muscle training) and the more elaborate patterns in this article extend the foundation. But the foundation alone produces most of the available benefit.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

Breathing practice is the most directly responsive intervention in this library for autonomic markers. Several patterns are worth noting:

**HRV (0.25).** Slow breathing produces immediate elevation in HRV during practice, which Dolphin's wearables can sometimes detect within sessions. The longer-term effect on baseline HRV (the 7-day rolling average that drives the Body score) is more gradual but reasonably reliable with sustained practice — typically visible within 4 to 8 weeks.

**RHR (0.15).** Sustained breathing practice produces modest reductions in baseline RHR through improved autonomic balance. The effects are smaller and slower than for HRV but accumulate over months.

**Sleep quality (0.25).** Users who incorporate breathing practice into their wind-down routine often report and measure improvements in sleep quality within weeks. The effect interacts with the broader sleep practices covered in Section 1.7.

**Stress recovery in metric data.** Users tracking their data through stressful periods often see breathing practice mitigate the typical declines in HRV and rises in RHR that chronic stress produces. The metrics still respond to stress, but with smaller magnitude and faster recovery.

**Indirect effects on training metrics.** Better autonomic recovery supports better training tolerance, which supports more consistent exercise frequency and the cardiovascular adaptations that follow.

The practical bottom line: among the time-investment / benefit ratios in this entire library, breathing practice is one of the highest. Five minutes per day, applied consistently, is genuinely transformative for autonomic markers and supportive of nearly every other Body metric. For users looking for a single intervention to add when nothing else fits, this is probably it.
