The previous article covered two ways to avoid injury: managing the load you put on the body, and identifying movement patterns likely to produce damage over time. This article covers a third lever — deliberately strengthening the tissues, joints, and movement patterns most likely to fail, before any failure has occurred. The term used in the literature is **prehabilitation**, or **prehab** — exercise designed to build resilience proactively rather than to recover after damage has been done.

The framing is that the body is not infinitely durable, but it is highly trainable. The tissues that fail most often in recreational athletes are predictable: rotator cuffs in lifters, hamstrings in runners and field-sport athletes, knees in users who squat and run, lower backs in nearly everyone. The patterns are predictable enough that targeted work on these areas, integrated into ongoing training, substantially reduces the risk of the injuries they prevent.

The literature on prehab is reasonably strong for specific applications. The strongest evidence is in athletic populations, where structured prehab programs have produced meaningful reductions in injury rates over training seasons. The same principles apply to recreational training; the magnitude of effect is somewhat smaller because the baseline risk is lower, but the direction is consistent.

## What prehab actually involves

Prehab differs from general training in three ways. It targets specific structures or patterns identified as vulnerable. It uses lower loads and higher repetitions than primary training. And it is performed regularly, often daily, integrated into existing training rather than scheduled as separate sessions.

The structures most commonly targeted in recreational populations:

**Rotator cuff and scapular stabilisers.** The small muscles around the shoulder that control fine positioning of the arm. Underdeveloped relative to the larger pressing and pulling muscles in most users. Vulnerable in any training that involves pressing, pulling, throwing, or overhead work — meaning most upper-body training.

**Hip stabilisers.** The smaller muscles around the hip (gluteus medius, deep hip rotators) that control fine alignment of the lower body. Often weak in users who sit much of the day. Vulnerable in any single-leg activity, including running, climbing stairs, and any sport involving lateral movement.

**Hamstrings.** Particularly the eccentric (lengthening) capacity of the hamstrings — their ability to absorb force as they lengthen. Hamstring strains are one of the most common injuries in athletic populations and are heavily mediated by eccentric strength.

**Calves and ankles.** The muscles around the ankle and the lower leg, which absorb impact during running and walking. Frequently undertrained in users whose primary training is at the gym; vulnerable in transitions to high-impact activity.

**Deep core and trunk stabilisers.** Not the visible abdominal muscles, but the deeper layer (transverse abdominis, multifidus, pelvic floor) that maintains spinal stability under load. Often weak in users who perform mostly trunk-flexion exercises (crunches, sit-ups) at the expense of stability work.

**Wrists and forearms.** Underdeveloped in users without grip-intensive training; can become a limit factor in pulling work and become a problem with sustained typing.

This is not an exhaustive list. Other tissues have their own vulnerabilities, and individual users have their own pattern of weaknesses based on their history. But these areas account for the majority of common injuries in recreational training, and addressing them produces meaningful protection.

## What the evidence shows

The single most well-studied prehab program in athletic populations is the **Nordic hamstring exercise** — a partner-assisted (or anchored) movement in which the user kneels and lowers their torso forward against the eccentric resistance of the hamstrings. The hamstring contracts hard while lengthening, which is exactly the loading pattern that produces hamstring strains in athletic contexts.

Multiple studies, including a substantial meta-analysis by Al Attar et al. (2017), have shown that programs incorporating the Nordic hamstring exercise reduce hamstring strain injury rates by approximately 50% compared to control conditions. The intervention is brief — typically 2-3 sets of 5-10 repetitions, performed 1-3 times per week — and the protection is substantial. This is one of the clearest examples of high-leverage prehab in the literature.

Similar evidence supports specific prehab programs for other commonly injured tissues. The **FIFA 11+** warm-up program, designed for football players, combines specific strengthening and movement patterns and has been shown to reduce injury rates in football populations by 30-50% in controlled studies (Soligard et al., 2008). Programs aimed at preventing knee injuries through neuromuscular training in female athletes have produced similar reductions in ACL tears (Hewett et al., 2006).

The pattern across these studies is consistent. Targeted, regular, low-load work aimed at vulnerable tissues meaningfully reduces injury risk. The interventions are brief — typically 10-20 minutes added to existing training — and the magnitude of protection is large enough to be practically meaningful.

For recreational populations, the evidence is somewhat thinner but generally favourable. The principle that targeted work on vulnerable areas produces protection appears to translate, even when the specific protocols are adapted from athletic contexts. The recreational user who builds appropriate prehab work into their training is doing what the evidence supports — not chasing minor benefits, but working in a domain with substantial documented effect sizes.

## What to actually do

A reasonable prehab routine for a recreational adult does not need to be elaborate. The principle is consistency over complexity. A few exercises, performed regularly, in service of the most commonly vulnerable areas.

**Shoulder prehab.** External rotation work with light bands or dumbbells (2-3 sets of 12-20 reps), face pulls (2-3 sets of 12-15 reps), scapular wall slides or band pull-aparts (2-3 sets of 12-20 reps). Aiming for 1-3 sessions per week. The work is light — much lighter than typical training loads — but the cumulative effect on shoulder health is meaningful. Particularly important for users who do significant pressing work.

**Hip prehab.** Lateral band walks (2-3 sets of 10-15 steps each direction), single-leg glute bridges (2-3 sets of 8-12 per leg), clamshells (2-3 sets of 12-15 per side). Performed as part of warm-ups or 2-3 times per week. Important for runners and any user who does significant lower-body training or sport.

**Hamstring prehab.** Nordic hamstring curls (2-3 sets of 5-8 reps, performed 1-2 times per week with adequate recovery between sessions, given how demanding the exercise is) or Romanian deadlifts at moderate loads (which serve a similar eccentric-loading function within ordinary training). Particularly important for runners, sprinters, and athletes in any sport involving acceleration or change of direction.

**Calf and ankle prehab.** Single-leg calf raises (2-3 sets of 12-20 per leg), heel walks and toe walks for ankle dorsiflexion and plantarflexion strength, single-leg balance work (30-60 seconds per leg). Useful for runners and for general ankle resilience.

**Trunk stability prehab.** Front planks (2-3 sets of 30-60 seconds), side planks (2-3 sets of 20-45 seconds per side), bird dogs (2-3 sets of 8-10 per side), dead bugs (2-3 sets of 8-10 per side), Pallof presses (2-3 sets of 8-12 per side). Built around stability rather than flexion. Useful for everyone, particularly users with any history of lower back issues.

**Wrist and forearm prehab.** Wrist curls (both flexion and extension, 2-3 sets of 10-15), pronation and supination work with light dumbbells, grip work (farmers carries, dead hangs). Useful for users doing significant pulling work, climbers, and users whose work involves substantial typing.

For most users, choosing 4-6 of these from across the categories most relevant to their training and life, and integrating them into existing warm-ups or short standalone sessions, produces substantial protection without dramatically expanding training time. The total commitment is typically 10-20 minutes per training day, or 15-30 minutes per week of dedicated standalone prehab work.

## How prehab fits into existing training

The integration question matters. Prehab is most sustainable when it is built into existing structures rather than added as separate sessions.

**As warm-up.** Most prehab exercises work well as part of a training warm-up. The pattern is typically: general movement (5 minutes light cardio or mobility), specific prehab for the day's training (5-10 minutes targeting relevant areas), then specific warm-up sets of the planned training movements. The prehab is performed before fatigue accumulates and serves both as injury prevention and as activation of muscles that will be used in the upcoming session.

**As cool-down.** A briefer alternative — 5-10 minutes of light prehab work after the main session, when the body is still warm. Less intense than warm-up integration, but lower friction for users who find warm-ups difficult to extend.

**As short standalone sessions.** A 15-20 minute prehab-only session, performed 1-2 times per week, can serve users who have specific limitations or who train in environments where warm-up time is restricted.

**As integrated into rest days.** Light prehab work fits naturally into rest days from primary training, supporting active recovery (covered in 1.6.2) while addressing vulnerable areas.

The choice between these is largely personal. The most effective approach is the one that gets done consistently. A prehab routine performed 5 times per week through warm-up integration produces more total benefit than a more elaborate routine performed once a week as a standalone session and abandoned within a month.

## What prehab cannot do

A few honest limits.

**Prehab does not eliminate injury risk.** It reduces it. Users who follow comprehensive prehab programs still occasionally get hurt, particularly through traumatic injuries (single-event failures from accidents) and through random tissue events that happen even in well-conditioned bodies. The goal is risk reduction, not zero risk.

**Prehab is not a substitute for load management.** A user with excellent prehab routines who increases their training load too aggressively will still get hurt, because the underlying problem — load exceeding adaptation — was not addressed. Prehab works in combination with the load management principles from 1.11.1, not in isolation.

**Prehab is most effective when matched to actual vulnerabilities.** Generic prehab routines are less effective than routines targeted at the specific weaknesses of the user. The screening approaches in 1.11.1 help identify these. Without specific targeting, prehab is still useful but produces smaller effects.

**The literature does not support every prehab claim.** Some popular prehab interventions have weaker evidence than their advocates suggest. The principles in this article — targeted strengthening of commonly injured tissues, performed regularly with low loads — are well-supported. Specific protocols and proprietary programs vary in evidence quality.

## When prehab becomes rehab

A natural question is what happens when an injury actually occurs despite prehab efforts. The principles do not change dramatically — the body still responds to gradual progressive loading, the same tissues still respond to similar interventions — but the context shifts. Returning from injury requires more careful management of progression, often involves clinical guidance, and benefits from specific approaches that the next article will cover in detail.

The relevant point for this article is that prehab is the proactive version of what becomes rehab when an injury occurs. The user who has been doing rotator cuff work for years has both better protection against shoulder injury and a substantial head start on recovery if something does go wrong. The work compounds; what is built before injury becomes the foundation of recovery if injury happens.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

The metrics Dolphin tracks reflect prehab indirectly, primarily through their effects on training consistency.

**Exercise frequency (0.20).** Users who avoid the kinds of overuse injuries that prehab targets accumulate substantially more weeks of consistent training. The metric rewards what prehab makes possible.

**HRV (0.25), RHR (0.15), and sleep quality (0.25).** The cardiovascular and recovery adaptations these metrics reflect come from sustained training. Sustained training requires not getting injured. The connection runs through consistency.

**Active calories (0.10) and steps (0.05).** Injuries reduce daily movement, often for weeks. Prehab that prevents injuries supports the daily activity these metrics capture.

The integration here is the same point made throughout this section: foundational practices support multiple metrics simultaneously, and prehab is one of the foundational practices that produces years of consistent practice rather than months interrupted by injury. The 10-15 minutes per day of prehab work is small in absolute terms; the cumulative effect on training consistency over a decade is substantial.
