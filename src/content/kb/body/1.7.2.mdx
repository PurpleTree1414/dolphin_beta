The previous article covered what sleep is and why it matters. This article covers what to actually do about it. The good news is that the evidence base on sleep improvement is one of the best-developed in this entire research library. The interventions that actually improve sleep quality have been studied extensively, and the findings are reasonably consistent. The bad news is that most of these interventions are simple in description and hard in execution — they require behavioural changes that come up against the realities of modern life.

The framing throughout is that "sleep hygiene" — the term used in clinical medicine for the set of practices that support good sleep — is not a luxury or an indulgence. It is the practical implementation of what the body's biology actually requires to do its sleep work properly. Most users have substantial room to improve their sleep without making radical changes; the gains come from a handful of well-supported interventions, applied consistently.

## What sleep quality actually means

Before discussing how to improve sleep quality, it is worth defining what the term means precisely. In sleep research, "sleep quality" refers to several distinct dimensions:

**Sleep duration.** Total time spent asleep across the night. The most basic measure, and the one most strongly correlated with health outcomes when extreme.

**Sleep efficiency.** The proportion of time spent in bed that is actually spent asleep. Healthy adults typically show 85–95% sleep efficiency. Lower values reflect frequent awakenings, difficulty falling asleep, or extended early-morning wakefulness.

**Sleep architecture.** The distribution of time across the different sleep stages. Adequate slow-wave sleep, adequate REM sleep, and a normal pattern of cycling between them. Disrupted architecture — for example, reduced slow-wave sleep or fragmented REM — is associated with poor recovery and worse health outcomes even when total duration is adequate.

**Sleep timing consistency.** Whether sleep happens at similar clock times across days. Irregular sleep timing — sometimes called "social jet lag" — produces worse outcomes than the same total sleep distributed consistently (Roenneberg et al., 2012).

**Subjective sleep quality.** The user's own report of how restful and refreshing their sleep was. This is correlated with but not identical to the objective measures.

What Dolphin's wearables measure is some combination of duration, efficiency, and stage distribution, plus heart rate and HRV during sleep that produces a composite quality estimate. Different devices weigh these inputs differently, but the general direction is consistent: more sleep, more efficiently used, with reasonable architecture and consistent timing, scores better.

The interventions that follow target all of these dimensions, with varying effects. Some primarily improve duration. Some primarily improve efficiency. Some primarily improve architecture or timing. Together they form the practical core of what users can actually change.

## The interventions with strongest evidence

A few practices have produced consistent benefits across many controlled studies. These are the foundation of sleep improvement.

**Consistent sleep timing.** Going to bed and waking at similar times across most days, including weekends, produces measurably better sleep quality than the same average sleep distributed irregularly. Wittmann et al. (2006) coined the term "social jet lag" for the phenomenon of weekend sleep timing differing substantially from weekday timing — and showed that even modest social jet lag (weekend wake times two hours later than weekday wake times) is associated with worse health outcomes. The mechanism is that the body's circadian clock works best when it can predict what is coming. Dramatic shifts on weekends produce something analogous to jet lag, with the body recovering for several days afterwards.

The practical target: keep sleep timing within roughly one hour across most days, including weekends. The user who routinely shifts their schedule by three or four hours on weekends is, in effect, flying across time zones twice a week.

**Bedroom temperature.** The body's core temperature drops by roughly 1°C during sleep, and a cool sleeping environment supports this drop. Most sleep research suggests an optimal bedroom temperature in the range of 16-19°C (61-67°F) for adults (Okamoto-Mizuno & Mizuno, 2012). Warmer rooms tend to produce more frequent awakenings and reduced slow-wave sleep. The exact optimal varies by individual, bedding, and sleep clothing, but the principle that "cooler is generally better, within reason" is well-supported.

**Light exposure timing.** Light is the most powerful regulator of the circadian system. Two specific patterns produce most of the available benefit.

*Bright morning light* helps the body's clock align consistently to a stable schedule. Even 10 to 30 minutes of natural light exposure within an hour of waking produces meaningful effects on alertness during the day and sleep timing the following night. For users who wake in dark winters or work indoors, light therapy lamps (10,000 lux) can substitute. Wright and colleagues (2013) showed that participants on a camping trip in summer — with morning sun exposure and no artificial evening light — showed substantial circadian normalisation within a week.

*Reduced evening light*, particularly bright and blue-spectrum light, supports the natural rise in melatonin that promotes sleep. The effect is dose-dependent: brighter light, closer to bedtime, produces more melatonin suppression and later sleep onset. Chang et al. (2015) showed that reading on a backlit device for several hours before bed delayed melatonin onset by approximately 1.5 hours and reduced subjective alertness the following morning compared to reading printed material.

Practical interventions for evening light: dim the lights in your home for the last hour or two before bed; reduce screen brightness; use night-shift modes that reduce blue light; consider blue-blocking glasses for users particularly sensitive. The magnitude of the effect varies by individual, but the direction is consistent.

**Caffeine timing.** Caffeine has a substantial effect on sleep architecture even at moderate doses, and the effect persists much longer than most users assume. The half-life of caffeine in healthy adults is roughly 5 to 6 hours, meaning that caffeine consumed at noon is still meaningfully active in the body at bedtime (Drake et al., 2013). Drake's controlled study found that caffeine consumed even six hours before bed reduced total sleep time by approximately one hour and significantly disrupted sleep architecture, even when participants did not subjectively perceive any sleep effect.

The practical target: most evidence supports consuming the day's caffeine before noon, or at the very latest before 2 p.m. Users particularly sensitive to caffeine should consider earlier cutoffs. The user who drinks coffee at 4 p.m. and reports "I can sleep fine after coffee" is almost certainly experiencing reduced sleep quality without subjective awareness.

**Alcohol timing.** Alcohol is one of the most commonly underestimated sleep disruptors. While alcohol initially produces sedation and shorter sleep onset, it substantially disrupts sleep architecture during the second half of the night — reducing REM sleep, increasing fragmentation, and producing the early-morning awakening that many drinkers experience without connecting it to the alcohol. Ebrahim et al. (2013) reviewed the evidence and concluded that alcohol within 3 hours of bedtime produces measurable architecture disruption even at moderate doses.

The practical implication: drinking does not produce good sleep, regardless of how easily it produces sleep onset. For users prioritising sleep quality, the most useful interventions are reducing total alcohol consumption and avoiding it within 3 to 4 hours of bedtime.

**Bedroom darkness.** A dark bedroom supports both melatonin maintenance and sleep depth. Even modest light exposure during sleep — from streetlights through curtains, electronic device displays, or hallway lights — has been shown to suppress melatonin and disrupt sleep architecture (Cho et al., 2015). Blackout curtains, removing light-emitting devices from the bedroom, and addressing leaks of ambient light are simple interventions with consistent benefits.

**Noise control.** Sound disrupts sleep architecture even when it does not produce conscious awakening. Multiple studies have shown that ambient noise — traffic, snoring partners, household sounds — reduces slow-wave sleep duration and produces subtle awakenings the sleeper does not remember. Earplugs, white noise machines, or addressing the underlying noise source are reasonable interventions for users in noisy environments.

## The interventions with weaker evidence

A few popular sleep interventions have less robust support than their cultural prominence suggests. Worth knowing what is actually demonstrated and what is mostly marketing.

**Melatonin supplements.** Melatonin is one of the most popular sleep supplements globally, and the evidence is more limited than common assumption suggests. For sleep onset in healthy adults, melatonin produces only modest effects — typically reducing sleep onset by 5 to 10 minutes and adding limited total sleep time (Ferracioli-Oda et al., 2013). It is more useful in specific contexts: jet lag, shift work, and circadian rhythm disorders, where the issue is timing rather than insufficient sleep drive. For routine use as a "sleep aid," melatonin is unlikely to produce dramatic benefits and may have small effects on hormonal regulation that are still being characterised.

The conventional dose used in commercial supplements (3 to 10 mg) is typically far higher than what the body produces naturally. Lower doses (0.3 to 1 mg) appear to produce similar effects with less perturbation. Users considering melatonin should generally start at 0.5 mg.

**Magnesium.** A popular sleep supplement with mixed evidence. Some studies show modest improvements in sleep quality in older adults with low magnesium status. The effect in younger or magnesium-replete adults is less clear. Probably not harmful at reasonable doses, but not the dramatic intervention sometimes advertised.

**CBD and herbal remedies.** Limited research base for most products in this category. Some users report benefit; controlled evidence is thin. Generally low-priority compared to the better-supported interventions above.

**Sleep tracking obsession.** A behavioural concern worth naming. Users who become anxious about their sleep tracking data sometimes develop what has been called "orthosomnia" — sleep-related anxiety produced by the tracking itself, which then disrupts the sleep being tracked (Baron et al., 2017). The data is useful when it informs behaviour and counterproductive when it produces anxiety. The right relationship to sleep tracking is informational, not evaluative.

## What to do when you cannot fall asleep

A common practical issue is what to do when sleep does not come — when the user is in bed but cannot fall asleep, or wakes in the night and cannot return to sleep.

The standard cognitive behavioural approach (CBT-I, the most evidence-based treatment for insomnia) recommends not staying in bed when not sleeping. Edinger and Carney (2014) describe the principle of "stimulus control": the bed should be associated with sleep, not with wakeful frustration. The recommended response when sleep does not come within roughly 20 minutes:

- Get out of bed.
- Go to a different room with dim light.
- Engage in something quiet and not stimulating — light reading, gentle stretching, journalling.
- Return to bed when sleepy.
- If sleep still does not come, repeat the cycle.

This approach can feel counterintuitive — the natural inclination is to remain in bed and try harder to sleep — but the controlled evidence consistently favours stimulus control. The mechanism is that lying awake in bed creates an association between bed and wakeful frustration, which trains the body to be aroused (rather than relaxed) in the bedroom over time.

For occasional sleeplessness in otherwise good sleepers, this is generally not necessary. For chronic difficulty falling or staying asleep, stimulus control is one of the foundations of effective behavioural treatment.

## How this connects to your Flow Score

The interventions in this article directly target Sleep Quality (0.25 of the Body score), which is among the highest-weighted single metrics Dolphin tracks.

The integration with other metrics is also substantial. A user who improves sleep quality typically sees:

- HRV (0.25) improving over weeks as autonomic recovery becomes more complete
- RHR (0.15) declining as the cardiovascular system recovers more fully each night
- Exercise frequency (0.20) becoming more sustainable as recovery improves
- Active calories (0.10) and steps (0.05) rising spontaneously as energy availability increases during the day

This integration is part of why sleep is so heavily weighted. The Sleep Quality score is partly capturing direct sleep effects, and partly serving as a proxy for the broader recovery state the body is achieving. A user with persistently high Sleep Quality scores tends to perform well across the entire Body pillar; a user with persistently low scores typically struggles across multiple metrics.

The practical implication is that for users whose Body score is suboptimal, sleep quality is almost always the highest-leverage intervention. The interventions in this article — consistent timing, cool dark bedroom, morning light, reduced evening light, careful caffeine and alcohol timing — produce most of the available benefit. They are unglamorous and require behavioural change, but the cumulative effect on the Flow Score is substantial.
